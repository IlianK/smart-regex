\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Regular expressions are a fundamental formalism for describing regular languages 
and are among the most widely used abstractions in computer science. 
They underpin applications including lexical analysis, 
input validation, log processing, search tools, and network security. 
Across these applications, the problem is regular expression matching. 
Given a regular expression \texttt{(a|ab)*} and an input string \texttt{"aba"}, 
determine whether the string belongs to the language denoted by the expression. 
This is the \emph{membership problem}.
For many applications, this Boolean answer is enough, 
such as when an input validator only needs to accept or reject a password or URL.
Other applications require more information. 
A lexer, for example, must determine how the input decomposes into tokens, 
while capturing groups require identifying 
which parts of the input correspond to parts of the expression. 
In these cases, determining whether an input 
is a \emph{match} or a \emph{non-match} is not enough.
The \emph{structure} of the match must also be recovered.

Efficiently solving these problems remains an active research area.
The choice of algorithm influences performance and memory consumption.
Once matching becomes parsing, 
the way in which the parse tree is recovered also raises the question
of how to resolve ambiguity when multiple valid parses exist.

Existing approaches include backtracking and finite-automata-based techniques, 
such as nondeterministic (NFA) and deterministic (DFA) automata. 
These approaches have different trade-offs in implementation complexity, 
memory consumption, and worst-case performance. 
Backtracking engines are flexible and widely used, 
but may exhibit exponential running time on constructed inputs, 
leading to regular expression denial-of-service (ReDoS) vulnerabilities~\citep{cox2007}.
Automata-based approaches provide stronger worst-case guarantees 
but may require preprocessing or suffer from state-space explosion.

Regular expressions can also be processed using derivative-based algorithms, 
which transform the expression as input symbols are consumed. 
Brzozowski introduced regular expression derivatives 
as a way of transforming a regular expression 
after consuming an input symbol~\citep{brzozowski1964}. 
Antimirov later introduced partial derivatives, 
which represent sets of residual expressions and are closely related 
to nondeterministic automata~\citep{antimirov1996}.
Each derivative represents the language remaining after an input symbol has been consumed.
This relationship between an expression and its successive residuals
provides an elegant foundation for regular expression matching and, 
with suitable extensions, structured parsing.
However, derivative-based techniques are not commonly used 
in practical regular expression tooling. 
This work explores their implementation 
and practical application to regular expression parsing, 
moving ``From Theory to Tools''.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}



The work of ~\citep{sulzmannlu2014} developed a derivative-based algorithm 
for POSIX regular expression parsing. 
They also describe an optimized bit-coded variant 
that represents parse information as a compact bit sequence,
which can be decoded after a successful match 
rather than maintaining an explicit parse tree throughout the computation. 
In an earlier work, \citep{sulzmannlu2012} 
also studied partial derivatives in the context 
of regular expression submatching. 
While submatching is not the focus of this thesis, 
their work provides relevant background 
for implementing partial-derivative-based parsers. 

The authors have primarily studied these constructions 
in theoretical and functional programming settings, 
with less emphasis on practical considerations 
such as execution time and memory consumption.
Haskell implementations of the relevant constructions are also presented
in Sulzmann's \emph{Programming Paradigms} teaching material
\citep{sulzmann_slides}.

This thesis examines the practical aspects of derivative-based parsing
by implementing derivative-based and partial-derivative-based parsers,
including their bit-coded variants, in Rust. 
Rust's fine-grained control over memory allocation and data representation 
makes it suitable for evaluating their execution time and memory consumption.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The thesis implements ~\citep{sulzmannlu2014}'s derivative-based 
and \citep{sulzmannlu2012}'s partial-derivative-based 
parsing constructions in Rust, including their direct and bit-coded variants.
The implementations are based on the theory described in the
literature and the Haskell reference.
The derivative-based parsing function is further
implemented in recursive and iterative forms, 
which are evaluated as separate candidates.

The five parser candidates are evaluated 
in terms of execution time and memory consumption using two datasets. 
The first is the \emph{S2Elab} dataset \citep{s2elab-regexeval-dataset}, 
containing real-world regular expression patterns. 
The second is the \emph{Synthetic Stress Set}, 
developed for this evaluation,
with patterns designed to stress-test the parsers.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:thesis-outline}

The chapters are structured to present the theoretical concepts and
parsing constructions alongside their Rust implementations. 
\emph{Chapter~\ref{sec:regular-expressions} -- Regular Expressions}
introduces regular expression syntax, semantics, 
with their Rust representation, followed by
\emph{Chapter~\ref{sec:derivatives} -- Derivatives}, 
which covers Brzozowski's derivatives and Antimirov's partial derivatives 
matching algorithms. 
\emph{Chapter~\ref{sec:matching-vs-parsing} -- Matching vs. Parsing}
then introduces the distinction between matching and parsing and the
associated disambiguation problem. 
The two parsing approaches are presented in 
\emph{Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} -- Derivative Parser} and 
\emph{Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} -- Partial-Derivative Parser}, 
including their direct and bit-coded Rust implementations. 
\emph{Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend} -- Translation} describes the
translation of regular expression strings into the internal representation. Finally,
\emph{Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} -- Evaluation} 
examines the five parser candidates in terms of execution time, 
memory consumption, and correctness, while
\emph{Chapter~\ref{sec:related} -- Related Work and Conclusion}
summarizes the findings, discusses their implications, 
and outlines directions for future work.
