\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Regular expressions are a fundamental formalism for describing regular
languages and are among the most widely used abstractions in computer
science. They underpin applications including lexical analysis, input
validation, log processing, search tools, and network security. Across
these applications, the fundamental problem is regular expression
matching: given a regular expression and an input string, determine
whether the string belongs to the language denoted by the expression.
Formally, this is the \emph{membership problem}.

For example, the expression \texttt{(ab|a)*} matches the input string
\texttt{"aba"}, as it can be decomposed into \texttt{"ab"} followed by
\texttt{"a"}, but does not match \texttt{"abb"}, since no sequence of
subexpressions can produce that input.

For many applications, a Boolean answer is enough. An input validator
may only need to accept or reject a password, URL, or configuration
value. Other applications require more: a lexer must determine how an
input decomposes into tokens, while capturing groups, schema validation,
and information extraction require identifying which parts of the input
correspond to different parts of the expression. In these settings,
recognition alone is insufficient: a matcher must also recover the
\emph{structure} of a match, turning a membership problem into a parsing
problem.

Efficiently solving these problems remains an active research area: 
the choice of algorithm influences performance, 
memory consumption, and security, and, once matching becomes parsing, 
the choice of how a match's structure is recovered 
influences correctness as well.

Existing approaches include
backtracking, finite-automata-based techniques such as nondeterministic
(NFA) and deterministic (DFA) automata.
Each approach provides different trade-offs between implementation
complexity, memory consumption, and worst-case performance. Backtracking
engines are flexible and widely adopted, but may exhibit exponential
running time on carefully constructed inputs, leading to regular
expression denial-of-service (ReDoS) vulnerabilities \citep{cox2007}. 
Automata-based approaches provide stronger worst-case guarantees but may 
require costly preprocessing and can suffer from state-space explosion.

Derivative-based algorithms provide an alternative by transforming the
regular expression itself as input symbols are consumed. Each derivative
represents the language that remains to be matched after a particular
input symbol has been consumed. By maintaining a direct correspondence
between an expression and its successive residuals, derivative-based
methods provide an elegant foundation for regular expression matching
and, with suitable extensions, structured parsing.

However, derivative-based techniques are less commonly used in practice 
than other matching approaches. Bridging this gap
"From Theory to Tools" motivates the work presented in this thesis.



\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Brzozowski introduced regular expression derivatives as an algebraic
characterization of regular expression matching~\citep{brzozowski1964}.
Antimirov later introduced partial derivatives, representing sets of
residual expressions and provide a construction closely related to
nondeterministic automata while reducing redundancy compared with ordinary
derivatives~\citep{antimirov1996}. Building on these ideas, Sulzmann and Lu
presented a derivative-based algorithm for POSIX-correct regular expression
parsing \citep{sulzmannlu2014}.

Although the algorithm is formally developed and accompanied by a Haskell
reference implementation, its practical behaviour can be further studied
through an implementation in a systems programming language. Rust provides
such an environment through its compile-time ownership model, allowing
memory allocation, stack usage, and execution time to be studied as direct
properties of the implementation.

This thesis investigates the implementation of Sulzmann and
Lu's POSIX derivative-based parsing algorithm in Rust and explores an
extension of this construction based on Antimirov's partial derivatives.
The implementations also incorporate the bit-coded optimization proposed
by the same authors, to reduce memory usage and improve performance.

The resulting implementations are evaluated in terms of performance and
resource usage, both against each other and on real-world regular
expression patterns drawn from network-signature, spam-filtering, and
general-purpose corpora (Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}). The evaluation
also includes a direct empirical comparison with two established engines:
Rust's \texttt{regex} crate and Google's RE2.

Furthermore, the derivative-based representation provides an opportunity
to show the reasoning of the algorithm. This thesis
therefore also shows how derivative computations can be used as a
basis for diagnostic tooling, allowing matches and mismatches to be
explained through the transformations performed during the matching
process.


\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A Rust implementation of Sulzmann and Lu's derivative-based
  POSIX parser}, including a direct two-pass construction following the
  original algorithm and a bit-coded single-pass optimization. The
  implementation additionally explores partial derivatives as an alternative
  representation, resulting in four parser variants: 
  derivative-based and partial-derivative-based, in direct and bit-coded forms.

  \item \textbf{An empirical evaluation} comparing all four
  parser combinations with each other, on both synthetic benchmarks and a triaged corpus of
  real-world regular expressions, based on the datasets used in the paper 
  of Mamouras et al. \citep{mamouras2024}. 
  Then comparing them against established regular expression matchers such as
  Rust's \texttt{regex} crate and Google's RE2.

  \item \textbf{A derivative-tracing diagnostics system},
  showing the parser's computation steps to explain successful matches and mismatches,
  to provide a tool for understanding the algorithm's behaviour and debugging of regular expressions.
\end{itemize}



\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:thesis-outline}

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:regular-expressions} introduces the syntax and semantics
of regular expressions, together with the Rust representation of their
abstract syntax used throughout the rest of this work.
Section~\ref{sec:derivatives} builds on this representation to introduce
Brzozowski's derivatives and Antimirov's partial derivatives as algorithms
for deciding language membership, along with their Rust implementation --
the foundation on which the parsers presented later are built.

Before extending these algorithms to parsing, Section~\ref{sec:matching-vs-parsing}
distinguishes matching from parsing and introduces the disambiguation
problem that arises once a parse tree, not just a boolean, is required.
Section~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} then presents the derivative-based
POSIX parser adopted as this thesis's algorithmic basis, Sulzmann and Lu's
construction~\citep{sulzmannlu2014}, together with its Rust implementation
in both a direct ("inj") and a bit-coded form.
Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} extends this construction from
Brzozowski derivatives to Antimirov's partial derivatives, again in both
its direct and bit-coded variants. And Section~\ref{sec:frontend} 
describes the front-end of the implementation, which translates 
the input regular expression into the internal representation used by the parsers.

With all four parser variants in place, Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}
evaluates their correctness and performance against each other, on both
synthetic benchmarks and a corpus of real-world regular expressions, and
positions this evaluation relative to established regular expression
engines.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} situates this work relative to related
approaches, reflects on translating the underlying algorithm from Haskell
to Rust, and discusses the derivative-tracing facility built alongside the
parser, their usefulness and potential extensions.

Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} closes by summarizing the main findings and
contributions of the thesis and mentioning of related work.
