\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Regular expressions are a fundamental formalism for describing regular
languages and are among the most widely used abstractions in computer
science. They underpin applications including lexical analysis, input
validation, log processing, search tools, and network security. Across
these applications, the problem is regular expression
matching: given a regular expression and an input string, determine
whether the string belongs to the language denoted by the expression.
Formally, this is the \emph{membership problem}.

For example, the expression \texttt{(ab|a)*} matches the input string
\texttt{"aba"}, as it can be decomposed into \texttt{"ab"} followed by
\texttt{"a"}, but does not match \texttt{"abb"}, since no sequence of
subexpressions can produce that input.

For many applications, a Boolean answer is enough. An input validator
may only need to accept or reject a password, URL, or configuration
value. Other applications require more: a lexer must determine how an
input decomposes into tokens, while capturing groups, schema validation,
and information extraction require identifying which parts of the input
correspond to different parts of the expression. In these settings,
recognition alone is insufficient: a matcher must also recover the
\emph{structure} of a match, turning a membership problem into a parsing
problem.

Efficiently solving these problems remains an active research area: 
the choice of algorithm influences performance, 
memory consumption, and security, and, once matching becomes parsing, 
the choice of how a match's structure is recovered 
influences correctness as well.

Existing approaches include
backtracking, finite-automata-based techniques such as nondeterministic
(NFA) and deterministic (DFA) automata.
Each approach provides different trade-offs between implementation
complexity, memory consumption, and worst-case performance. Backtracking
engines are flexible and widely adopted, but may exhibit exponential
running time on carefully constructed inputs, leading to regular
expression denial-of-service (ReDoS) vulnerabilities \citep{cox2007}. 
Automata-based approaches provide stronger worst-case guarantees but may 
require costly preprocessing and can suffer from state-space explosion.

Derivative-based algorithms provide an alternative by transforming the
regular expression itself as input symbols are consumed. Each derivative
represents the language that remains to be matched after a particular
input symbol has been consumed. By maintaining a direct correspondence
between an expression and its successive residuals, derivative-based
methods provide an elegant foundation for regular expression matching
and, with suitable extensions, structured parsing.

However, derivative-based techniques are
less commonly used in practical regular expression tooling than
backtracking and automata-based approaches.
Investigating how these techniques behave in a systems implementation, 
and whether they can support practical matching, parsing, and diagnostic tools, 
therefore provides the motivation for this thesis: "From Theory to Tools".


\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Derivative-based regular expression matching has been studied extensively
from a theoretical perspective. Brzozowski introduced regular expression
derivatives as an algebraic characterization of regular language
membership~\citep{brzozowski1964}, while Antimirov introduced partial
derivatives, which represent sets of residual expressions and are closely
related to nondeterministic automata~\citep{antimirov1996}. Building on
these ideas, Sulzmann and Lu developed a derivative-based algorithm for
POSIX-correct regular expression parsing and provided a Haskell reference
implementation~\citep{sulzmannlu2014}.

Sulzmann and Lu's work therefore provides a formally developed basis 
for structured regular expression matching, but leaves several practical questions open. 
In particular, the behaviour of the construction in a systems programming language can be
investigated with respect to execution time, memory consumption, and other
implementation-level resource usage. 
Rust provides an appropriate setting for such an investigation through 
its ownership model and fine-grained control over data representation 
and memory allocation.

A further question is whether the construction can be extended to use
Antimirov's partial derivatives as an alternative representation of
residual expressions, and how such an implementation compares with the
original derivative-based construction. The bit-coded representation
proposed by Sulzmann and Lu also provides an opportunity to investigate
the practical effect of reducing the memory required to represent
derivative expressions.

The central problem addressed by this thesis is therefore to investigate 
the practical behaviour of derivative-based approaches to POSIX parsing: 
first, by implementing Sulzmann and Lu's construction in Rust and 
evaluating its performance and resource usage; second, 
by investigating an alternative construction based on partial derivatives; 
and third, by comparing the resulting implementations with established 
regular expression engines. In addition, the thesis investigates
whether the intermediate derivative computations can be exposed to
provide useful explanations of successful and unsuccessful matches.


\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A Rust implementation of Sulzmann and Lu's derivative-based
  POSIX parser.} The implementation includes both a direct two-pass
  construction following the original algorithm and a bit-coded
  single-pass optimization. It additionally explores Antimirov's partial
  derivatives as an alternative representation, resulting in four parser
  variants: derivative-based and partial-derivative-based implementations,
  each in direct and bit-coded forms.

  \item \textbf{An empirical evaluation of the parser variants.} The four
  variants are evaluated against one another using synthetic benchmarks
  and a corpus of real-world regular expressions based on the datasets
  used by Mamouras et al.~\citep{mamouras2024}. Their performance is also
  compared with established regular expression engines, including Rust's
  \texttt{regex} crate and Google's RE2.

  \item \textbf{A derivative-tracing diagnostic system.} The implementation
  exposes the intermediate derivative computations performed during
  matching, providing a basis for explaining successful and unsuccessful
  matches and for investigating the behaviour of the parser.
\end{itemize}



\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:thesis-outline}

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows.

Chapter~\ref{sec:regular-expressions} introduces the syntax and semantics
of regular expressions, together with the Rust representation of their
syntax used throughout the rest of this work.

Building on this representation, Chapter~\ref{sec:derivatives} introduces
Brzozowski's derivatives and Antimirov's partial derivatives as algorithms
for deciding language membership, along with their Rust implementation.

Before extending these algorithms to parsing, Chapter~\ref{sec:matching-vs-parsing}
distinguishes matching from parsing and introduces the disambiguation
problem that arises once a parse tree, not just a Boolean, is required.

Then Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} 
presents Sulzmann and Lu's derivative-based POSIX parser ~\citep{sulzmannlu2014}, 
which serves as the algorithmic basis of this thesis, 
together with its Rust implementation in both a direct and a bit-coded form.

Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} extends this construction from
Brzozowski derivatives to Antimirov's partial derivatives, again in both
its direct and bit-coded variants, resulting in a Greedy parser. 

The translation of the input regular expression 
into the internal representation used by the parsers 
is described in Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}, presenting the front-end of the implementation.

With all four parsers variants in place, Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}
evaluates their correctness and performance against each other, on both
synthetic benchmarks and a corpus of real-world regular expressions, and
positions this evaluation relative to established regular expression
engines.

Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses and interprets the evaluation
results, situates the work relative to related approaches, and reflects on
the translation of the underlying algorithm from Haskell to Rust. It also
discusses the derivative-tracing system, its usefulness and limitations.

Finally, Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the thesis by summarizing the main
findings and contributions and relating them to the research questions
introduced in this chapter. It also discusses directions for future work,
including potential extensions and improvements to the implementations and
diagnostic tooling.
