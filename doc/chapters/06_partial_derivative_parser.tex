\chapter{Partial-Derivative-Based Parser}
\label{sec:partial-derivative-parser}

Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} built two parsers around
Brzozowski's derivative. This chapter repeats the exercise with
Antimirov's partial derivative from
Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives} in place of
$r\backslash x$ throughout.

The substitution looks local and is not. Both operators consume one
character and return what remains to be matched, and either can drive a
parser. But $r\backslash x$ returns one expression and $pd(r,x)$ returns
a \emph{set}, and a set has no single top-level alternation whose left
branch a rule can prefer. That is where
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s two constructions made their POSIX
choice, in $\mathit{mkEps}$ and in $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$, and with the set
representation there is nothing there to make it on. The choice moves
somewhere else, and it comes out differently.
Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory} locates it, and
Section~\ref{sec:posix-vs-greedy-example} shows the consequence on an
expression small enough to check by inspection.

Neither flops14-extended \citep{sulzmannlu2014} nor Sulzmann's
teaching-page Haskell reference \citep{sulzmann_slides} defines a POSIX
partial-derivative parser at all. The paper's bit-coded construction is
Brzozowski-only, and the teaching page's own bit-coded
partial-derivative parser, \texttt{pDerivBC}, is built around a
deduplication rule that settles the matter by itself, without comparing
candidate trees. This chapter builds two parsers on that basis:
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} (Sections~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-theory} and
\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-theory-rust}), following \texttt{pDerivBC} directly,
and \texttt{pderiv\_std} (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-std-theory-rust}),
this project's own construction, built to give \texttt{pderiv\_bc} the
same kind of matched non-bit-coded counterpart
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s \texttt{deriv\_bc} has in
\texttt{deriv\_std}. Both are verified to agree with each other exactly,
and with the two POSIX parsers on membership alone, in
Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} and again in
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-worked-example}.

Trace excerpts follow the conventions of
Section~\ref{sec:deriv-tracing} and use the same running example. The
two parsers' level-3 reports have their own section structure, since a
frontier is not a single expression:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\hline
Report section & Construction phase \\
\hline
\texttt{INITIAL FRONTIER} & the one-strand starting state \\
\texttt{FORWARD PASS} & one block per character: before, rule, after \\
\texttt{SELECTION} & the first-nullable-strand decision \\
\texttt{MKEEPSBC + DECODE} & bit-coded only; \texttt{pderiv\_std} injects in place \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The four full reports are
Appendices~\ref{app:report-pderiv-std-match},
\ref{app:report-pderiv-bc-match},
\ref{app:report-pderiv-std-nomatch}
and~\ref{app:report-pderiv-bc-nomatch}.



\section{From One Expression to a Frontier}
\label{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory}

Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives} defined $pd(r,x)$ as a
set of residual expressions, not one combined expression, and
noted the NFA reading: each element is one state of an implicit NFA that
$r$ denotes, alive after reading $x$. Extending this to parsing means
pairing every surviving element with enough information to rebuild a
parse tree, should that element turn out to be the winner at the end of
the input. That extra payload is either an accumulated bit-string per
element (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-theory}) or, in this project's own
extension, an accumulated tree-injection closure per element
(Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-std-theory-rust}).

Either way the algorithm has the same shape. Maintain a \emph{frontier}
of \texttt{(residual, extra)} pairs, one per live \emph{strand}; at each
character replace it with the union of $pd$ applied to every current
strand, each contributing its own possibly-empty set of successors; and
at the end of the input select the first strand in the frontier whose
residual is nullable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} draws this for the
running example.

\subsection{Where the Choice Is Actually Made}

That last step, "the \emph{first} nullable strand in the frontier", is
this construction's disambiguation policy. It is decided implicitly, as
a side effect of keeping the frontier's growth finite, and no rule
anywhere in the construction states a preference between candidate
trees.

The reason no such rule appears is worth following, because it is not an
oversight. Antimirov's finiteness theorem
(Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}) bounds the set of
\emph{distinct} partial derivatives, but nothing about the extended,
tree-carrying construction keeps strands distinct as they accumulate
payload: two different derivation paths can reach the identical residual
\emph{shape} by different routes, each carrying its own bits or
injection. Kept without pruning, the frontier would multiply copies of
an already-explored residual instead of staying inside the bounded
syntactic set $pd$ guarantees. So pruning is needed. But pruning by
comparing the accumulated bits or trees, keeping whichever strand has
consumed more in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}, would mean comparing
incomplete, still-growing state: a strand's tree is finished only once
the input is exhausted, and at the moment the pruning must happen there
is nothing complete to compare.

The Haskell reference's choice is \texttt{nub2}: deduplicate purely on
residual \emph{shape}, keeping whichever strand reached that shape
\emph{first}. This sidesteps the problem by discarding the comparison
altogether. Once a shape has been claimed, every later strand reaching
the identical shape is dropped, whatever either one's payload would
eventually have produced. "First" means first in the list-ordered
traversal $pd$'s own case structure produces, which explores the left
operand of $+$ before the right at every step. The construction is
therefore left-biased and commits as soon as something succeeds, which
is not the longest-wins-with-left-as-tiebreak rule of
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}.

It is worth being careful about what that establishes. It says which
strand this construction selects. It does not say that the construction
implements any particular named disambiguation policy in general. The
trace states the consequence in one line, at the point of decision:

\begin{verbatim}
SELECTION (first nullable residual, priority/list order)
any nullable residual in final frontier = true -> proceed to
  mkEpsBC + decode
\end{verbatim}

There is no comparison here, because there is nothing to compare
against: the moment a nullable residual is found, scanning stops. That
is the shaded node in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}, and the strand below it
is never examined although it is nullable too.

This reading is this thesis's own account of where the construction
settles the question. Neither primary source states it in these terms,
and neither claims the result coincides with a named policy. What can be
checked is narrower, and is checked in Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}: the
two parsers of this chapter agree with each other on every case tried,
they agree with the derivative parsers on membership always, and they
disagree with them on the tree wherever the expression is ambiguous,
including the \texttt{paper\_r1} and \texttt{paper\_r2} examples of
Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem}.

\subsection{A Family of Witnesses}
\label{sec:posix-vs-greedy-example}

The two paper examples are not the only place the divergence shows, and
it helps to have a case where the pattern is simple enough that both
answers can be read off by inspection. Take $r = (b + bb)^{*}$ against a
run of $\mathtt{b}$s. The derivative parsers prefer the longer
alternative at each iteration; the partial-derivative parsers take the
left alternative as soon as it works. The two therefore partition the
same input differently:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Input & Derivative & Partial derivative \\
\hline
\texttt{"bb"} & \texttt{[Right (b, b)]} & \texttt{[Left b, Left b]} \\
\texttt{"bbb"} & \texttt{[Right (b, b), Left b]} & \texttt{[Left b, Left b, Left b]} \\
\texttt{"bbbbbb"} & \texttt{[Right (b, b)} $\times 3$\texttt{]} & \texttt{[Left b]} $\times 6$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The derivative parsers use as few iterations as the longest-alternative
rule allows, padding with a single \texttt{Left b} only when the length
is odd. The partial-derivative parsers use one iteration per character
throughout, because $b$ is the left alternative and it always matches.
Both flatten back to the same input and both are valid parse trees for
it: the expression is ambiguous, and the two constructions resolve the
ambiguity differently. These rows are the output of
\texttt{cargo run -- parse "(b|bb)*" "bb" --parser all}, which runs all
five entry points on one input and prints the trees side by side.



\section{The Bit-Coded Construction}
\label{sec:partial-derivative-parser-bitcoded-theory}
\label{sec:pderiv-bc-theory}

Sulzmann's teaching-page Haskell transcript gives a bit-coded
partial-derivative construction, \texttt{pDerivBC}, that this thesis's
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} follows directly \citep{sulzmann_slides}. It extends
$pd$ with per-strand bits the same way
Section~\ref{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory}'s $\backslash_b$ extends
the Brzozowski derivative, with one visible difference: the bits stay
\emph{external} to the expression tree, as a plain list carried
alongside a plain, un-annotated $r$, instead of being folded into an
\texttt{ARegex}-shaped tree.

Writing $D_r$ for $\mathit{pDerivBC}(x,r)$ throughout, to keep the set
comprehensions readable, the base and non-sequence cases are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pderivbc}
\begin{aligned}
D_{\varepsilon} &= \{\} \qquad\qquad D_{\emptyset} = \{\}
  &&(\mathsf{PB}_{\varepsilon}),\ (\mathsf{PB}_{\emptyset}) \\
D_{y} &= \begin{cases} \{(\varepsilon, [\,])\} & x = y \\
                       \{\} & x \neq y \end{cases}
  &&(\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{lit}}) \\
D_{r+s} &= \mathit{nub2}\big(
  \{(r', 0{:}bs) \mid (r',bs) \in D_r\} \\
  &\qquad\quad \cup\ \{(s', 1{:}bs) \mid (s',bs) \in D_s\}\big)
  &&(\mathsf{PB}_{+}) \\
D_{r^{*}} &= \mathit{nub2}\big(
  \{(\mathit{smartC}(r', r^{*}),\, 0{:}bs) \mid (r',bs) \in D_r\}\big)
  &&(\mathsf{PB}_{*})
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

and the sequence case splits on the nullability of the left factor
exactly as Equation~\eqref{eq:derivative} does:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pderivbc-seq}
\begin{aligned}
D_{rs} &= \{(\mathit{smartC}(r',s),\, bs) \mid (r',bs) \in D_r\}
  && (\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}),\ n(r) = \mathrm{false} \\
D_{rs} &= \mathit{nub2}\big(
  \{(r's,\ bs) \mid (r',bs) \in D_r\} \\
  &\qquad\quad \cup\ \{(s',\ \mathit{mkEpsBC}_r \mathbin{+\!\!+} bs)
       \mid (s',bs) \in D_s\}\big)
  && (\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}),\ n(r) = \mathrm{true}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The names extend Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}'s
$\mathsf{P}$ scheme, one $\mathsf{PB}$ rule per $\mathsf{P}$ rule, and
the derivation below cites them with the binding of $r$ and $s$ given at
each application. Here $\mathit{mkEpsBC}_r$ is the plain-$r$ analogue of
Section~\ref{sec:mk-eps-bc}'s $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$, identical in structure
but walking an un-annotated tree; $\mathit{smartC}$ is the smart
concatenation constructor of
Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}, collapsing
$\varepsilon\cdot s$ to $s$; and \texttt{nub2} deduplicates a list of
\texttt{(residual, bits)} pairs by comparing the residual alone, keeping
the first occurrence.

Two details of Equation~\eqref{eq:pderivbc-seq} are worth holding onto.
$\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$'s second set is where a strand
inherits $\mathit{mkEpsBC}_r$, the bits $r$ would have contributed by
matching empty, which is the same move
$\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$ made with $\mathit{fuse}$ in
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and the same one
$\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{R}$ made with $\mathit{mkEps}$. And the two
sequence cases build their residuals differently:
$\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ uses $\mathit{smartC}$ while
$\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$ builds its first strand with a plain
$r's$. That asymmetry is present in the reference itself and is
preserved here and not "corrected", since correctness of this
construction is judged by literal agreement with that reference
(Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}) and not against an independently derived
ideal form. Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-std-theory-rust} returns to the same
question from the other side, where the choice between the two
constructors is not free at all.

\subsection{The Running Example, Strand by Strand}

Run $r_1 = (a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$ against $\mathtt{ab}$ through this
construction, so that it can be seen disagreeing with
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} on the same input, and not merely be
asserted to do so. The frontier starts as one strand, the whole
expression with no bits, which is the leftmost node of
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}.

The first character binds $r = a+ab$ and $s = b+\varepsilon$ at the
outer node. That $r$ is not nullable, by $\mathsf{N}_{+}$ over a literal
and a concatenation, so $\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ applies and $s$
contributes no strand at all. The strands that do appear come from
$\mathsf{PB}_{+}$ one level down:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pd-step1}
\begin{aligned}
D_{a+ab}
&= \{(\varepsilon,[0])\} \cup \{(b,[1])\}
&&\mathsf{PB}_{+},\ r = a,\ s = ab \\
D_{r_1}
&= \{(\mathit{smartC}(\varepsilon,\, b+\varepsilon),\ [0]),\
    (\mathit{smartC}(b,\, b+\varepsilon),\ [1])\}
&&\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}},\ n(a+ab) = \mathrm{false} \\
&= \{(b+\varepsilon,\ [0]),\ (b\,(b+\varepsilon),\ [1])\}
&&\mathsf{SC}\ \text{twice}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The two applications of $\mathsf{SC}$ take different branches, which is
why the two strands look so different. Against the first, $r' =
\varepsilon$, so $\mathsf{SC}$ absorbs it and leaves $b+\varepsilon$
alone; against the second, $r' = b \neq \varepsilon$, so it keeps the
concatenation. The trace agrees:

\begin{verbatim}
Step 1 (char: 'a', position 1)
      rule: pDerivBC('a', r1.r2)  [nullable(r1)=false]
           = [(smartC(r1',r2), bs) | (r1',bs) <- pDerivBC(c,r1)]
  frontier after (2 residual(s)):
    [0] Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)   bits = [0]
    [1] Seq(Lit('b'), Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))   bits = [1]
\end{verbatim}

Both bits were emitted by $\mathsf{PB}_{+}$ one level down, and
$\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ carried them outward unchanged, which is
why neither strand's bits grew at this step. Where the plain bit-coded
construction of Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} kept the two
alternatives inside one expression, joined by $\oplus$, they are now two
separate objects that will be advanced independently.

The second character steps \emph{both} strands, and each is matched
against the rule set on its own, so two different rules fire on the same
character:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pd-step2}
\begin{aligned}
D_{b+\varepsilon}
&= \{(\varepsilon,[0])\} \cup \{\}
&&\mathsf{PB}_{+},\ r = b,\ s = \varepsilon;\ \mathsf{PB}_{\varepsilon} \\
D_{b\,(b+\varepsilon)}
&= \{(\mathit{smartC}(\varepsilon,\, b+\varepsilon),\ [\,])\}
   = \{(b+\varepsilon,\ [\,])\}
&&\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}},\ n(b) = \mathrm{false}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Strand \texttt{[0]} is an alternation whose left disjunct contributes
$\varepsilon$ tagged $0$ and whose right contributes nothing, since
$\mathsf{PB}_{\varepsilon}$ returns the empty set. Strand \texttt{[1]}
is a concatenation whose left factor is a bare literal, so
$\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ applies and only that factor is derived.
Each strand's accumulated bits are its own prefix followed by whatever
the step produced, so \texttt{[0]} gains a $0$ and reaches
\texttt{[0,0]} while \texttt{[1]} gains nothing and stays at
\texttt{[1]}:

\begin{verbatim}
Step 2 (char: 'b', position 2)
    from [0]:
      rule: pDerivBC('b', r1+r2) = tag(0, pDerivBC('b',r1)) ++
        tag(1, pDerivBC('b',r2)), nub2
      -> (Eps, [0])
    from [1]:
      rule: pDerivBC('b', r1.r2)  [nullable(r1)=false]
           = [(smartC(r1',r2), bs) | (r1',bs) <- pDerivBC(c,r1)]
      -> (Alt(Lit('b'), Eps), [])
  frontier after (2 residual(s)):
    [0] Eps   bits = [00]
    [1] Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)   bits = [1]
\end{verbatim}

Selection now scans this final frontier in list order and takes the
first nullable strand. Strand \texttt{[0]}, $(\varepsilon,[0,0])$, is
checked and accepted before strand \texttt{[1]} is inspected at all,
although \texttt{[1]} is nullable too:

\begin{verbatim}
selected residual's accumulated bits ++ mkEpsBC(residual) = [00]

decode((a|ab)(b|), [00]):
  = (Left a, Left b)
\end{verbatim}

This is the leftmost-commits rule made mechanical. Strand \texttt{[0]}
is the "matched $\mathtt{a}$, then $\mathtt{b}$ separately" reading,
the first alternative $\mathit{pDerivBC}$ generated back at step one, and
it wins purely because it comes first in the frontier, not because any
length comparison favours it. The decoded tree is the \emph{shorter}
reading, and it is the opposite answer from
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s
$(\mathit{Right}(a,b),\mathit{Right}())$ on the identical expression and
input. Nothing about $r_1$ or \texttt{"ab"} changed between the two
traces. Only the policy governing which nullable candidate wins did.

\paragraph{Where \texttt{nub2} is and is not applied.} \texttt{nub2}
appears inside $\mathit{pDerivBC}$, once per case that can produce more
than one strand, and nowhere else. In particular the frontier-driving
loop concatenates the per-strand results into the next frontier without
deduplicating across them, so the guarantee \texttt{nub2} provides is
local to a single strand's expansion and does not extend to the frontier
as a whole. Two strands that were distinct at step $i$ can converge on
the same residual at step $i+1$ and both survive.
Section~\ref{sec:frontier-growth} measures what that costs.



\section{Rust Implementation: \texttt{pderiv\_bc}}
\label{sec:partial-derivative-parser-rust}
\label{sec:pderiv-bc-theory-rust}

The module \texttt{pderiv\_bc/pderiv.rs} transcribes
$\mathit{pDerivBC}$ case for case. Each $D_r$ becomes a vector of
\texttt{(Regex, Vec<bool>)} pairs and not a set, because order is what
encodes priority here, and a \texttt{HashSet} would discard exactly the
information selection depends on. \texttt{mk\_eps\_bits} implements
$\mathit{mkEpsBC}_r$. \texttt{nub2} deduplicates on the residual
alone, through a \texttt{HashSet} used only to test membership, keeping
the first-seen pair for each distinct residual; this is the one place in
the codebase where \texttt{Regex}'s derived \texttt{Hash} implementation
(Section~\ref{sec:regex-rust-implementation}) is load-bearing.
The \texttt{Seq} case matches the reference literally, down to the same
asymmetric use of plain \texttt{Regex::seq} in place of
\texttt{smart\_seq} for its first strand.

\texttt{step\_frontier} and \texttt{select\_bits}
(\texttt{src/parsers/pderiv\_bc/parse.rs}) implement the loop of
Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory}. At each character,
\texttt{step\_frontier} replaces every \texttt{(residual, bits)} pair
with the union of \texttt{pderiv\_bc} applied to it, concatenating each
result's bits onto that strand's accumulated prefix. At the end of the
input, \texttt{select\_bits} scans in list order and returns the first
nullable strand's \texttt{bits ++ mk\_eps\_bits(residual)}.
\texttt{parse\_pderiv\_bc} drives the loop and passes the winning
bit-string, together with the \emph{original} \texttt{Regex} and never
the derived residual, to \texttt{decode} (Section~\ref{sec:decode}).
That is the same shared decoder \texttt{deriv\_bc} uses, since both
constructions arrive at the identical \texttt{(Regex, bits)} shape by
the time decoding is needed, whatever route each took to get there.



\section{Rust Implementation: \texttt{pderiv\_std}}
\label{sec:pderiv-std-theory-rust}

\texttt{pderiv\_std} (\texttt{src/parsers/pderiv\_std/}) is this
project's own construction, with no counterpart in either reference. Its
purpose is to stand to \texttt{pderiv\_bc} as \texttt{deriv\_std} stands
to \texttt{deriv\_bc}: the same traversal, the same disambiguation
policy and the same answers, but building a \texttt{ParseTree} directly
instead of a bit-string that has to be decoded afterwards. Having both
means this chapter's answer is computed twice by routes sharing almost
no code, which is what makes the agreement check of
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-worked-example} worth anything.

\subsection{What Each Strand Carries}

Where \texttt{pderiv\_bc} pairs each residual with a
\texttt{Vec<bool>}, \texttt{pderiv\_std} pairs it with an
\emph{injection closure}, defined in this module's \texttt{inject.rs}:

\begin{verbatim}
pub(crate) type Inj = Rc<dyn Fn(ParseTree) -> ParseTree>;
\end{verbatim}

A strand's \texttt{Inj} is a function that, given the parse tree of that
strand's \emph{residual}, returns the parse tree of the \emph{original}
expression. The frontier starts with one strand carrying
\texttt{identity\_inj()}, and each step composes the step's own
injection inside the strand's accumulated one.
Figure~\ref{fig:strand-state} contrasts the two representations after
two steps.

This is the same idea as Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s
$\mathit{inj}$, with one difference that matters.
\texttt{deriv\_std} applies \texttt{inject} once per character during an
explicit backward pass, walking back along the expression sequence as it
unwinds; \texttt{pderiv\_std} never runs a backward pass at all. It
builds the composition forward, one layer per step, and applies the
finished composition exactly once at the end. In that sense
\texttt{pderiv\_std} is as single-pass as \texttt{pderiv\_bc}: the
closure chain plays the role the bit-string plays there.

The \texttt{Rc} is required and not incidental. A single step can expand
one strand into several, and every successor needs the same parent
injection, so the parent has to be shared, not cloned or moved.
\texttt{Rc<dyn Fn(...)>} gives shared ownership of a heap-allocated
closure at the cost of a reference-count bump per successor.
Section~\ref{sec:heap-allocation} measures what that costs against
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s flat \texttt{Vec<bool>}; the structural point here
is the one Figure~\ref{fig:strand-state} draws, that a strand's
accumulated state is a chain whose length grows with the input where
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s is a contiguous buffer.

\subsection{One Table, Two Representations}

The module \texttt{pderiv\_std/pderiv\_tree.rs} is the
\texttt{(Regex, Inj)} analogue of \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, case for case,
with each case's bit-tagging replaced by the tree constructor that bit
would have decoded to:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Case & \texttt{pderiv\_bc} attaches & \texttt{pderiv\_tree} attaches \\
\hline
$\varepsilon$, $\emptyset$ & no strands & no strands \\
$x$ (matching) & \texttt{[]} & \texttt{|\_| Char(x)} \\
$r+s$, left & \texttt{0:}\textit{bs} & \texttt{|v| Left(inj1(v))} \\
$r+s$, right & \texttt{1:}\textit{bs} & \texttt{|v| Right(inj2(v))} \\
$rs$, continue $r$ & \textit{bs} & \texttt{|Pair(v1,v2)| Pair(inj1(v1), v2)} \\
$rs$, jump to $s$ & $\mathit{mkEpsBC}_r$ \texttt{++} \textit{bs} & \texttt{|v2| Pair(mk\_eps(r), inj2(v2))} \\
$r^{*}$ & \texttt{0:}\textit{bs} & \texttt{|Pair(v1,vs)| Star(inj1(v1) : vs)} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every row is the same decision, expressed once as a bit and once as a
constructor. The correspondence is exact enough that the "jump to $s$"
row calls the shared \texttt{mk\_eps} (Section~\ref{sec:mk-eps-impl}) at
precisely the point \texttt{pderiv\_bc} calls \texttt{mk\_eps\_bits},
and for the reason $\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{R}$ gave in
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}: the left factor matched empty, so
its contribution to the pair has to be synthesized and not derived.
\texttt{nub\_tree} is the \texttt{Inj}-carrying \texttt{nub2},
deduplicating on the residual alone and keeping the first occurrence, so
the priority order is preserved identically.

\subsection{Why the Smart Constructor Cannot Be Used Here}

\texttt{pderiv\_bc} is free to apply $\mathit{smartC}$ in its
non-nullable \texttt{Seq} case and in its \texttt{Star} case, collapsing
$\varepsilon\cdot s$ to $s$. \texttt{pderiv\_tree} is not, and uses
plain \texttt{Regex::seq} everywhere its sibling uses the smart one. The
reason follows directly from what an \texttt{Inj} is applied to.

Selection ends by calling \texttt{inj(mk\_eps(r))} on the winning
strand, so each accumulated closure is applied to a tree whose
\emph{shape is determined by its own residual}. The \texttt{Seq}
strands' closures pattern-match on \texttt{ParseTree::Pair}, which is
what \texttt{mk\_eps} produces for a \texttt{Seq} residual. Collapsing
\texttt{Seq(Eps, s)} to \texttt{s} would make \texttt{mk\_eps} produce
$s$'s own tree shape instead, and the closure expecting a \texttt{Pair}
would receive something else. The residual and the injection are two
halves of one invariant, and a smart constructor rewrites one half
without the other.

This is not a speculative concern. Substituting \texttt{smart\_seq} for
\texttt{Regex::seq} in the two \texttt{Seq} strands of
\texttt{pderiv\_tree} makes the test suite fail immediately, on the
simplest possible sequence pattern, with the panic that case's own guard
raises:

\begin{verbatim}
---- parsers::pderiv_std::parse::tests::seq_matches stdout ----
thread '...::seq_matches' panicked at
  src/parsers/pderiv_std/pderiv_tree.rs:71:38:
pderiv_tree: Seq(non-nullable) strand expected Pair, got Char('b')
\end{verbatim}

The panics in \texttt{pderiv\_tree} therefore serve the purpose those in
\texttt{inject} (Section~\ref{sec:inject-impl}) and \texttt{decode}
(Section~\ref{sec:decode}) serve: they encode a precondition that holds
by construction and convert a violation into an immediate, located
failure instead of a silently wrong parse tree.

\subsection{The Same Example, and What the Trace Cannot Show}

The driver in \texttt{src/parsers/pderiv\_std/parse.rs} mirrors its
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} counterpart exactly. \texttt{step\_frontier} expands
every strand and composes injections; \texttt{select} scans in list
order and returns \texttt{inj(mk\_eps(r))} for the first nullable
residual; \texttt{parse\_pderiv\_std} returns \texttt{None} as soon as
the frontier becomes empty, since a frontier with no strands can never
regain one. As with \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, no deduplication is applied
across the flattened frontier, only within each strand's own expansion.

Running $r_1$ against $\mathtt{ab}$ produces the same two-strand fork at
step one and the same winner at step two. Two differences from
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s trace are visible, and both were predicted by the
smart-constructor argument above.

The first is the residuals themselves. Where \texttt{pderiv\_bc} lists
\texttt{Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)}, this parser lists
\texttt{Seq(Eps, Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))}; where that one lists
\texttt{Seq(Lit('b'), ...)}, this one lists
\texttt{Seq(Seq(Eps, Lit('b')), ...)}. The $\varepsilon$ its sibling
discards is exactly the one whose \texttt{Pair} shape the closure needs.

The second follows from the first, and is sharper: because the residuals
differ, the two parsers do not even take the same rules. At step two,
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s strand \texttt{[0]} is an \texttt{Alt} and fires
$\mathsf{PB}_{+}$, while \texttt{pderiv\_std}'s is a \texttt{Seq} whose
left factor is the retained \texttt{Eps}, so it fires the
\emph{nullable} sequence rule instead:

\begin{verbatim}
      rule: pderiv_tree('b', r1.r2)  [nullable(r1)=true]
           = nub_tree $ [(r1'.r2, inj: continue r1) | ...]
                     ++ [(r2', inj: mk_eps(r1) paired with r2's
                          result) | ...]
\end{verbatim}

The two parsers are therefore not running over the same residuals, and
not applying the same rules to them, and they still select the same
tree. Because \texttt{nub\_tree} deduplicates on residual syntax, they
can in principle prune at different points as well, which makes their
agreement on every tested case a stronger check than it would be if they
shared a residual representation.

One thing the trace cannot show is what each strand would build if it
won:

\begin{verbatim}
      -> Seq(Eps, Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))  (+ injection closure, not
        printable)
\end{verbatim}

A \texttt{Vec<bool>} can be printed; an \texttt{Rc<dyn Fn>} cannot. The
trace reports which residuals are alive and which rule produced each,
but not the tree each carries, which is precisely the information
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s \texttt{bits = [00]} annotations do carry. That is
a direct cost of the representation choice in
Figure~\ref{fig:strand-state}: a trace can only report state the
construction actually keeps, and this one does not keep the bits.



\section{Frontier Growth}
\label{sec:frontier-growth}

Both parsers inherit Antimirov's finiteness result for the set of
distinct partial derivatives
(Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}), and neither inherits
a bound on the size of the frontier, for the reason
Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory} identified: the
frontier holds \texttt{(residual, extra)} pairs, and only the residual
component is subject to $pd$'s bound. This section measures the
consequence.

Take $(a + (b + ab))^{*}$, the \texttt{paper\_r2} expression, against
$(\mathtt{ab})^{n}$, and record both the number of strands in the
frontier after each character and the number of \emph{distinct}
residuals among them.

Every strand beyond the second is a duplicate of one already present,
differing only in the bits or injection it carries. \texttt{nub2} does
not remove them because it never sees them together: it runs inside
$\mathit{pDerivBC}$, on the successors of one strand, and these
duplicates arise \emph{between} strands, at the point
\texttt{step\_frontier} concatenates the per-strand results.

This is the partial-derivative counterpart of
Section~\ref{sec:simplification-rules-theory}'s finding for
\texttt{deriv\_bc}, and the two have one root cause seen from different
sides. There, alternation branches that were structurally identical
survived deduplication because their bit annotations made them
syntactically distinct. Here, residuals that are syntactically identical
survive because deduplication is never applied at the level where they
meet. In both cases the bounded object is the set of residual
\emph{shapes}, and in both cases what actually grows is the set of
shapes paired with accumulated parse-tree state.

\paragraph{Deduplicating across the frontier.} The obvious question is
whether the duplicates can simply be dropped. Moving \texttt{nub2} out
of $\mathit{pDerivBC}$ and applying it across the whole flattened
frontier, keeping the first occurrence, is a change of one
deduplication site, and it preserves the priority order for the same
reason \texttt{nub2} does inside a single expansion: the frontier is
built in priority order, so the first occurrence of a residual is the
highest-priority strand that reaches it.

Whether that argument is enough to preserve the selected \emph{tree} is
a different question, and this thesis does not prove it. It was tested
instead, by running both variants over the 300 generated expressions and
39 inputs of Section~\ref{sec:cross-implementation-equivalence},
11\,700 cases in total. The two agree on every case, on membership and
on the exact tree returned, and the peak frontier over the whole run
falls from 170 strands to 4.

The shipped \texttt{pderiv\_bc} nonetheless keeps \texttt{nub2} where
the reference puts it, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-theory}: this parser's correctness criterion
is literal agreement with \texttt{pDerivBC}, and a change verified on
11\,700 cases but not proved is a change to the thing being verified
against. Section~\ref{sec:further-performance-optimizations} records it
as an available optimization and not an applied one, with the
measurement above as the evidence for its value.



\section{The Two Policies Compared}
\label{sec:pderiv-worked-example}

Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem} constructed by hand the
\emph{second} valid derivation of $r_1 = (a + ab)(b + \varepsilon)$
against $\mathtt{ab}$ (\texttt{paper\_r1}) that
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} set aside:
$v' = (\mathit{Left}\ a, \mathit{Left}\ b)$, matching the \emph{first}
alternative of $a + ab$ against $\mathtt{a}$ alone instead of the second
against $\mathtt{ab}$ in one step. That is this chapter's result, and
both of its parsers have now been shown computing it.
Figure~\ref{fig:posix-vs-greedy} sets the two answers side by side.

The bit-strings underneath make the comparison exact. Four alternation
choices exist in $r_1$, two of which are reached on any given parse, and
the two parsers disagree on both: \texttt{[1,1]} against \texttt{[0,0]}.
Nothing else differs. The same expression, the same input, the same
membership answer, and the entire distinction between the two chapters
compressed into two bits.

\paragraph{Failure behaviour is shared.} Against $\mathtt{b}$ instead of
$\mathtt{ab}$, the first partial derivative of $r_1$ produces no
residual at all, since neither alternative of $a + ab$ begins with
$\mathtt{b}$:

\begin{verbatim}
      rule: pDerivBC('b', r1.r2)  [nullable(r1)=false]
           = [(smartC(r1',r2), bs) | (r1',bs) <- pDerivBC(c,r1)]
      -> []
  frontier after (0 residual(s)):
  no nullable residual in this frontier  [FAIL]
\end{verbatim}

An empty frontier is the partial-derivative counterpart of
\texttt{deriv\_bc} simplifying to \texttt{phi}
(Section~\ref{sec:deriv-worked-example}), and it is detected the same
way but earlier: both parsers return as soon as the frontier empties,
without consuming the rest of the input, because a frontier with no
strands has no way to acquire one. For a long input whose first
character already fails, that is the difference between one step and
$n$.

Both then emit the same error summary as
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s two, character for character:

\begin{verbatim}
Failure at position:  1 (1-indexed)
Found:                'b'
Expected:             'a'
\end{verbatim}

The two chapters' constructions disagree, deliberately, on which tree a
successful match produces, and agree exactly on the Boolean question of
membership and on the diagnostics reported for a non-match. Neither the
disambiguation policy nor the choice between bits and closures is ever
reached until the underlying walk has already found at least one live
residual. The full reports are
Appendix~\ref{app:report-pderiv-std-nomatch} and
Appendix~\ref{app:report-pderiv-bc-nomatch}.

\paragraph{All five entry points at once.} The \texttt{--parser all}
mode runs every parser on one input and checks the three invariants this
and the previous chapter claim, with no diagnostics level selected:

\begin{verbatim}
$ cargo run -- parse "(a|ab)(b|)" "ab" --parser all
Regex: (a|ab)(b|)
Input: "ab"

Parser       | Policy | Result
-------------+--------+-------------------------------
DERIV_REC    | POSIX  | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
DERIV_LOOP   | POSIX  | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
DERIV_BC     | POSIX  | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
PDERIV_STD   | GREEDY | (Left a, Left b) -> "ab"
PDERIV_BC    | GREEDY | (Left a, Left b) -> "ab"

POSIX parsers agree
PDERIV parsers agree
GREEDY agrees with POSIX on membership
\end{verbatim}

The three closing lines are the invariants themselves, checked on this
input: the three derivative entry points must return identical trees,
the two partial-derivative ones must return identical trees, and all
five must agree on whether the input matched. Nothing requires the two
\emph{policies} to agree, and on this input they do not, which is the
entire subject of both chapters.
Section~\ref{sec:correctness-verification} runs the same check at scale.

\paragraph{What the substitution cost.} Replacing $r\backslash x$ with
$pd(r,x)$ changed three things, and only the first was intended. The
representation changed, from one expression to a frontier of strands.
The bound changed: Antimirov's theorem bounds the residual shapes, and
Section~\ref{sec:frontier-growth} showed the frontier growing anyway,
because what the algorithm carries is shapes paired with parse-tree
state. And the answer changed, because the left-preference that
$\mathit{mkEps}$ applied to a single alternation had nowhere to live in
a set, and \texttt{nub2}'s first-occurrence-wins rule took its place.
The last of these is not a consequence of partial derivatives as such.
It is a consequence of how this particular construction keeps its
frontier finite, and Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory}
set out why a construction of this shape has little room to do
otherwise.
