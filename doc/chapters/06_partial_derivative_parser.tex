\chapter{Partial-Derivative-Based Parser}
\label{sec:partial-derivative-parser}

Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} built two parsers around Brzozowski's
derivative, both computing the identical POSIX $\geq_r$-maximal tree of
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}. This chapter repeats the
exercise with Antimirov's partial derivative from
Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives} in place of $r
\backslash x$ throughout. As Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory}
shows first, repeating the construction with a different derivative
operator does not repeat its disambiguation guarantee.

Neither flops14-extended \citep{sulzmannlu2014} nor Sulzmann's
teaching-page Haskell reference \citep{sulzmann_slides} defines a POSIX
partial-derivative parser at all. The paper's bit-coded construction
(Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}) is Brzozowski-derivative-only, and
the teaching page's own bit-coded partial-derivative parser,
\texttt{pDerivBC}, is built around a deduplication rule this chapter
shows to be fundamentally Greedy rather than POSIX. This chapter
therefore builds two \emph{Greedy} parsers:
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-theory-rust}), following
\texttt{pDerivBC} directly, and \texttt{pderiv\_std}
(Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-std-theory-rust}), this project's own
construction, built to give \texttt{pderiv\_bc} the same kind of matched
non-bit-coded counterpart Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s
\texttt{deriv\_bc} has in \texttt{deriv\_std}. Both are verified to agree
with each other exactly, and with the two POSIX parsers on membership
alone, in Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} and again in
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-worked-example}.

Trace excerpts are used here exactly as in
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}, under the conventions
Section~\ref{sec:deriv-tracing} sets out, and against the same running
example. The two parsers' level-3 reports have their own section
structure, since a frontier is not a single expression:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\hline
Report section & Construction phase \\
\hline
\texttt{INITIAL FRONTIER} & the one-strand starting state \\
\texttt{FORWARD PASS} & one block per character: before, rule, after \\
\texttt{SELECTION} & the first-nullable-strand decision \\
\texttt{MKEEPSBC + DECODE} & bit-coded only; \texttt{pderiv\_std} injects in place \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The four full reports are
Appendices~\ref{app:report-pderiv-std-match},
\ref{app:report-pderiv-bc-match},
\ref{app:report-pderiv-std-nomatch}
and~\ref{app:report-pderiv-bc-nomatch}.



\section{Extending Derivatives to Partial Derivatives}
\label{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory}

Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives} already defined $pd(r,x)$
as a \emph{set} of residual expressions rather than one combined
expression, and noted the NFA reading: each element of $pd(r,x)$ is one
state of an implicit NFA the original $r$ denotes, alive after reading
$x$. Extending this to parsing means pairing each surviving element of
that set with enough information to reconstruct a parse tree once a
particular strand turns out to be the winning one at the end of the
input. Concretely, that is either an accumulated bit-string per strand
(Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-theory}) or, in this project's own
extension, an accumulated tree-injection closure per strand
(Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-std-theory-rust}).

Either way the algorithm shape is the same, and
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} draws it: maintain a \emph{frontier} of
\texttt{(residual, extra)} pairs, one per live strand, replace it at
each character with the union of $pd$ applied to every current strand,
each contributing its own possibly-empty set of successor strands, and
at the end of the input select the first strand in the frontier whose
residual is nullable.

\paragraph{Why the result is Greedy, not POSIX.} That last step, "the
\emph{first} nullable strand in the frontier", is where this
construction's disambiguation policy is actually decided, and it is
decided implicitly, as a consequence of how the frontier's growth is
kept finite rather than by any explicit ordering rule analogous to
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}'s (A1) to (A4) and (K1) to
(K4).

Antimirov's finiteness theorem
(Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}) guarantees the set of
\emph{distinct} partial derivatives is bounded, but nothing about the
extended, tree-or-bit-carrying construction guarantees strands stay
distinct as they accumulate: two different derivation paths can easily
reach the identical residual \emph{shape} by two different routes, each
carrying its own bits or injection. Kept without pruning, this set would
multiply extra copies of an already-fully-explored residual instead of
staying within the bounded, syntactic set $pd$ itself guarantees.
Pruned instead by comparing the accumulated bits or trees for
POSIX-style "longest match wins", the way
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}'s ordering does, the comparison
would need to look inside possibly-incomplete, still-growing accumulated
state rather than a finished \texttt{ParseTree}, which is not what the
ordering relation of Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost} is defined
over.

The Haskell reference's actual choice is \texttt{nub2}: deduplicate
purely on residual \emph{shape}, keeping whichever strand reached that
shape \emph{first}. This sidesteps the problem entirely by discarding
the comparison altogether. Once a residual shape has been claimed by
some strand, every later strand reaching the identical shape is simply
dropped, regardless of what either one's accumulated bits or tree would
eventually have produced. "First" here means first in the list-ordered
traversal $pd$'s own case structure produces, which explores the left
operand of $+$ before the right at every step, so "first claim wins" is
exactly Greedy's leftmost-commits-immediately rule
(Section~\ref{sec:greedy-leftmost}) and not POSIX's
longest-wins-with-left-as-tiebreak rule.

The trace states the consequence in one line, at the point where the
decision is made:
\begin{verbatim}
SELECTION (first nullable residual, priority/list order)
any nullable residual in final frontier = true -> proceed to
  mkEpsBC + decode
\end{verbatim}
There is no comparison here, because there is nothing to compare
against: the moment a nullable residual is found, scanning stops. That
is the shaded node in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}, and the strand below it
is never examined even though it is nullable too.

This reading is this thesis's own account of why the construction lands
on Greedy rather than POSIX; neither primary source states it in these
terms. Both parsers built in this chapter are verified empirically in
Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} to compute exactly the Greedy answer
wherever it is known to differ from POSIX, including the
\texttt{paper\_r1} and \texttt{paper\_r2} examples of
Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem}, which is the strongest
evidence available for it in the absence of a reference construction to
check against directly.

\paragraph{A family of witnesses.} The two paper examples are not the
only place the divergence is visible, and it is worth having a case
where the pattern is simple enough that both answers can be read off by
inspection. Take $r = (b + bb)^{*}$ against a run of $\mathtt{b}$s.
POSIX's rule (A1) prefers the longer alternative at each iteration,
while Greedy commits to the leftmost alternative that works, so the two
policies partition the same input differently:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Input & POSIX & Greedy \\
\hline
\texttt{"bb"} & \texttt{[Right (b, b)]} & \texttt{[Left b, Left b]} \\
\texttt{"bbb"} & \texttt{[Right (b, b), Left b]} & \texttt{[Left b, Left b, Left b]} \\
\texttt{"bbbbbb"} & \texttt{[Right (b, b)} $\times 3$\texttt{]} & \texttt{[Left b]} $\times 6$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

POSIX uses as few iterations as the longest-alternative rule allows,
padding with a single \texttt{Left b} only when the length is odd;
Greedy uses one iteration per character throughout, because $b$ is the
left alternative and it always matches. Both flatten back to the same
input, and both are valid parse trees for it. Only the policy differs.
These rows are the output of
\texttt{cargo run -- parse "(b|bb)*" "bb" --parser all}, which runs all
five entry points on one input and prints the trees side by side.



\section{Bit-Coded Variant}
\label{sec:partial-derivative-parser-bitcoded-theory}
\label{sec:pderiv-bc-theory}

Sulzmann's teaching-page Haskell transcript gives a bit-coded
partial-derivative construction, \texttt{pDerivBC}, that this thesis's
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} follows directly \citep{sulzmann_slides}. It extends
$pd$ with per-strand bits the same way
Section~\ref{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory}'s $\backslash_b$ extends
the Brzozowski derivative, but, notably, keeps those bits
\emph{external} to the regular-expression tree, as a plain list carried
alongside a plain, un-annotated $r$, rather than folding them into an
\texttt{ARegex}-shaped tree the way $\backslash_b$ does.

Writing $D_r$ for $\mathit{pDerivBC}(x,r)$ throughout, to keep the set
comprehensions readable, the base and non-sequence cases are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pderivbc}
\begin{aligned}
D_{\varepsilon} &= \{\} \qquad\qquad D_{\emptyset} = \{\}
  &&(\mathsf{PB}_{\varepsilon}),\ (\mathsf{PB}_{\emptyset}) \\
D_{y} &= \begin{cases} \{(\varepsilon, [\,])\} & x = y \\
                       \{\} & x \neq y \end{cases}
  &&(\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{lit}}) \\
D_{r+s} &= \mathit{nub2}\big(
  \{(r', 0{:}bs) \mid (r',bs) \in D_r\} \\
  &\qquad\quad \cup\ \{(s', 1{:}bs) \mid (s',bs) \in D_s\}\big)
  &&(\mathsf{PB}_{+}) \\
D_{r^{*}} &= \mathit{nub2}\big(
  \{(\mathit{smartC}(r', r^{*}),\, 0{:}bs) \mid (r',bs) \in D_r\}\big)
  &&(\mathsf{PB}_{*})
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and the sequence case, Equation~\eqref{eq:pderivbc-seq}, splits on the
nullability of the left factor exactly as
Equation~\eqref{eq:derivative} does:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pderivbc-seq}
\begin{aligned}
D_{rs} &= \{(\mathit{smartC}(r',s),\, bs) \mid (r',bs) \in D_r\}
  && (\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}),\ \text{if } n(r) = \mathrm{false} \\
D_{rs} &= \mathit{nub2}\big(
  \{(r's,\ bs) \mid (r',bs) \in D_r\} \\
  &\qquad\quad \cup\ \{(s',\ \mathit{mkEpsBC}_r \mathbin{+\!\!+} bs)
       \mid (s',bs) \in D_s\}\big)
  && (\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}),\ \text{if } n(r) = \mathrm{true}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The names extend Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}'s
$\mathsf{P}$ scheme, one $\mathsf{PB}$ rule per $\mathsf{P}$ rule, and
the worked example below cites them with the binding of $r$ and $s$
given at each application. The two sequence cases differ in exactly
one respect worth keeping in view: $\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ builds its
residual with $\mathit{smartC}$, while $\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$
builds the first of its two strands with a plain $r's$.
Here $\mathit{mkEpsBC}_r$ is the plain-$r$ analogue of
Section~\ref{sec:mk-eps-bc}'s $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$, identical in structure
but walking an un-annotated tree instead of an \texttt{ARegex}, and
\texttt{nub2} deduplicates a list of \texttt{(residual, bits)} pairs
purely by comparing the residual, keeping the first occurrence.
$\mathit{smartC}$ is the smart concatenation constructor of
Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}, collapsing
$\varepsilon \cdot s$ to $s$.

The concatenation case's first strand deliberately keeps $r'$ wrapped in
a literal, non-smart-constructed $r's$ rather than $\mathit{smartC}$.
This asymmetry is present in the reference itself and is preserved here
rather than "corrected", since correctness of this construction is
judged against literal agreement with that reference
(Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}) and not against an independently-derived
ideal form. Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-std-theory-rust} returns to the same
question from the other side, where the choice between the two
constructors is not free at all.



\section{Rust Implementation: The \texttt{pderiv\_bc} Parser}
\label{sec:partial-derivative-parser-rust}
\label{sec:pderiv-bc-theory-rust}

\texttt{src/parsers/pderiv\_bc/pderiv.rs} transcribes
$\mathit{pDerivBC}$ case for case. Each $D_r$ becomes a
\texttt{Vec<(Regex, Vec<bool>)>} rather than a set. Order matters here,
encoding Greedy priority directly in list position, so a
\texttt{HashSet} would lose exactly the information the construction
depends on. \texttt{mk\_eps\_bits} implements $\mathit{mkEpsBC}_r$;
\texttt{nub2} deduplicates on the residual \texttt{Regex} alone, via a
\texttt{HashSet<Regex>} used only to test membership, keeping the
first-seen pair for each distinct residual and discarding the rest. This
is the one place in the codebase where \texttt{Regex}'s derived
\texttt{Hash} implementation
(Section~\ref{sec:regex-rust-implementation}) is load-bearing.
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own \texttt{Seq} case matches the reference
literally, down to the same asymmetric use of plain \texttt{Regex::seq}
rather than \texttt{smart\_seq} for its first strand.

\texttt{step\_frontier} and \texttt{select\_bits}
(\texttt{src/parsers/pderiv\_bc/parse.rs}) implement the frontier-driving
loop of Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory}. At each
character, \texttt{step\_frontier} replaces every
\texttt{(residual, bits)} pair in the current frontier with the union of
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} applied to it, concatenating each result's bits onto
that strand's already-accumulated prefix. At the end of the input,
\texttt{select\_bits} scans the frontier in list order and returns the
first nullable strand's \texttt{bits ++ mk\_eps\_bits(residual)}.
\texttt{parse\_pderiv\_bc} drives this loop to completion and passes the
winning bit-string, together with the \emph{original} \texttt{Regex} and
never the derived residual, to \texttt{decode}
(Section~\ref{sec:decode}). That is the same shared decoder
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s \texttt{deriv\_bc} uses, since
both constructions end up at the identical \texttt{(Regex, bits)} shape
by the time decoding is needed, whatever route each took to get there.

\paragraph{The worked example, one step at a time.} Run
Section~\ref{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory}'s
$r_1 = (a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$ against $\mathtt{ab}$ through
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} instead of \texttt{deriv\_bc}: same regex, same
input, different parser, so that Greedy and POSIX can be seen
disagreeing on the same worked example rather than only being asserted
to. The frontier starts as a single strand, the whole regex with no bits
yet, which is the trace's \texttt{INITIAL FRONTIER} section and the
leftmost node of Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}.

Deriving with respect to $\mathtt{a}$, the outer node binds
$r = a+ab$ and $s = b+\varepsilon$. That $r$ is not nullable, by
$\mathsf{N}_{+}$ over two non-nullable literals and a concatenation, so
$\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ applies and no second strand is contributed by
$s$. The strands that do appear come from $\mathsf{PB}_{+}$ one level
down, binding $r = a$ and $s = ab$ and tagging the two alternatives
$0$ and $1$. Where the plain bit-coded construction kept one expression,
there are now two strands:
\begin{verbatim}
Step 1 (char: 'a', position 1)
      rule: pDerivBC('a', r1.r2)  [nullable(r1)=false]
           = [(smartC(r1',r2), bs) | (r1',bs) <- pDerivBC(c,r1)]
  frontier after (2 residual(s)):
    [0] Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)   bits = [0]
    [1] Seq(Lit('b'), Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))   bits = [1]
\end{verbatim}
Strand \texttt{[0]} came from $a$'s own derivative, $\varepsilon$, which
$\mathsf{SC}$ then absorbed against $(b+\varepsilon)$ by its first
branch, leaving \texttt{Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)} alone; it carries
\texttt{[0]} for $(a+ab)$'s left disjunct. Strand \texttt{[1]} came from
$ab$'s derivative, $b$, which $\mathsf{SC}$ kept as a concatenation by
its second branch since $b \neq \varepsilon$; it carries \texttt{[1]}
for the right disjunct. Both bits were emitted by $\mathsf{PB}_{+}$ one
level down, and $\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ shown in the rule line carried
them outward unchanged, which is why neither strand's bits grew at this
step.

Deriving with respect to $\mathtt{b}$ steps \emph{both} strands
independently, and the trace prints a rule line per strand, which is
where the frontier's per-strand character becomes explicit:
\begin{verbatim}
Step 2 (char: 'b', position 2)
    from [0]:
      rule: pDerivBC('b', r1+r2) = tag(0, pDerivBC('b',r1)) ++
        tag(1, pDerivBC('b',r2)), nub2
      -> (Eps, [0])
    from [1]:
      rule: pDerivBC('b', r1.r2)  [nullable(r1)=false]
           = [(smartC(r1',r2), bs) | (r1',bs) <- pDerivBC(c,r1)]
      -> (Alt(Lit('b'), Eps), [])
  frontier after (2 residual(s)):
    [0] Eps   bits = [00]
    [1] Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)   bits = [1]
\end{verbatim}
Two different rules fired on the same character, because the two strands
are different expressions and each is matched against the rule set
independently. Strand \texttt{[0]} is an \texttt{Alt}, so
$\mathsf{PB}_{+}$ applies with $r = b$ and $s = \varepsilon$: the left
disjunct contributes $\varepsilon$ tagged $0$, and the right contributes
nothing, since $\mathsf{PB}_{\varepsilon}$ returns the empty set. Strand
\texttt{[1]} is a \texttt{Seq} binding $r = b$ and
$s = b+\varepsilon$, and that $r$ is a bare literal and so not nullable,
so $\mathsf{PB}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ applies and derives only the left factor. The
accumulated bits differ correspondingly: strand \texttt{[0]} gains a
$0$ and reaches \texttt{[00]}, while strand \texttt{[1]} gains nothing
and stays at \texttt{[1]}.

Selection then scans this final frontier in list order and takes the
\emph{first} nullable strand. Strand \texttt{[0]},
\texttt{(Eps, [00])}, is checked and accepted before strand \texttt{[1]}
is even inspected, regardless of strand \texttt{[1]} also being
nullable:
\begin{verbatim}
selected residual's accumulated bits ++ mkEpsBC(residual) = [00]

decode((a|ab)(b|), [00]):
  = (Left a, Left b)
\end{verbatim}
This is Greedy's leftmost-commits rule made mechanical. Strand
\texttt{[0]} is exactly the "matched \texttt{a}, then \texttt{b}
separately" reading, the first alternative $\mathit{pDerivBC}$ generated
back at the first derivative step, and it wins here purely because it
appears first in the frontier, not because any length comparison favors
it. The decoded tree is the \emph{shorter} reading, matching
$\mathtt{a}$ and then $\mathtt{b}$ separately, and it is the opposite
answer from Section~\ref{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory}'s
$(\mathit{Right}(a,b), \mathit{Right}())$ on the identical regex and
input. Nothing about $r_1$ or $\mathtt{"ab"}$ changed between the two
traces; only the disambiguation policy governing which nullable strand
or branch wins did, exactly the distinction
Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory} argues is built into
\texttt{nub2}'s shape-only, first-occurrence-wins deduplication.

\paragraph{Where \texttt{nub2} is and is not applied.}
\texttt{nub2} appears inside \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, once per case that can
produce more than one strand, and nowhere else. In particular
\texttt{step\_frontier} concatenates the per-strand results into the
next frontier without deduplicating across them, so the guarantee
\texttt{nub2} provides is local to a single strand's expansion and does
not extend to the frontier as a whole. Two strands that were distinct at
step $i$ can converge on the same residual at step $i+1$ and both
survive. Section~\ref{sec:frontier-growth} measures what that costs.



\section{Rust Implementation: The \texttt{pderiv\_std} Parser}
\label{sec:pderiv-std-theory-rust}

\texttt{pderiv\_std} (\texttt{src/parsers/pderiv\_std/}) is this
project's own construction, with no counterpart in either reference. Its
purpose is to stand to \texttt{pderiv\_bc} as \texttt{deriv\_std} stands
to \texttt{deriv\_bc}: the same traversal, the same disambiguation
policy, and the same answers, but building a \texttt{ParseTree} directly
instead of a bit-string that has to be decoded afterward. Having both
means the Greedy answer is computed twice by routes that share almost no
code, which is what makes the agreement check of
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-worked-example} worth anything.

\paragraph{What each strand carries.} Where \texttt{pderiv\_bc} pairs
each residual with a \texttt{Vec<bool>}, \texttt{pderiv\_std} pairs it
with an \emph{injection closure} instead, defined in this module's
\texttt{inject.rs}:
\begin{verbatim}
pub(crate) type Inj = Rc<dyn Fn(ParseTree) -> ParseTree>;
\end{verbatim}
A strand's \texttt{Inj} is a function that, given the parse tree of that
strand's \emph{residual}, returns the parse tree of the \emph{original}
expression. The frontier starts with one strand carrying
\texttt{identity\_inj()}, and each step composes the step's own
injection inside the strand's accumulated one.
Figure~\ref{fig:strand-state} contrasts the two representations after
two steps.

This is the same idea as Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s
\texttt{inject}, with one difference that matters:
\texttt{deriv\_std} applies \texttt{inject} once per character during an
explicit backward pass, re-deriving the expression sequence it needs as
it unwinds, whereas \texttt{pderiv\_std} never runs a backward pass at
all. It builds the composition forward, one layer per step, and applies
the finished composition exactly once at the end. In that sense
\texttt{pderiv\_std} is as single-pass as \texttt{pderiv\_bc} is; the
closure chain plays the role the bit-string plays there.

The \texttt{Rc} is required rather than incidental. A single step can
expand one strand into several, and every one of those successors needs
the same parent injection, so the parent has to be shared rather than
cloned or moved. \texttt{Rc<dyn Fn(...)>} gives shared ownership of a
heap-allocated closure at the cost of a reference-count bump per
successor. Section~\ref{sec:heap-allocation} measures what this costs
against \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s flat \texttt{Vec<bool>}; the structural
point here is the one Figure~\ref{fig:strand-state} draws, that a
strand's accumulated state is a chain whose length grows with the input
where \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s is a contiguous buffer.

\paragraph{\texttt{pderiv\_tree}.}
\texttt{src/parsers/pderiv\_std/pderiv\_tree.rs} is the
\texttt{(Regex, Inj)} analogue of \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, case for case,
with each case's bit-tagging replaced by the tree constructor that bit
would have decoded to:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Case & \texttt{pderiv\_bc} attaches & \texttt{pderiv\_tree} attaches \\
\hline
$\varepsilon$, $\emptyset$ & no strands & no strands \\
$x$ (matching) & \texttt{[]} & \texttt{|\_| Char(x)} \\
$r+s$, left & \texttt{0:}\textit{bs} & \texttt{|v| Left(inj1(v))} \\
$r+s$, right & \texttt{1:}\textit{bs} & \texttt{|v| Right(inj2(v))} \\
$rs$, continue $r$ & \textit{bs} & \texttt{|Pair(v1,v2)| Pair(inj1(v1), v2)} \\
$rs$, jump to $s$ & $\mathit{mkEpsBC}_r$ \texttt{++} \textit{bs} & \texttt{|v2| Pair(mk\_eps(r), inj2(v2))} \\
$r^{*}$ & \texttt{0:}\textit{bs} & \texttt{|Pair(v1,vs)| Star(inj1(v1) : vs)} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every row is the same decision, expressed once as a bit and once as a
constructor. The correspondence is exact enough that the \texttt{Seq}
case's "jump to $s$" strand calls the shared \texttt{mk\_eps}
(Section~\ref{sec:mk-eps-impl}) at precisely the point
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} calls \texttt{mk\_eps\_bits}, and for the same
reason: the left factor matched empty, so its contribution to the pair
has to be synthesized rather than derived. \texttt{nub\_tree} is the
\texttt{Inj}-carrying \texttt{nub2}, deduplicating on the residual alone
and keeping the first occurrence, so the Greedy priority order is
preserved identically.

\paragraph{Why the smart constructor cannot be used here.}
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} is free to apply $\mathit{smartC}$ in its
non-nullable \texttt{Seq} case and in its \texttt{Star} case, so as to
collapse $\varepsilon \cdot s$ down to $s$. \texttt{pderiv\_tree} is
not, and uses plain \texttt{Regex::seq} in every position where its
sibling uses the smart one. The reason is a direct consequence of what
an \texttt{Inj} is applied to.

Selection ends by calling \texttt{inj(mk\_eps(r))} on the winning
strand, so each accumulated closure is applied to a tree whose
\emph{shape is determined by its own residual}. The \texttt{Seq} strands'
closures pattern-match on \texttt{ParseTree::Pair}, which is what
\texttt{mk\_eps} produces for a \texttt{Seq} residual. Collapsing
\texttt{Seq(Eps, s)} to \texttt{s} would make \texttt{mk\_eps} produce
\texttt{s}'s own tree shape instead, and the closure expecting a
\texttt{Pair} would receive something else. The residual and the
injection are two halves of one invariant, and a smart constructor
rewrites one half without the other.

This is not a speculative concern. Substitute \texttt{smart\_seq} for
\texttt{Regex::seq} in the two \texttt{Seq} strands of
\texttt{pderiv\_tree}, and the test suite fails immediately, on the
simplest possible sequence pattern, with the panic that case's own guard
raises:
\begin{verbatim}
---- parsers::pderiv_std::parse::tests::seq_matches stdout ----
thread '...::seq_matches' panicked at
  src/parsers/pderiv_std/pderiv_tree.rs:71:38:
pderiv_tree: Seq(non-nullable) strand expected Pair, got Char('b')
\end{verbatim}
The panics in \texttt{pderiv\_tree} therefore serve the same purpose as
those in \texttt{inject} (Section~\ref{sec:inject-impl}) and
\texttt{decode} (Section~\ref{sec:decode}): they encode a precondition
that holds by construction, and they convert a violation into an
immediate, located failure rather than a silently wrong parse tree.

This is also why the two parsers' frontiers look different in the traces
even though they compute the same answer. After one step on $r_1$,
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s first residual is \texttt{Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)} and
\texttt{pderiv\_std}'s is \texttt{Seq(Eps, Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))}: the
same language, the same strand, one of them still carrying the
\texttt{Eps} its sibling discarded. Because \texttt{nub\_tree}
deduplicates on residual syntax, the two parsers can in principle prune
at different points, which makes their agreement on every tested case a
stronger check than it would be if they shared a residual
representation.

\paragraph{Driving the frontier.} The driver in
\texttt{src/parsers/pderiv\_std/parse.rs} mirrors its
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} counterpart exactly. \texttt{step\_frontier} expands
every strand and composes injections; \texttt{select} scans the final
frontier in list order and returns \texttt{inj(mk\_eps(r))} for the
first nullable residual; \texttt{parse\_pderiv\_std} runs the loop,
returning \texttt{None} as soon as the frontier becomes empty, since a
frontier with no strands can never regain one. As with
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}, no deduplication is applied across the flattened
frontier, only within each strand's own expansion.

\paragraph{The same worked example, and what the trace cannot show.}
Running $r_1$ against $\mathtt{ab}$ through \texttt{pderiv\_std}
produces the same two-strand fork at step 1, and the same winner at step
2. The one visible difference from \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s trace is a
recurring annotation:
\begin{verbatim}
      -> Seq(Eps, Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))  (+ injection closure, not
        printable)
      -> Seq(Seq(Eps, Lit('b')), Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))  (+ injection
        closure, not printable)
\end{verbatim}
This is the one place where this parser's state is less inspectable than
its bit-coded sibling's. A \texttt{Vec<bool>} can be printed; an
\texttt{Rc<dyn Fn>} cannot. The trace can therefore show which residuals
are alive and which rule produced each, but not what tree each strand
would build if it won, which is precisely the information
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s \texttt{bits = [00]} annotations do carry. That
limitation is a direct cost of the representation choice drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:strand-state}, and
Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics-scope-limits} returns to it as a general
point about what a tracing layer can and cannot recover.

The residuals themselves also differ from \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s, in
exactly the way the smart-constructor argument above predicts. Where
that parser lists \texttt{Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)}, this one lists
\texttt{Seq(Eps, Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))}; where that one lists
\texttt{Seq(Lit('b'), ...)}, this one lists
\texttt{Seq(Seq(Eps, Lit('b')), ...)}. The two parsers are not running
over the same residuals at all, and still select the same tree.



\section{Frontier Growth}
\label{sec:frontier-growth}

Both parsers of this chapter inherit Antimirov's finiteness result for
the set of distinct partial derivatives
(Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}), and neither inherits
a bound on the size of the frontier, for the reason
Section~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser-theory} identified: the
frontier holds \texttt{(residual, extra)} pairs, and only the residual
component is subject to $pd$'s bound. This section measures the
consequence.

Take $(a + (b + ab))^{*}$, the \texttt{paper\_r2} expression, against
$(\mathtt{ab})^{n}$, and record both the number of strands in the
frontier after each character and the number of \emph{distinct}
residuals among them.

Every strand beyond the second is a duplicate of one already present,
differing only in the bits or injection it carries. \texttt{nub2} does
not remove them because it never sees them together: it runs inside
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}, on the successors of one strand, and the duplicates
here arise \emph{between} strands, at the point
\texttt{step\_frontier} concatenates the per-strand results.

This is the partial-derivative counterpart of
Section~\ref{sec:simplification-rules-theory}'s finding for
\texttt{deriv\_bc}, and the two have the same root cause seen from
different sides. There, alternation branches that were structurally
identical survived deduplication because their bit annotations made them
syntactically distinct. Here, residuals that are syntactically identical
survive because the deduplication is never applied at the level where
they meet. In both cases the bounded object is the set of residual
\emph{shapes}, and in both cases what actually grows is the set of
shapes paired with accumulated parse-tree state.

\paragraph{Deduplicating across the frontier.} The obvious question is
whether the duplicates can simply be dropped. Moving \texttt{nub2} out
of \texttt{pderiv\_bc} and applying it across the whole flattened
frontier, keeping the first occurrence, is a change of one deduplication
site, and it preserves the Greedy priority order for the same reason
\texttt{nub2} does inside a single expansion: the frontier is built in
priority order, so the first occurrence of a residual is the
highest-priority strand that reaches it.

Whether that argument is enough to preserve the selected \emph{tree} is
a different question, and this thesis does not prove it. It was tested
instead, by running both variants over the 300 generated expressions and
39 inputs of Section~\ref{sec:cross-implementation-equivalence}, 11\,700
cases in total. The two agree on every case, on membership and on the
exact tree returned, and the peak frontier over the whole run falls from
170 strands to 4.

The shipped \texttt{pderiv\_bc} nonetheless keeps \texttt{nub2} where
the reference puts it. The reason is the one stated in
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-theory}: this parser's correctness criterion
is literal agreement with \texttt{pDerivBC}, and a change that is
verified on 11\,700 cases but not proved is a change to the thing being
verified against. Section~\ref{sec:further-performance-optimizations}
records it as an available optimization rather than an applied one, with
the measurement above as the evidence for its value.



\section{Comparing the Two Policies}
\label{sec:pderiv-worked-example}

Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem} constructed, by hand, the
\emph{second} valid derivation of $r_1 = (a + ab)(b + \varepsilon)$
against $\mathtt{ab}$ (\texttt{paper\_r1}) that
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} deliberately left aside:
$v' = (\mathit{Left}\ a, \mathit{Left}\ b)$, matching the \emph{first}
alternative of $a + ab$ against $\mathtt{a}$ alone rather than the
second alternative against $\mathtt{ab}$ in one step. That is the Greedy
result, and the two preceding sections have shown both of this chapter's
parsers computing it. Figure~\ref{fig:posix-vs-greedy} sets the two
answers side by side.

The bit-strings underneath make the comparison exact. Four alternation
choices exist in $r_1$, two of which are reached on any given parse, and
the two parsers disagree on both:
\texttt{[1,1]} against \texttt{[0,0]}. Nothing else differs. The same
expression, the same input, the same membership answer, and the entire
distinction between the two chapters compressed into two bits.

\paragraph{Failure behaviour is shared.} Against $\mathtt{b}$ instead of
$\mathtt{ab}$, the first partial derivative of $r_1$ produces no
residual at all, since neither alternative of $a + ab$ begins with
$\mathtt{b}$:
\begin{verbatim}
      rule: pDerivBC('b', r1.r2)  [nullable(r1)=false]
           = [(smartC(r1',r2), bs) | (r1',bs) <- pDerivBC(c,r1)]
      -> []
  frontier after (0 residual(s)):
  no nullable residual in this frontier  [FAIL]
\end{verbatim}
An empty frontier is the partial-derivative counterpart of
\texttt{deriv\_bc} simplifying to \texttt{phi}
(Section~\ref{sec:deriv-worked-example}), and it is detected the same
way but earlier: both \texttt{parse\_pderiv\_std} and
\texttt{parse\_pderiv\_bc} return as soon as the frontier empties,
without consuming the rest of the input, because a frontier with no
strands has no way to acquire one. For a long input whose first
character already fails, that is the difference between one step and
$n$.

Both parsers then emit the same error summary as
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}'s two, character for character:
\begin{verbatim}
Failure at position:  1 (1-indexed)
Found:                'b'
Expected:             'a'
\end{verbatim}
The two chapters' constructions disagree, deliberately, on which tree a
successful match produces, Greedy against POSIX, but agree exactly on
the Boolean question of membership and on the diagnostics reported for a
non-match. Neither disambiguation policy nor the choice between bits and
closures is ever reached until after the underlying derivative or
partial-derivative walk has already found at least one live residual.
The full reports are Appendix~\ref{app:report-pderiv-std-nomatch} and
Appendix~\ref{app:report-pderiv-bc-nomatch}.

\paragraph{All five entry points at once.} The \texttt{--parser all}
mode runs every parser on one input and checks the three invariants this
and the previous chapter claim, without any diagnostics level being
selected:
\begin{verbatim}
$ cargo run -- parse "(a|ab)(b|)" "ab" --parser all
Regex: (a|ab)(b|)
Input: "ab"

Parser       | Policy | Result
-------------+--------+-------------------------------
DERIV_REC    | POSIX  | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
DERIV_LOOP   | POSIX  | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
DERIV_BC     | POSIX  | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
PDERIV_STD   | GREEDY | (Left a, Left b) -> "ab"
PDERIV_BC    | GREEDY | (Left a, Left b) -> "ab"

POSIX parsers agree
PDERIV parsers agree
GREEDY agrees with POSIX on membership
\end{verbatim}
The three closing lines are the invariants themselves, checked on this
input: the three POSIX entry points must return identical trees, the two
Greedy ones must return identical trees, and all five must agree on
whether the input matched. Nothing requires the two \emph{policies} to
agree, and on this input they do not, which is the entire subject of
both chapters. Section~\ref{sec:correctness-verification} runs the same
three checks across the full corpus rather than a single input.
