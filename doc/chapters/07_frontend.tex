\chapter{Translation}
\label{sec:frontend}

The five parsers of Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}
and~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} operate on the
six-constructor \texttt{Regex} of
Chapter~\ref{sec:regular-expressions}. 
Real patterns may be more complex and 
contain shorthand character classes, bounded repetition,
alternation over ranges, and a PCRE flag suffix, none of
which the \texttt{Regex} can represent. 
This chapter introduces \texttt{ExtPat}, 
an AST that represents a surface syntax that allows
for such richer patterns,
and shows how pattern strings are first parsed into this \texttt{ExtPat}
and subsequently translated into the internal \texttt{Regex}.

%
%

\section{Parsing: String to ExtPat}
\label{sec:string-to-extpat}

Take the pattern string \verb|[a-c]+\d{2}|. A recursive-descent
parser turns it into the intermediate tree \texttt{ExtPat},
as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:frontend-worked-extpat}. 
Precedence is fixed by the call chain, from loosest to tightest,
with alternation and concatenation being left-associated
and postfix quantifiers binding most tightly.
Concatenation splits the pattern into \texttt{([a-c]+)(\textbackslash d\{2\})}, 
and the postfix turns them into \texttt{Plus} over a class and a
bounded repetition of a shorthand:
\begin{figure}[ht]
\small
\centering
\begin{verbatim}
Concat [
    Plus(Any(['a', 'b', 'c'])),
    Concat [
        Escape('d'),
        Escape('d')
    ]
]
\end{verbatim}
\caption{The \texttt{ExtPat} produced from
\texttt{[a-c]+{\textbackslash}d\{2\}}.}
\label{fig:frontend-worked-extpat}
\end{figure}

\section{Translate: ExtPat to Regex}
\label{sec:extpat-to-regex}

\texttt{translate} then turns the \texttt{ExtPat} 
into the \texttt{Regex} value, that can be consumed by the parsing algorithms 
of the previous chapters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{Any(['a','b','c'])} expands over the working
        alphabet to a 3-leaf \texttt{Alt} chain.
  \item \texttt{Plus} is rewritten structurally as
        \texttt{Seq(r, Star(r))}.
  \item \texttt{Escape('d')} expands to a 10-leaf
        \texttt{Alt} chain, with one leaf for each digit.
  \item The two \texttt{Concat} layers are translated into
        nested \texttt{Seq} nodes, with the resulting
        structure folded into a single \texttt{Seq} chain.
\end{itemize}

The resulting \texttt{Regex} has 54 nodes
(Figure~\ref{fig:frontend-worked-regex}). The two digit expansions
account for 38 of them, 20 digit leaves and 18 \texttt{Alt} nodes, and
the remaining 16 come from the two \texttt{[a-c]} expansions, the
\texttt{Plus} rewrite, the unrolling, and the surrounding
\texttt{Seq} nodes.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\small
\centering
\begin{verbatim}
Seq(                                             
   Seq(                                            
      Alt(a, Alt(b, c)),                           
      Star(Alt(a, Alt(b, c))))                    
   Seq(
      Seq(                                          
      Eps,                                        
      Alt(0, Alt(1, Alt(2, Alt(3, Alt(4, Alt(5, Alt(6, Alt(7, Alt(8, 9))))))))),
      Alt(0, Alt(1, Alt(2, Alt(3, Alt(4, Alt(5, Alt(6, Alt(7, Alt(8, 9)))))))))
   ))
)
\end{verbatim}
\caption{The full \texttt{Regex} produced by translating
the \texttt{ExtPat}.}
\label{fig:frontend-worked-regex}
\end{figure}

\section{The Surface Syntax}
\label{sec:surface-syntax}

%

\textbf{Rejected constructs:} The example pattern is accepted, but not all are. 
A construct is rejected whenever it cannot be translated without changing what the
pattern denotes.

%

\textbf{Full-string matching:} A translated pattern matches the whole
input or nothing. There is no search padding, no substring search, and
no anchor handling.

%

\textbf{A 98-character working alphabet:} Shorthand and negated
classes enumerate a fixed set of 98 characters, the 95 printable ASCII
characters \verb|0x20|--\verb|0x7E| together with tab, newline, and
carriage return. 
The shorthands expand to \texttt{\textbackslash d} (digit)
\texttt{0}--\texttt{9}, \texttt{\textbackslash w} (word)
\texttt{a}--\texttt{z}, \texttt{A}--\texttt{Z}, \texttt{0}--\texttt{9},
and underscore, and \texttt{\textbackslash s} (whitespace) space, tab,
newline, and carriage return.

%

\textbf{PCRE flags:} Rule sets commonly encode matching options in a
\texttt{/PATTERN/FLAGS} wrapper such as \texttt{/abc/i}, so the suffix
must be interpreted alongside the pattern. Each flag falls into one of
three groups (Table~\ref{tab:flags}).
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{6pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\hline
Flag & Group & Effect \\
\hline
\texttt{i} & honoured & case-fold literal letters \\
\texttt{s} & honoured & dot matches newline \\
\texttt{m} & rejected & anchors match at newlines \\
\texttt{x} & rejected & whitespace insignificant \\
\texttt{R} & ignored  & search positioning \\
\texttt{A} & ignored  & anchor selection \\
\texttt{E} & ignored  & dollar tuning \\
\texttt{U}, \texttt{H}, \texttt{P}, \texttt{C}, ... & ignored
           & buffer or normalisation selection \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{PCRE flags by group. Honoured flags are part of the pattern's
meaning and are reproduced by a structural rewrite on \texttt{ExtPat}. Some flags
are rejected outright. Ignored flags choose a buffer region or search
position and do not affect the denoted language.}
\label{tab:flags}
\end{table}

\texttt{ExtPat::Escape} covers \texttt{\textbackslash d},
\texttt{\textbackslash w}, \texttt{\textbackslash s}, their
capitalised forms, and escaped punctuation such as
\texttt{\textbackslash .}. Control, hexadecimal, and octal
escapes are represented as \texttt{ExtPat::Char}. Capitalised
shorthands and negated classes denote the working alphabet
minus the positive set. Literals can be any Unicode
\texttt{char} and become \texttt{Regex::Lit} values. Thus,
\texttt{é} matches itself, but \texttt{\textbackslash W}
cannot match it. Shorthands expand over the working alphabet;
escaped punctuation becomes one literal, so
\texttt{\textbackslash .} matches only a period. Within
character classes, shorthands expand inline and cannot serve
as range endpoints.

\section{Translation Sizes}
\label{sec:frontend-sizes}

The example in Figure~\ref{fig:frontend-worked-regex} translates
to 54 nodes. Table~\ref{tab:alphabet-expansion-sizes} shows
the contribution of individual character classes. A class with
$n$ members produces $n$ literals and $n-1$ \texttt{Alt} nodes,
for $2n-1$ nodes in total. Negated classes are complemented
against the 98-character working alphabet before expansion.
The dot construct expands to the full working alphabet when
\texttt{s} is set, or to the alphabet excluding newline otherwise.

Translated pattern size depends on both structural and alphabet
expansion, as shown by the effects of repetition and case
folding in Table~\ref{tab:translation-sizes}. 
Repetition increases the size by cloning the repeated
expression, while case folding replaces each letter with an
\texttt{Alt} between its uppercase and lowercase forms, adding
two nodes per letter.

\phantomsection\label{sec:frontend-bounds}
Bounds are another source of growth. Since \texttt{Regex} has
no direct representation for \texttt{ExtPat::Bound}, a pattern
\texttt{r\{m,n\}} becomes $m$ exact copies followed by $n-m$
optional copies. An open upper bound, \texttt{r\{m,\}}, becomes
$m$ copies followed by a \texttt{Star}. Since both forms clone the
body, the resulting size grows with the bound.

However, the implementation must impose limits to keep translated
patterns manageable.
Unrolling clones the body for each
repetition, so large bounds can cause substantial growth.
This justifies setting a limit in \texttt{translate},
to rejects bounded repetitions whose \texttt{m} or
\texttt{n} exceeds 1000.
This limits individual repetitions but not total size, as nested bounds
and multiple classes can still compound. 
As will be seen in Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}, it is also
necessary to limit the translated \texttt{Regex} to 20{.}000
nodes and its estimated recursion depth to 2000.
