\chapter{External To Internal Regex}
\label{sec:frontend}

The examples presented thus far have been expressed directly in terms of
\texttt{Regex}, the six-constructor core algebra introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:regex-rust-implementation}. This is the representation
consumed by \texttt{deriv} and \texttt{pderiv}. External regular-expression
patterns, however, are provided as strings using a PCRE-style syntax. For
example, a pattern such as \texttt{"[a-c]+\textbackslash{}d\{2\}"} must
first be converted into the internal representation before it can be
processed by the parsers.

The frontend in \texttt{src/frontend/} provides this conversion. It first
parses the external pattern into a surface-syntax AST and then translates
that AST into the \texttt{Regex} representation used by the parsers in
Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}. Its design is based on the
\texttt{Text.Regex.PDeriv} implementation in \texttt{luzhuomi/regex-pderiv},
in particular the \texttt{ExtPattern}, \texttt{Parse}, and
\texttt{Translate} components used in Kenny Lu's implementation of the
partial-derivative approach of \citet{sulzmannlu2012}. This implementation
provides a separate reference for the treatment of external syntax.
The construction in \citet{sulzmannlu2014}, by contrast, operates directly
on the six-constructor \texttt{Regex} algebra and does not define a
PCRE-oriented frontend.

The real-world corpus described in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation} depends on this frontend to convert
Suricata, SpamAssassin, and RegexLib rule patterns into \texttt{Regex}
values. This conversion includes parsing the surface syntax, desugaring
constructs that are not represented by \texttt{Regex}, and applying the
substring-search padding scheme.

\section{Two ASTs}
\label{sec:frontend-two-asts}

The frontend separates surface syntax from the internal regular-expression
algebra by using two abstract syntax trees connected by a single translation
step:

\[
\text{pattern string}
\xrightarrow{\texttt{parse\_ext\_pattern}}
\texttt{ExtPat}
\xrightarrow{\texttt{translate}}
\texttt{Regex}
\]

\texttt{ExtPat} (\texttt{ext\_pattern.rs}) represents the external
surface syntax. It includes constructs such as marking and non-marking
groups, bounded repetitions, character-class shorthands such as
\texttt{\textbackslash{}d}, \texttt{\textbackslash{}w}, and
\texttt{\textbackslash{}s}, anchors, and the \texttt{greedy: bool} field
associated with quantifiers. These constructs remain explicit in
\texttt{ExtPat} and are not desugared during parsing.

\texttt{Regex}, introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:regex-rust-implementation}, is the internal
six-constructor representation
\texttt{Phi | Eps | Lit | Seq | Alt | Star}. It has no representation for
groups, repetition bounds, anchors, or quantifier laziness. Constructs
expressible in \texttt{ExtPat} but absent from \texttt{Regex} are therefore
desugared by \texttt{translate}
(Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}). Anchors are handled separately:
\texttt{\textbackslash{}\^{}} and \texttt{\$} are translated to
\texttt{Eps}, while their effect on substring search is handled by the
frontend entry points described in Section~\ref{sec:frontend-substring-search}.

The explicit intermediate representation also isolates parsing from
translation. The unit tests in \texttt{translate.rs}, for example, can
construct \texttt{ExtPat} values directly and verify their translated
\texttt{Regex} representations without depending on the recursive-descent
parser in \texttt{parse.rs}. This separation is analogous to the distinction
between parse-tree construction and operations such as
\texttt{flatten} and \texttt{decode} discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:parse-trees-as-evidence}: each stage has a distinct input
and output representation and can therefore be tested independently.

\section{Parsing Surface Syntax}
\label{sec:frontend-parsing}

The parser in \texttt{frontend/parse.rs} is a recursive-descent parser
operating on a \texttt{Chars} iterator. Its structure follows the grammar
used by \texttt{Text.Regex.PDeriv.Parse}. The reference functions
\texttt{p\_ere}, \texttt{p\_branch}, \texttt{p\_exp},
\texttt{p\_atom}, \texttt{p\_group}, \texttt{p\_charclass},
\texttt{p\_enum}, and \texttt{p\_bound} correspond to
\texttt{parse\_or}, \texttt{parse\_concat}, \texttt{parse\_postfixed},
\texttt{parse\_atom}, \texttt{parse\_group},
\texttt{parse\_charclass}, \texttt{parse\_class\_enum}, and
\texttt{parse\_bound}. These functions implement the respective levels of
the surface grammar.

The implementation differs from the reference parser in several respects.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Class shorthands are interpreted as character classes.}
  The reference implementation's \texttt{Translate.hs} treats a bare
  \texttt{EEscape c} as the literal character \texttt{c}. This frontend
  instead interprets \texttt{d}, \texttt{D}, \texttt{w}, \texttt{W},
  \texttt{s}, and \texttt{S} as character-class shorthands
  (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}). This interpretation corresponds
  to their use in the external patterns processed by the project.

  \item \textbf{Backreferences and lookaround are rejected.}
  Constructs such as \texttt{\textbackslash{}1}--\texttt{\textbackslash{}9}
  and lookaround assertions result in parse errors. Backreferences and
  lookaround are not represented by the regular-expression algebra used by
  the derivative constructions. Rejecting them explicitly prevents constructs
  outside the supported formal model from being silently misinterpreted. This
  is important for corpus extraction described in Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation},
  where unsupported constructs must be identified explicitly.

  \item \textbf{Named groups are accepted.}
  Both \texttt{(?<name>...)} and \texttt{(?P<name>...)} are parsed and
  subsequently translated to their unnamed group contents. Group names
  have no representation in \texttt{Regex} and therefore have no effect
  after translation. Supporting the syntax nevertheless permits patterns
  containing named groups to be processed without introducing capture-group
  semantics into the internal representation.

  \item \textbf{Hexadecimal escapes are accepted.}
  The parser additionally accepts \texttt{\textbackslash{}xHH}
  hexadecimal character escapes, which are not provided by the reference
  frontend.
\end{itemize}

The function \texttt{parse\_bound} also recognizes lazy-quantifier markers,
such as \texttt{a*?}, \texttt{a+?}, and \texttt{a\{2,4\}?}. The marker is
stored in the \texttt{greedy: bool} field of \texttt{ExtPat}, although it
does not affect the subsequent translation, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}.

The precedence of the surface syntax follows the reference grammar.
Alternation has the lowest precedence, followed by concatenation and then
postfix quantifiers, with each quantifier applying to a single atom. At the
atom level, the parser handles literals, escapes, character classes,
groups, and anchors.

\section{Translating \texttt{ExtPat} into \texttt{Regex}}
\label{sec:frontend-translate}

The function \texttt{translate} in \texttt{frontend/translate.rs} is
defined by structural recursion over \texttt{ExtPat}. Each constructor has
a corresponding match arm that either maps directly to a
\texttt{Regex} constructor or explicitly desugars the surface construct:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{array}{ll}
\hline
\texttt{ExtPat} & \texttt{Regex} \\
\hline
\texttt{Empty}, \texttt{Eps} & \texttt{Eps} \\
\texttt{Never} & \texttt{Phi} \\
\texttt{Char(c)} & \texttt{Lit(c)} \\
\texttt{Group}/\texttt{GroupNonMarking(r)}
  & \texttt{translate(r)} \\
\texttt{Or([r1, ..., rn])}
  & \text{left-folded } \texttt{Alt} \text{ chain} \\
\texttt{Concat([r1, ..., rn])}
  & \text{left-folded } \texttt{Seq} \text{ chain} \\
\texttt{Opt(r)}
  & \texttt{Alt(translate(r), Eps)} \\
\texttt{Plus(r)}
  & \texttt{Seq(translate(r), Star(translate(r)))} \\
\texttt{Star(r)}
  & \texttt{Star(translate(r))} \\
\texttt{Carat}, \texttt{Dollar}
  & \texttt{Eps} \\
\texttt{Dot}, \texttt{Any(cs)}, \texttt{NoneOf(cs)}
  & \text{sorted, deduplicated } \texttt{Alt} \text{ of } \texttt{Lit} \\
\texttt{Bound(r, lo, hi)}
  & \text{unrolled exact/optional repetition} \\
\hline
\end{array}
\end{equation*}

The translation therefore removes all surface constructs that are not
present in the core algebra while preserving the language represented by
the supported constructs.

\paragraph{Character classes are represented by alternation.}

\texttt{Any}, \texttt{NoneOf}, \texttt{Dot}, and class shorthands
are translated via \texttt{alt\_of\_chars}, which sorts and deduplicates
the character set into a left-folded \texttt{Alt} chain. The file
\texttt{alphabet.rs} provides 98 characters (printable ASCII, tab, newline,
carriage return). The functions \texttt{digit\_chars}, \texttt{word\_chars},
and \texttt{space\_chars} define sets for \texttt{\textbackslash{}d},
\texttt{\textbackslash{}w}, and \texttt{\textbackslash{}s}, with negated
forms via \texttt{complement}.

The \texttt{Regex} representation contains no dedicated character-class
constructor. Consequently, every character class is reduced to the six
constructors of the core algebra. For example, a
\texttt{\textbackslash{}d} shorthand is represented as an alternation over
its ten characters, which the derivative implementations subsequently
process through the ordinary \texttt{Alt} structure.

\paragraph{Bounds are desugared by explicit repetition.}

The function \texttt{desugar\_bound} constructs the exact lower-bound
portion using \texttt{repeat\_exact}. This function produces a left-folded
\texttt{Seq} chain and returns \texttt{Eps} when \texttt{lo == 0}. For an
unbounded upper limit, the remaining repetitions are represented by a
\texttt{Star}. Thus, \texttt{r\{2,\}} becomes two explicit copies followed
by \texttt{Star(r)}. For a finite upper bound, \texttt{hi - lo} additional
copies are represented using nested optional expressions constructed by
\texttt{optional\_extra}.

Bounded repetition can substantially increase the size of the internal
expression. A short source pattern such as \texttt{r\{1586,\}} would
otherwise produce 1586 nested \texttt{Seq} nodes before the trailing
\texttt{Star} is introduced. The frontend therefore imposes
\texttt{MAX\_BOUND = 1000}. This limit is specific to the implementation
and is not inherited from the reference parser. Bounds exceeding the
limit result in a descriptive translation error rather than the
construction of an excessively large \texttt{Regex}.

\paragraph{Greedy and lazy quantifiers are parsed but not distinguished.}

The translation of \texttt{Opt}, \texttt{Plus}, \texttt{Star}, and
\texttt{Bound} does not inspect the \texttt{greedy} flag recorded by
\texttt{parse\_bound}. The internal \texttt{Regex} representation has no
per-quantifier notion of greediness or laziness. Disambiguation is instead
determined by the parser as a whole, with the derivative-based and
partial-derivative-based constructions implementing their respective
policies, as described in Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem}.

Consequently, \texttt{a*} and \texttt{a*?} translate to identical
\texttt{Regex} values. A pattern containing both greedy and lazy
quantifiers can therefore be parsed successfully, but the distinction
between the two quantifier forms is not preserved by translation. The
frontend consequently accepts a broader surface syntax than can be
distinguished by the internal representation.

\section{Substring Search}
\label{sec:frontend-substring-search}

The parsers in Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} perform full-string matching: after
the input has been consumed, the resulting expression is tested for
nullability. They do not provide a separate operation for determining
whether a pattern occurs as a substring of a longer input. The derivative
constructions of \citet{sulzmannlu2014} and the corresponding teaching
material likewise define matching over the complete input.

The external patterns used by Suricata and SpamAssassin generally have
search semantics, where a rule is triggered when its pattern occurs within
a larger payload. The frontend therefore implements substring search before
the resulting \texttt{Regex} is passed to a parser. The translated pattern
is surrounded by \texttt{wildcard\_run()}, a \texttt{Star} over a
98-character alternation representing an arbitrary sequence of input
characters. Padding is added only on sides that are not explicitly
anchored:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{array}{ll}
\texttt{\textbackslash{}\^{}abc}
  &\to \texttt{abc} \cdot \Sigma^{*}
  \quad\text{(leading anchor: pad the end only)} \\[2pt]
\texttt{abc\$}
  &\to \Sigma^{*} \cdot \texttt{abc}
  \quad\text{(trailing anchor: pad the start only)} \\[2pt]
\texttt{abc}
  &\to \Sigma^{*} \cdot \texttt{abc} \cdot \Sigma^{*}
  \quad\text{(no anchor: pad both sides)} \\[2pt]
\texttt{\textbackslash{}\^{}abc\$}
  &\to \texttt{abc}
  \quad\text{(fully anchored: no padding)}
\end{array}
\end{equation*}

The functions \texttt{starts\_with\_carat} and
\texttt{ends\_with\_dollar}, defined in \texttt{ext\_pattern.rs}, determine
whether padding is required. They inspect the structure of
\texttt{Concat} and \texttt{Group} nodes to identify leading
\texttt{\textbackslash{}\^{}} and trailing \texttt{\$} anchors, including
anchors nested inside groups. The anchors themselves are subsequently
translated to \texttt{Eps}, since their effect has already been accounted
for when determining the padding.

This behaviour applies to the search-oriented entry points
\texttt{parse\_dataset\_pattern} and \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule}. The
function \texttt{parse\_pattern}, used for full-string examples throughout
the thesis, does not introduce padding and retains ordinary full-string
matching semantics.

\paragraph{\texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} additionally unwraps PCRE delimiters
and case-folds on the \texttt{i} flag.}

Suricata and SpamAssassin rule text is not a bare pattern: it carries its
own \texttt{/PATTERN/FLAGS} wrapper, the same convention PCRE tools use to
separate the pattern from modifiers such as case-insensitivity.
\texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} strips this wrapper with
\texttt{strip\_pcre\_delimiters} before parsing, and, if the \texttt{i}
flag is present, applies \texttt{case\_fold} to the resulting
\texttt{ExtPat} before translation. \texttt{case\_fold} rewrites every
literal character into an \texttt{Any} of both cases, so \texttt{a}
becomes \texttt{Any(['a','A'])}; the fold happens at the \texttt{ExtPat}
level rather than at match time because \texttt{Regex} itself has no
case-insensitivity concept for a parser to consult. \texttt{parse\_dataset\_pattern}
has no such flag to inspect and is always case-sensitive.
\texttt{parse\_pattern} passes any input through unchanged if it is not
\texttt{/}-delimited, so a bare pattern with no wrapper is accepted by
either search entry point exactly as written.

\paragraph{Search finds \emph{a} match, not necessarily the first one.}
\label{sec:search-first-occurrence}
Padding turns "found somewhere in this input" into an ordinary
full-match question over a larger expression, and that substitution is
not free: it inherits whichever iteration count a $\mathtt{Star}$
prefers under the policy in force, and both policies this thesis
implements prefer \emph{more} iterations of the leading $\Sigma^{*}$
when the rest of the input can still be consumed, exactly as either
would prefer more iterations of any other $\mathtt{Star}$.

At every character, the leading $\Sigma^{*}$ faces the same fork any
nullable first factor of a $\mathtt{Seq}$ produces: keep deriving into
the $\mathtt{Star}$ itself (treat this character as filler), or also
derive into what follows starting here (try to begin the core pattern
at this position). Both survive as one $\mathtt{Alt}$; whichever
survives to the end is read off by the disambiguation policy in force.
The following uses a reduced three-letter alphabet $\{a,b,c\}$ in
place of the real 98-character $\Sigma$, purely so the resulting parse
tree stays short enough to display; the mechanism is identical either
way. With $r = \Sigma^{*}\,ab\,\Sigma^{*}$ against \texttt{"cabc"},
which contains \texttt{ab} once, at position~1:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:search-single}
v_1 = \big(\big((\mathit{Star}[\mathit{Right}(c)],\,a),\,b\big),\,\mathit{Star}[\mathit{Right}(c)]\big)
\end{equation}

the leading $\Sigma^{*}$ takes one iteration ($c$), the literal $ab$
matches, and the trailing $\Sigma^{*}$ takes one iteration ($c$) —
exactly where \texttt{ab} sits. Against \texttt{"abcab"}, which
contains \texttt{ab} \emph{twice}, at position~0 and position~3:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:search-multi}
v_2 = \big(\big((\mathit{Star}[\mathit{Left}(\mathit{Left}(a)),\,\mathit{Left}(\mathit{Right}(b)),\,\mathit{Right}(c)],\,a),\,b\big),\,\mathit{Star}[\,]\big)
\end{equation}

the leading $\Sigma^{*}$ instead runs \emph{three} iterations
($a$, $b$, $c$) before yielding to the literal $ab$, and the trailing
$\Sigma^{*}$ is empty: at position~0, consuming $a$ as filler and
finding $ab$ later is a complete, valid parse, so the policy takes it,
and \eqref{eq:search-multi} reports the \emph{second} occurrence, not
the first.
