\chapter{External To Internal Regex}
\label{sec:frontend}

The examples presented thus far have been expressed directly in terms of
\texttt{Regex}, the six-constructor core algebra introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:regex-rust-implementation}. This is the representation
consumed by \texttt{deriv} and \texttt{pderiv}. External regular-expression
patterns, however, are provided as strings using a PCRE-style syntax. For
example, a pattern such as \texttt{"[a-c]+\textbackslash{}d\{2\}"} must
first be converted into the internal representation before it can be
processed by the parsers.

The frontend in \texttt{src/frontend/} provides this conversion. It first
parses the external pattern into a surface-syntax AST and then translates
that AST into the \texttt{Regex} representation used by the parsers in
Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}. Its design is based on the
\texttt{Text.Regex.PDeriv} implementation in \texttt{luzhuomi/regex-pderiv},
in particular the \texttt{ExtPattern}, \texttt{Parse}, and
\texttt{Translate} components used in Kenny Lu's implementation of the
partial-derivative approach of \citet{sulzmannlu2012}. This implementation
provides a separate reference for the treatment of external syntax.
The construction in \citet{sulzmannlu2014}, by contrast, operates directly
on the six-constructor \texttt{Regex} algebra and does not define a
PCRE-oriented frontend.

The real-world corpus described in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation} depends on this frontend to convert
Suricata, SpamAssassin, and RegexLib rule patterns into \texttt{Regex}
values. This conversion includes parsing the surface syntax and
desugaring constructs that are not represented by \texttt{Regex}; a
construct the algebra cannot express at all is rejected rather than
desugared.

\paragraph{This frontend accepts a strict subset of PCRE syntax.}
Three categories of construct are rejected outright: backreferences
(\texttt{\textbackslash{}1}--\texttt{\textbackslash{}9}), lookaround
assertions (\texttt{(?=...)}, \texttt{(?!...)}, \texttt{(?<=...)},
\texttt{(?<!...)}), and anchors, which this frontend takes to include
both the string anchors \texttt{\^{}}/\texttt{\$} and the word-boundary
assertions \texttt{\textbackslash{}b}/\texttt{\textbackslash{}B}. None of
the three denote a regular language over \texttt{Regex}'s six
constructors: a backreference requires matching against a previously
captured substring, which no alphabet-indexed derivative can express;
and lookaround and word boundaries test the input around the current
position without consuming it, a property of a match \emph{position}
rather than of a language. String anchors are the same kind of problem
one level up: they constrain \emph{where} in the input a pattern may
match, and Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchors} discusses why this
frontend has nothing left to attach that constraint to once it performs
no search at all. Rejecting all three explicitly, rather than silently
ignoring or mistranslating them, is what lets corpus extraction
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation}) identify unsupported patterns
instead of miscounting them as supported.

\section{Two ASTs}
\label{sec:frontend-two-asts}

The frontend separates surface syntax from the internal regular-expression
algebra by using two abstract syntax trees connected by a single translation
step:

\[
\text{pattern string}
\xrightarrow{\texttt{parse\_ext\_pattern}}
\texttt{ExtPat}
\xrightarrow{\texttt{translate}}
\texttt{Regex}
\]

\texttt{ExtPat} (\texttt{ext\_pattern.rs}) represents the external
surface syntax. It includes constructs such as marking and non-marking
groups, bounded repetitions, character-class shorthands such as
\texttt{\textbackslash{}d}, \texttt{\textbackslash{}w}, and
\texttt{\textbackslash{}s}, anchors, and the \texttt{greedy: bool} field
associated with quantifiers. These constructs remain explicit in
\texttt{ExtPat} and are not desugared during parsing.

\texttt{Regex}, introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:regex-rust-implementation}, is the internal
six-constructor representation
\texttt{Phi | Eps | Lit | Seq | Alt | Star}. It has no representation for
groups, repetition bounds, anchors, or quantifier laziness. Constructs
expressible in \texttt{ExtPat} but absent from \texttt{Regex} are
desugared by \texttt{translate} (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate})
whenever a \texttt{Regex} equivalent exists. Anchors have none: unlike
groups or bounds, \texttt{\^{}} and \texttt{\$} are not desugared into an
equivalent combination of the six constructors, they are rejected by
\texttt{translate}, the same treatment the word-boundary assertions
\texttt{\textbackslash{}b}/\texttt{\textbackslash{}B} already receive.
Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchors} returns to this once the rest of
translation has been presented.

The explicit intermediate representation also isolates parsing from
translation. The unit tests in \texttt{translate.rs}, for example, can
construct \texttt{ExtPat} values directly and verify their translated
\texttt{Regex} representations without depending on the recursive-descent
parser in \texttt{parse.rs}. This separation is analogous to the distinction
between parse-tree construction and operations such as
\texttt{flatten} and \texttt{decode} discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:parse-trees-as-evidence}: each stage has a distinct input
and output representation and can therefore be tested independently.

\section{Parsing Surface Syntax}
\label{sec:frontend-parsing}

The parser in \texttt{frontend/parse.rs} is a recursive-descent parser
operating on a \texttt{Chars} iterator. Its structure follows the grammar
used by \texttt{Text.Regex.PDeriv.Parse}. The reference functions
\texttt{p\_ere}, \texttt{p\_branch}, \texttt{p\_exp},
\texttt{p\_atom}, \texttt{p\_group}, \texttt{p\_charclass},
\texttt{p\_enum}, and \texttt{p\_bound} correspond to
\texttt{parse\_or}, \texttt{parse\_concat}, \texttt{parse\_postfixed},
\texttt{parse\_atom}, \texttt{parse\_group},
\texttt{parse\_charclass}, \texttt{parse\_class\_enum}, and
\texttt{parse\_bound}. These functions implement the respective levels of
the surface grammar.

The implementation differs from the reference parser in several respects.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Class shorthands are interpreted as character classes.}
  The reference implementation's \texttt{Translate.hs} treats a bare
  \texttt{EEscape c} as the literal character \texttt{c}. This frontend
  instead interprets \texttt{d}, \texttt{D}, \texttt{w}, \texttt{W},
  \texttt{s}, and \texttt{S} as character-class shorthands
  (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}). This interpretation corresponds
  to their use in the external patterns processed by the project.

  \item \textbf{Backreferences, lookaround, and anchors are all
  rejected, at two different stages.} Constructs such as
  \texttt{\textbackslash{}1}--\texttt{\textbackslash{}9} and lookaround
  assertions have no \texttt{ExtPat} constructor to parse into at all,
  so \texttt{parse\_ext\_pattern} itself returns a parse error the
  moment it encounters one. Anchors are different: \texttt{\^{}},
  \texttt{\$}, and the word-boundary escapes
  \texttt{\textbackslash{}b}/\texttt{\textbackslash{}B} each parse
  successfully, into \texttt{ExtPat::Carat}, \texttt{ExtPat::Dollar},
  and \texttt{ExtPat::WordBoundary}; it is \texttt{translate}
  (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}) that rejects them, one stage
  later. Both stages exist for the same reason: none of the five
  constructs are represented by the regular-expression algebra used by
  the derivative constructions, and rejecting them explicitly, rather
  than silently ignoring or mistranslating them, is what corpus
  extraction (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation}) relies on to
  identify unsupported patterns instead of miscounting them as
  supported.

  \item \textbf{Named groups are accepted.}
  Both \texttt{(?<name>...)} and \texttt{(?P<name>...)} are parsed and
  subsequently translated to their unnamed group contents. Group names
  have no representation in \texttt{Regex} and therefore have no effect
  after translation. Supporting the syntax nevertheless permits patterns
  containing named groups to be processed without introducing capture-group
  semantics into the internal representation.

  \item \textbf{Hexadecimal escapes are accepted.}
  The parser additionally accepts \texttt{\textbackslash{}xHH}
  hexadecimal character escapes, which are not provided by the reference
  frontend.
\end{itemize}

The function \texttt{parse\_bound} also recognizes lazy-quantifier markers,
such as \texttt{a*?}, \texttt{a+?}, and \texttt{a\{2,4\}?}. The marker is
stored in the \texttt{greedy: bool} field of \texttt{ExtPat}, although it
does not affect the subsequent translation, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}.

The precedence of the surface syntax follows the reference grammar.
Alternation has the lowest precedence, followed by concatenation and then
postfix quantifiers, with each quantifier applying to a single atom. At the
atom level, the parser handles literals, escapes, character classes,
groups, and anchors.

\section{Translating \texttt{ExtPat} into \texttt{Regex}}
\label{sec:frontend-translate}

The function \texttt{translate} in \texttt{frontend/translate.rs} is
defined by structural recursion over \texttt{ExtPat}. Each constructor has
a corresponding match arm that either maps directly to a
\texttt{Regex} constructor or explicitly desugars the surface construct:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{array}{ll}
\hline
\texttt{ExtPat} & \texttt{Regex} \\
\hline
\texttt{Empty}, \texttt{Eps} & \texttt{Eps} \\
\texttt{Never} & \texttt{Phi} \\
\texttt{Char(c)} & \texttt{Lit(c)} \\
\texttt{Group}/\texttt{GroupNonMarking(r)}
  & \texttt{translate(r)} \\
\texttt{Or([r1, ..., rn])}
  & \text{left-folded } \texttt{Alt} \text{ chain} \\
\texttt{Concat([r1, ..., rn])}
  & \text{left-folded } \texttt{Seq} \text{ chain} \\
\texttt{Opt(r)}
  & \texttt{Alt(translate(r), Eps)} \\
\texttt{Plus(r)}
  & \texttt{Seq(translate(r), Star(translate(r)))} \\
\texttt{Star(r)}
  & \texttt{Star(translate(r))} \\
\texttt{Dot}, \texttt{Any(cs)}, \texttt{NoneOf(cs)}
  & \text{sorted, deduplicated } \texttt{Alt} \text{ of } \texttt{Lit} \\
\texttt{Bound(r, lo, hi)}
  & \text{unrolled exact/optional repetition} \\
\texttt{Carat}, \texttt{Dollar}
  & \text{rejected} \\
\texttt{WordBoundary(b)}
  & \text{rejected} \\
\hline
\end{array}
\end{equation*}

The translation therefore removes all surface constructs that are not
present in the core algebra while preserving the language represented by
the supported constructs, with one exception: the last two rows above
have no \texttt{Regex} to remove them \emph{into}, so \texttt{translate}
reports an error instead of returning one at all.
Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchors} discusses the consequences once the
rest of translation has been presented.

\paragraph{Character classes are represented by alternation.}

\texttt{Any}, \texttt{NoneOf}, \texttt{Dot}, and class shorthands
are translated via \texttt{alt\_of\_chars}, which sorts and deduplicates
the character set into a left-folded \texttt{Alt} chain. The file
\texttt{alphabet.rs} provides 98 characters (printable ASCII, tab, newline,
carriage return). The functions \texttt{digit\_chars}, \texttt{word\_chars},
and \texttt{space\_chars} define sets for \texttt{\textbackslash{}d},
\texttt{\textbackslash{}w}, and \texttt{\textbackslash{}s}, with negated
forms via \texttt{complement}.

The \texttt{Regex} representation contains no dedicated character-class
constructor. Consequently, every character class is reduced to the six
constructors of the core algebra. For example, a
\texttt{\textbackslash{}d} shorthand is represented as an alternation over
its ten characters, which the derivative implementations subsequently
process through the ordinary \texttt{Alt} structure.

\paragraph{Bounds are desugared by explicit repetition.}

The function \texttt{desugar\_bound} constructs the exact lower-bound
portion using \texttt{repeat\_exact}. This function produces a left-folded
\texttt{Seq} chain and returns \texttt{Eps} when \texttt{lo == 0}. For an
unbounded upper limit, the remaining repetitions are represented by a
\texttt{Star}. Thus, \texttt{r\{2,\}} becomes two explicit copies followed
by \texttt{Star(r)}. For a finite upper bound, \texttt{hi - lo} additional
copies are represented using nested optional expressions constructed by
\texttt{optional\_extra}.

Bounded repetition can substantially increase the size of the internal
expression. A short source pattern such as \texttt{r\{1586,\}} would
otherwise produce 1586 nested \texttt{Seq} nodes before the trailing
\texttt{Star} is introduced. The frontend therefore imposes
\texttt{MAX\_BOUND = 1000}. This limit is specific to the implementation
and is not inherited from the reference parser. Bounds exceeding the
limit result in a descriptive translation error rather than the
construction of an excessively large \texttt{Regex}.

The check is on the repetition count alone, not on the size of the
expression it produces, and the two differ sharply depending on what is
being repeated. \texttt{a\{1586,\}} desugars to 1586 copies of a single
\texttt{Lit} node: about 1586 nodes in total. \texttt{.\{1586,\}} desugars
to 1586 copies of \texttt{Dot}, and \texttt{Dot} alone already lowers to
195 nodes (an \texttt{Alt} chain over the 98-character alphabet), so the
result is on the order of 309{,}000 nodes before the trailing \texttt{Star}
is even added. \texttt{desugar\_bound} inspects only \texttt{lo} and
\texttt{hi}; it treats both patterns identically, since 1586 exceeds
\texttt{MAX\_BOUND} regardless of what is repeated. A separate, coarser
safeguard in the dataset-preparation pipeline
(\texttt{src/data/prepare.rs}'s \texttt{MAX\_CORE\_NODES = 20{,}000}, applied
to the pattern as a whole after translation) would catch
\texttt{.\{1586,\}}'s expansion on size alone, independently of
\texttt{MAX\_BOUND}. No equivalent check exists outside that pipeline,
however: a bare \texttt{parse} or \texttt{match} invocation has no
backstop beyond \texttt{MAX\_BOUND} itself, so a bound that passes the
cap but wraps an expensive sub-expression can still produce a
disproportionately large \texttt{Regex}.

\paragraph{Greedy and lazy quantifiers are parsed but not distinguished.}

The translation of \texttt{Opt}, \texttt{Plus}, \texttt{Star}, and
\texttt{Bound} does not inspect the \texttt{greedy} flag recorded by
\texttt{parse\_bound}. The internal \texttt{Regex} representation has no
per-quantifier notion of greediness or laziness. Disambiguation is instead
determined by the parser as a whole, with the derivative-based and
partial-derivative-based constructions implementing their respective
policies, as described in Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem}.

Consequently, \texttt{a*} and \texttt{a*?} translate to identical
\texttt{Regex} values. A pattern containing both greedy and lazy
quantifiers can therefore be parsed successfully, but the distinction
between the two quantifier forms is not preserved by translation. The
frontend consequently accepts a broader surface syntax than can be
distinguished by the internal representation.

\section{Anchors and the Two Entry Points}
\label{sec:frontend-anchors}

The parsers in Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} perform full-string matching: after
the input has been consumed, the resulting expression is tested for
nullability. They do not provide a separate operation for determining
whether a pattern occurs as a substring of a longer input. The derivative
constructions of \citet{sulzmannlu2014} and the corresponding teaching
material likewise define matching over the complete input, and this
frontend makes no attempt to build a search operation on top of them:
every pattern is matched against the whole of its input, nothing less
and nothing more. The two entry points below, \texttt{parse\_pattern}
and \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule}, both end by calling \texttt{translate}
and handing its result straight to a parser; neither pads, searches, or
otherwise changes what the translated pattern is matched against.
Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation} discusses what this costs against
a real-world corpus whose rules generally do expect search semantics;
this section is only concerned with what the frontend itself does,
which is to reject any construct whose behaviour depends on
\emph{where} in the input it appears, since \texttt{Regex} has no
notion of where at all.

\paragraph{\texttt{\^{}}, \texttt{\$}, and \texttt{\textbackslash{}b}/\texttt{\textbackslash{}B} are rejected at the same site, for the same reason.}
Figure~\ref{fig:anchor-rejection-rust} is the two match arms of
\texttt{translate} (\texttt{frontend/translate.rs}) responsible, already
marked as rejected in the table of Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}.
Both produce a descriptive \texttt{Err} rather than silently
approximating the construct with something else, such as the
\texttt{Eps} this frontend used to lower anchors to.

\begin{figure}[H]
\small
\begin{verbatim}
ExtPat::Carat | ExtPat::Dollar => {
    Err("anchor '^'/'$' is not supported by this frontend -- unsupported".to_string())
}
// ...
ExtPat::WordBoundary(_) => {
    Err("word boundary '\b'/'\B' is position-dependent, not a regular-language \
         construct -- unsupported".to_string())
}
\end{verbatim}
\caption{The two rejecting arms of \texttt{translate}. Unlike
backreferences and lookaround (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-parsing}), which
have no \texttt{ExtPat} constructor and are rejected while parsing, these
four constructs parse successfully into \texttt{ExtPat::Carat},
\texttt{ExtPat::Dollar}, and \texttt{ExtPat::WordBoundary} and are
rejected one stage later, during translation.}
\label{fig:anchor-rejection-rust}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Rejection is total, not local.}
\texttt{translate} is defined by structural recursion
(Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}), and every recursive call site
either passes its result through Rust's \texttt{?} operator or, for
\texttt{Group}/\texttt{GroupNonMarking}, returns it directly as a tail
call. Figure~\ref{fig:anchor-propagation-rust} shows three representative
arms: \texttt{Group} forwards whatever its inner call returns, unchanged;
\texttt{Or} accumulates over a list, applying \texttt{?} to each
element's translation in turn; \texttt{Star} applies \texttt{?} inline to
its one recursive call. \texttt{Concat}, \texttt{Plus}, and
\texttt{Bound}'s \texttt{desugar\_bound} all propagate the same way,
through a loop-accumulated or inline \texttt{?}. An \texttt{Err} returned
by any recursive call is therefore returned immediately by its caller,
without constructing a partial \texttt{Regex} around it, and that
caller's own caller does the same, all the way back to \texttt{translate}'s
original entry point.

\begin{figure}[H]
\small
\begin{verbatim}
ExtPat::Group(inner) | ExtPat::GroupNonMarking(inner) => translate(inner),
// ...
ExtPat::Or(alts) => {
    let mut iter = alts.iter();
    let mut acc = translate(iter.next().ok_or("empty alternation")?)?;
    for a in iter {
        acc = Regex::alt(acc, translate(a)?);
    }
    Ok(acc)
}
// ...
ExtPat::Star(inner) => Ok(Regex::star(translate(inner)?)),
\end{verbatim}
\caption{Three of \texttt{translate}'s recursive arms. Every other
compound arm (\texttt{Concat}, \texttt{Opt}, \texttt{Plus},
\texttt{Bound}) follows the same \texttt{?}-propagation shape.}
\label{fig:anchor-propagation-rust}
\end{figure}

The practical consequence is that an anchor anywhere in a pattern
rejects the pattern as a whole, regardless of how deeply it is nested or
whether it sits behind an optional quantifier that would, if taken,
not require it at all. Figure~\ref{fig:anchor-rejection-cli} confirms
this against the built CLI for five increasingly indirect placements of
\texttt{\^{}}: directly optional, optional behind a group, inside one
branch of an alternation, under a star, and under an explicit bound.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\small
\centering
\begin{verbatim}
$ cargo run -- parse "^?abc" "abc"
Regex parse error: anchor '^'/'$' is not supported by this frontend -- unsupported

$ cargo run -- parse "(^)?abc" "abc"
Regex parse error: anchor '^'/'$' is not supported by this frontend -- unsupported

$ cargo run -- parse "(^a|b)c" "bc"
Regex parse error: anchor '^'/'$' is not supported by this frontend -- unsupported

$ cargo run -- parse "(^a)*b" "b"
Regex parse error: anchor '^'/'$' is not supported by this frontend -- unsupported

$ cargo run -- parse "(^a){0,1}b" "b"
Regex parse error: anchor '^'/'$' is not supported by this frontend -- unsupported
\end{verbatim}
\caption{Every placement is rejected identically, including the three
where the anchor is reachable only through an optional, alternative, or
bounded branch that need not be taken at all. \texttt{translate} never
gets the chance to discover that: structural recursion visits the anchor
node regardless of what quantifier or alternation surrounds it, and
Figure~\ref{fig:anchor-propagation-rust}'s \texttt{?} chain reports it
immediately.}
\label{fig:anchor-rejection-cli}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{\texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} additionally unwraps PCRE delimiters,
case-folds on \texttt{i}, and rejects two more flags outright.}

Suricata and SpamAssassin rule text is not a bare pattern: it carries its
own \texttt{/PATTERN/FLAGS} wrapper, the same convention PCRE tools use to
separate the pattern from modifiers such as case-insensitivity.
\texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} strips this wrapper with
\texttt{strip\_pcre\_delimiters} before parsing, and, if the \texttt{i}
flag is present, applies \texttt{case\_fold} to the resulting
\texttt{ExtPat} before translation. \texttt{case\_fold} rewrites every
literal character into an \texttt{Any} of both cases, so \texttt{a}
becomes \texttt{Any(['a','A'])}; the fold happens at the \texttt{ExtPat}
level rather than at match time because \texttt{Regex} itself has no
case-insensitivity concept for a parser to consult. The \texttt{m}
(multiline) and \texttt{x} (extended) flags are rejected outright by
\texttt{PcreFlags::unsupported}, for the same underlying reason as
anchors: \texttt{m} only changes where \texttt{\^{}}/\texttt{\$} match,
which no longer exists to be changed, and \texttt{x} makes pattern
whitespace insignificant, which this parser does not model. \texttt{R}
(relative), \texttt{A} (anchored), and \texttt{E} (dollar-endonly) are
treated differently again: each used to position or tune a search or an
anchor this frontend no longer has, so each is now simply ignored, the
same way an unrecognized buffer-selector flag such as Snort's \texttt{U}
or \texttt{H} always was. \texttt{parse\_pattern} passes any input
through unchanged if it is not \texttt{/}-delimited, so a bare pattern
with no wrapper is accepted by either entry point exactly as written.
