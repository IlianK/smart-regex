\chapter{Translation: ExtPat to Regex}
\label{sec:frontend}

The four parsers of Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} operate on the six-constructor
\texttt{Regex} of Chapter~\ref{sec:regular-expressions}. A real
pattern uses a richer surface language. A pattern author writes
shorthand character classes, bounded repetition, alternation over
ranges, and sometimes a PCRE flag suffix, none of which appear in the
core language. The frontend reads such a pattern and produces the
\texttt{Regex} that the parsers consume.



\textbf{Rejection on constructs that cannot lower.} When a construct
cannot be translated without changing what the pattern denotes, 
it is rejected with an error.

\textbf{Full-string matching.} A translated pattern matches the whole
input or nothing. The frontend has no search padding, no substring
search, and no anchor handling.

\textbf{A 98-character working alphabet.} Shorthand classes and
negated classes enumerate a fixed set of 98 characters, printable
ASCII (95) plus tab, newline, and carriage return (3). A literal
character in the pattern is not translated through that set. It
becomes a \texttt{Regex::Lit} that carries the full Unicode
\texttt{char}. A literal \texttt{é} therefore matches \texttt{é} even
though \texttt{é} is not one of the 98, while \texttt{\textbackslash W}
enumerates only those 98 minus the word characters and cannot match
\texttt{é}. The contrast is visible in \texttt{café}. Every letter is
a literal, so the pattern matches the string \texttt{"café"} as-is,
including the \texttt{é}.

Two functions form the public interface. \texttt{parse\_pattern}
reads a bare pattern and the \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} reads a
\texttt{/PATTERN/FLAGS} wrapper and applies the flag suffix. Both
return either a translated \texttt{Regex} or a rejection reason.

Translation proceeds in two passes. A recursive-descent parser reads
the pattern string into an intermediate type, \texttt{ExtPat}. A
second pass, \texttt{translate}, lowers \texttt{ExtPat} to
\texttt{Regex}. PCRE flags are applied as structural rewriting between
the two passes.

\section{The External AST}
\label{sec:frontend-extpat}

The parser produces an intermediate type, \texttt{ExtPat}, that sits
between the surface string and the core \texttt{Regex}. It carries
precedence, grouping, quantifiers, and character classes as units,
without committing to a particular lowering. The 19 variants fall
into four groups.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\hline
Group & Variant & Surface syntax \\
\hline
Structural       & \texttt{Empty}           & empty branch of \texttt{a|} or \texttt{(|b)} \\
                 & \texttt{Concat}          & \texttt{abc} \\
                 & \texttt{Or}              & \texttt{a|b|c} \\
                 & \texttt{Group}           & \texttt{( ... )} \\
                 & \texttt{GroupNonMarking} & \texttt{(?: ... )} \\
\hline
Quantifier       & \texttt{Opt}             & \texttt{r?} \\
                 & \texttt{Plus}            & \texttt{r+} \\
                 & \texttt{Star}            & \texttt{r*} \\
                 & \texttt{Bound}           & \texttt{r\{m,n\}} \\
\hline
Class            & \texttt{Any}             & \texttt{[abc]} \\
                 & \texttt{NoneOf}          & \texttt{[\textasciicircum abc]} \\
                 & \texttt{Escape}          & \texttt{\textbackslash d}, \texttt{\textbackslash w}, \texttt{\textbackslash s}, ... \\
                 & \texttt{Char}            & a single literal \\
\hline
Atomic / special & \texttt{Carat}           & \texttt{\textasciicircum} \\
                 & \texttt{Dollar}          & \texttt{\textdollar} \\
                 & \texttt{Dot}             & \texttt{.} \\
                 & \texttt{WordBoundary}    & \texttt{\textbackslash b}, \texttt{\textbackslash B} \\
                 & \texttt{Eps}             & $\varepsilon$ \\
                 & \texttt{Never}           & $\emptyset$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{The 19 \texttt{ExtPat} variants, grouped by role.}
\label{tab:extpat}
\end{table}

The quantifier variants are single-level. \texttt{r*} carries its atom
but not a chain, since PCRE treats chained quantifiers as syntax
errors and the parser produces none. The anchor and word-boundary
variants are first-class. The parser produces
\texttt{ExtPat::Carat} and \texttt{ExtPat::WordBoundary} and leaves
their rejection to \texttt{translate}, so the rejection message can
name the construct.

\section{Parsing: String to ExtPat}
\label{sec:frontend-parse}

Parsing is a single recursive-descent pass with one function per
grammar level. Precedence is fixed by the call chain, from loosest to
tightest.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item alternation (\texttt{|}), left-associated;
  \item concatenation (implicit), left-associated;
  \item postfix quantifiers (\texttt{?}, \texttt{+}, \texttt{*},
        \texttt{\{m,n\}}), at most one per atom.
\end{enumerate}

So \texttt{abc*} parses as \texttt{ab(c*)} and \texttt{a|b|c} as
\texttt{(a|b)|c}. The association is observable in the resulting
\texttt{Regex} tree, and the growth measurements of
Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} depend on it.

A trailing \texttt{?} after a quantifier (\texttt{r*?},
\texttt{r+?}, \texttt{r??}, \texttt{r\{m,n\}?}) is accepted and
discarded. Laziness governs the order in which a backtracking engine
explores matches. The frontend has no backtracking engine, no capture
structure, and no operation that returns a substring. So \texttt{r*}
and \texttt{r*?} produce the same \texttt{ExtPat::Star}.

Two parts of the table deserve a note.

\textbf{Character classes with ranges.} A leading or trailing
\texttt{-} is a literal member, as is a leading \texttt{]}.
Shorthand escapes inside a class expand inline as separate members and
cannot be range endpoints, so \texttt{[a-\textbackslash d]} is
rejected.

\textbf{Octal escapes inside a class.} Inside a class,
\texttt{\textbackslash} followed by an octal digit reads up to three
octal digits. A pattern such as
\verb|[\042\223\224\262\263\271]| denotes a class of codepage
punctuation bytes. Reading \verb|\042| as the three literal characters
\texttt{0}, \texttt{4}, \texttt{2} would denote a different language.

\textbf{Named groups.} \texttt{(?<name>...)} and
\texttt{(?P<name>...)} are accepted with the name dropped. The name is
not recoverable from the resulting \texttt{Regex}, since
\texttt{Regex} carries no capture structure.

\subsection{One worked example}
\label{sec:frontend-worked-example}

Take the pattern \verb|[a-c]+\d{2}| (no flags). Parsing produces the
\texttt{ExtPat} tree in Figure~\ref{fig:frontend-worked-extpat}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\begin{verbatim}
Concat [
    Plus(Any(['a', 'b', 'c'])),
    Concat [
        Escape('d'),
        Escape('d'),
    ],
]
\end{verbatim}
\caption{The \texttt{ExtPat} produced from
\texttt{[a-c]+{\textbackslash}d\{2\}}.}
\label{fig:frontend-worked-extpat}
\end{figure}

Translation lowers each piece by the mechanism its row in
Table~\ref{tab:accepted-constructs} names.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{Any(['a','b','c'])} alphabet-expands to a 3-leaf
        \texttt{Alt} chain.
  \item \texttt{Plus} rewrites structurally to
        \texttt{Seq(r, Star(r))}.
  \item \texttt{Escape('d')} alphabet-expands to a 10-leaf
        \texttt{Alt} chain (one per digit), applied twice.
  \item The two \texttt{Concat} layers fold left into one
        \texttt{Seq} chain.
\end{itemize}

The resulting \texttt{Regex} is 54 nodes, the third entry in
Table~\ref{tab:translation-sizes}. The four remaining digit leaves
contribute 40 of those nodes; the rest is \texttt{Plus}'s
\texttt{Seq}-\texttt{Star} wrapper (3 nodes) and the surrounding
\texttt{Seq} structure (11 nodes).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\begin{verbatim}

    Alt(a, Alt(b, c)),                            -- [a-c], 5 nodes
    Star(Alt(a, Alt(b, c)))),                     -- Plus desugar
  Seq(                                            -- \d{2}
    Seq(                                          -- first copy
      Eps,                                        -- unroll seed
      Alt(0, Alt(1, Alt(2, Alt(3, Alt(4,
          Alt(5, Alt(6, Alt(7, Alt(8, 9))))))))), -- \d, 19 nodes
    Alt(0, Alt(1, Alt(2, Alt(3, Alt(4,
        Alt(5, Alt(6, Alt(7, Alt(8, 9))))))))))   -- \d, 19 nodes
\end{verbatim}

\label{fig:frontend-worked-regex}
\end{figure}

\section{PCRE Flags}
\label{sec:frontend-flags}

A pattern passed to \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} may be wrapped in a
\texttt{/PATTERN/FLAGS} form. The flag suffix is read and sorted into
three groups.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\hline
Flag & Group & Effect \\
\hline
\texttt{i} & honoured & case-fold every literal letter \\
\texttt{s} & honoured & \texttt{.} includes newline \\
\texttt{m} & rejected & would change anchor meaning this frontend lacks \\
\texttt{x} & rejected & would change whitespace meaning this parser lacks \\
\texttt{R} & ignored  & names a search this frontend does not perform \\
\texttt{A} & ignored  & names an anchor this frontend does not perform \\
\texttt{E} & ignored  & tunes a \texttt{\textdollar} this frontend rejects \\
\texttt{U}, \texttt{H}, \texttt{P}, \texttt{C}, ... & ignored & names a buffer or normalisation strategy \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{The three flag groups. An ignored flag leaves the parsed
pattern exactly as it would be without the flag.}
\label{tab:flags}
\end{table}

A flag that changes what the pattern denotes is honoured. A buffer
selector such as \texttt{U} names which region of the input to test
against, not what the pattern itself matches. Under full-string
matching there is only one region. A relative-position flag
\texttt{R} names how to position a subsequent search, which does not
exist in this frontend. Both leave the language unchanged. An honoured
flag such as \texttt{i} changes the language directly and is applied.

Honoured flags run between parse and translate as structural rewrites
over \texttt{ExtPat}. \texttt{case\_fold} turns every literal letter
into a two-element \texttt{Any}. \texttt{strip\_dot\_newline} turns
every \texttt{Dot} into \texttt{NoneOf(\textbackslash n)}.

\section{Translation: ExtPat to Regex}
\label{sec:frontend-translate}

\texttt{translate} is a structural pass over \texttt{ExtPat}. Every
accepted form lowers by one of the three mechanisms introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:frontend-parse}'s accepted-syntax table. Three parts
need more care than a single row can convey.

%
%
%

\subsection{Character classes and the working alphabet}
\label{sec:frontend-alphabet}

An \texttt{ExtPat::Any(cs)} lowers to an alternation over
\texttt{cs}, one \texttt{Alt} node per element folded left.
\texttt{NoneOf(cs)} first takes the complement of \texttt{cs} against
the working alphabet and then does the same. \texttt{Dot} lowers to
\texttt{Any} over the full alphabet when the \texttt{s} flag is set,
and has already been rewritten to \texttt{NoneOf(\textbackslash n)}
otherwise. Shorthand escapes (\texttt{\textbackslash d},
\texttt{\textbackslash w}, \texttt{\textbackslash s} and their
capitalised forms) lower through the same path.

\texttt{Dot}, the shorthand classes, and any negated class therefore
cannot match a character outside the working alphabet. A pattern with
a non-ASCII literal is unaffected, since a direct literal lowers to a
single \texttt{Regex::Lit} that carries the full \texttt{char}. The
alphabet constrains only the constructs that need to enumerate a set.

%
%
%

\subsection{Bounds and unrolling}
\label{sec:frontend-bounds}

\texttt{ExtPat::Bound} is the only quantifier without a direct core
construct, since \texttt{Regex} has no \texttt{\{m,n\}} form. It
lowers by unrolling. \texttt{r\{m,n\}} becomes \texttt{m} exact copies
of the translated body, followed by \texttt{n-m} optional copies.
\texttt{r\{m,\}} becomes \texttt{m} exact copies followed by a
\texttt{Star}. Both forms clone the body, so the resulting
\texttt{Regex} has size proportional to the bound.

Two caps guard this unrolling. A bound whose upper or lower endpoint
exceeds 1000 is rejected rather than unrolled. \verb|a{1001}| rejects
with a message naming the cap, before any translation runs. A second
cap is applied by \texttt{core\_regex\_tagged}, the function
Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}'s dataset preparation calls
(\texttt{src/data/prepare.rs}). That cap rejects a pattern if the
translated \texttt{Regex} would exceed 20{,}000 nodes or an estimated
recursion depth of 2000. \texttt{translate} itself has no node-count
limit. The 20{,}000-node cap is that pipeline's own decision.

%
%
%

\subsection{Anchor recovery}
\label{sec:frontend-anchor-recovery}

Translation is where anchors are rejected, with one exception. After
unwrapping any top-level \texttt{(...)} or \texttt{(?:...)} shell, if
the pattern is a \texttt{Concat} whose first element is
\texttt{Carat} and last element is \texttt{Dollar}, those two anchors
are stripped before translation. Under full-string matching they are
provably redundant. Consuming the whole input already requires both
endpoints, so removing them changes nothing about what the pattern
denotes. Both \texttt{\textasciicircum abc\textdollar} and
\texttt{(\textasciicircum abc\textdollar)} strip to the same
\texttt{ExtPat}, and both are accepted. \texttt{\textasciicircum
\textdollar} alone strips to \texttt{Eps}.

Every other anchor placement reaches \texttt{translate} and is
rejected there. Four shapes appear.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Start-only, \texttt{\textasciicircum abc}. The opening
        anchor is present but nothing closes the wrap.
  \item End-only, \texttt{abc\textdollar}. The closing anchor is
        present but nothing opens the wrap.
  \item Nested, \texttt{(a\textasciicircum |b)c} or
        \texttt{(a\textasciicircum b)*c}. An anchor inside a group,
        alternation branch, or quantifier. After shell unwrapping the
        top level is not a \texttt{Concat} whose first element is
        \texttt{Carat} and last element is \texttt{Dollar}, so
        nothing is stripped.
  \item Multiple pairs, \texttt{\textasciicircum a\textdollar
        b\textdollar}. The outermost pair strips, but the inner
        \texttt{Dollar} remains in the stripped expression and
        \texttt{translate} rejects it.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{parse\_pattern} and \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} strip no
anchors at all. Both reject every anchor they encounter, the
recoverable wrap included. A caller that has reason to recover the
redundant wrap does so before invoking \texttt{translate}.

\section{Translation Size}
\label{sec:frontend-sizes}

Classes expand to alternations and bounds unroll, so the translated
\texttt{Regex} can be much larger than the source pattern. The demo
\texttt{demo\_translate\_sizes} measures this directly.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrlr@{}}
\hline
Pattern & Nodes & Pattern & Nodes \\
\hline
\texttt{a*}         &   2 & \texttt{\textbackslash d}            &  19 \\
\texttt{abc}        &   5 & \texttt{\textbackslash d\{2\}}       &  41 \\
\texttt{a\{2,4\}}   &  15 & \texttt{[a-c]+\textbackslash d\{2\}} &  54 \\
\texttt{a+}         &   4 & \texttt{\textbackslash w}            & 125 \\
\texttt{a?}         &   3 & \texttt{[\textasciicircum a]}        & 193 \\
\texttt{a\{2\}}     &   5 & \texttt{.}                           & 195 \\
\texttt{a\{2,\}}    &   8 & \texttt{\textbackslash w+}           & 252 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Translated size in \texttt{Regex} nodes for representative
patterns.}
\label{tab:translation-sizes}
\end{table}

The table has two halves. The left seven patterns translate without
expanding through the alphabet, and each grows only with its own
structure. The right seven use at least one alphabet-wide construct
(\texttt{\textbackslash d}, \texttt{\textbackslash w},
\texttt{[\textasciicircum a]}, \texttt{.}), so their node counts are
dominated by the 98-character expansion.

Three reference points. \texttt{\textbackslash d} is 19 nodes: 10
digits plus 9 \texttt{Alt} wrappers. \texttt{\textbackslash w} is 125
nodes: 63 characters plus 62 wrappers.
\texttt{[\textasciicircum a]} is 193 nodes: 97 characters plus 96
wrappers.

Two facts follow. A class costs roughly two orders of magnitude more
than one literal, so \texttt{[abc...]} and \texttt{a|b|c|...}
translate to the same tree but with very different source size. Case
folding doubles the literal count, since each letter becomes a
two-element class, so \texttt{/abc/i} translates to 11 nodes against
\texttt{/abc/}'s 5.
