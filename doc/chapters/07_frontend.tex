\chapter{External versus Internal Syntax}
\label{sec:frontend}

The four parsers of Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} all operate on the six-constructor
core \texttt{Regex} from Chapter~\ref{sec:regular-expressions}. Real
patterns are written in a richer surface language. A rule authored by
a Snort or SpamAssassin contributor uses shorthand classes, bounded
repetition, alternation over character ranges, and a flag suffix ---
none of which appear in the core language.

Bridging the two is the job of the frontend. It is the layer that
takes a pattern string, decides what the pattern means, and produces
the \texttt{Regex} the parsers consume. This chapter explains that
layer in three stages: what intermediate representation it produces
(Section~\ref{sec:frontend-extpat}), how it parses a string into that
representation (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-parse}), and how it lowers
that representation to \texttt{Regex}
(Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}). It closes with a treatment of
PCRE flags (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-flags}) and a measurement of the
translation's own cost (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-sizes}).

\section{The Interface and Its Design Choices}
\label{sec:frontend-scope}

Two functions form the public interface. The first, for a bare
pattern, is

\begin{verbatim}
parse_pattern(s: &str) -> Result<Regex, String>
\end{verbatim}

The second, for a rule wrapped in the PCRE form
\texttt{/PATTERN/FLAGS}, additionally reads the flag suffix:

\begin{verbatim}
parse_pcre_rule(s: &str) -> Result<Regex, String>
\end{verbatim}

Both return either a translated \texttt{Regex} or a rejection reason.
Three design decisions shape everything below.

\textbf{Full-string matching only.} A translated pattern matches the
entire input or nothing. There is no search-padding, no substring
search, and no anchor handling. This choice is what makes the four
parsers of the previous chapters directly usable: each decides
membership in the pattern's own language, without a notion of
``found somewhere in the input''. It is also the reason anchors are
rejected rather than translated. A source pattern beginning with
\texttt{\textasciicircum} or ending with \texttt{\textdollar} means
``match at the start/end of the buffer'' under the source tool's own
semantics, and no lowering into a full-string matcher preserves that
meaning --- except in the one case where both anchors wrap the whole
pattern, which is provably redundant. That one case is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchor-recovery}.

\textbf{A fixed working alphabet.} Shorthand classes and negated
classes expand to alternations over a fixed set of 98 characters:
printable ASCII plus tab, newline, and carriage return. The dot
metacharacter lowers to the same set when the \texttt{s} flag is set,
and to the 97-character complement of \texttt{\textbackslash n}
otherwise, matching PCRE's default reading of \texttt{.}. A literal
character written directly in the pattern is not bounded by this set
--- the core \texttt{Regex::Lit} carries a full Unicode \texttt{char}
--- so \texttt{café} still matches the string \texttt{"café"}, but
\texttt{\textbackslash W} cannot match a non-ASCII character, since
the alphabet has no such character to produce. The size consequences
of this choice are quantified in Section~\ref{sec:frontend-sizes}.

\textbf{Rejection over approximation.} When a construct cannot be
translated without changing what the pattern denotes, the frontend
rejects the pattern rather than silently mismatching it. The rejected
constructs are exactly the ones no derivative or partial-derivative
parser can consume without extension: backreferences, lookaround
assertions, word boundaries, and anchors outside the recoverable
top-level \texttt{\textasciicircum...\textdollar} wrap. The two
remaining flags, \texttt{m} and \texttt{x}, are rejected for the same
underlying reason: honouring them would require a matching model this
frontend does not have. \texttt{m} redefines
\texttt{\textasciicircum} and \texttt{\textdollar} to match at
newlines as well as at buffer boundaries, and the frontend has no
anchors to begin with; \texttt{x} makes whitespace insignificant,
which this parser does not model.

Everything outside this small set is accepted and translated. The
remainder of this chapter explains how.

\section{The External AST}
\label{sec:frontend-extpat}

The frontend parses into an intermediate type, \texttt{ExtPat}, which
sits between the surface string and the core \texttt{Regex}. It
carries enough structure to preserve what the source pattern says
--- precedence, grouping, quantifiers, and character classes as units
--- without committing to any particular lowering. The 19 variants
fall into four groups.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Group & Variant & Surface syntax \\
\hline
Structural     & \texttt{Empty}          & empty branch of \texttt{a|} or \texttt{(|b)} \\
               & \texttt{Concat}         & \texttt{abc} \\
               & \texttt{Or}             & \texttt{a|b|c} \\
               & \texttt{Group}          & \texttt{( ... )} \\
               & \texttt{GroupNonMarking}& \texttt{(?: ... )} \\
\hline
Quantifier     & \texttt{Opt}            & \texttt{r?} \\
               & \texttt{Plus}           & \texttt{r+} \\
               & \texttt{Star}           & \texttt{r*} \\
               & \texttt{Bound}          & \texttt{r\{m,n\}} \\
\hline
Class          & \texttt{Any}            & \texttt{[abc]} \\
               & \texttt{NoneOf}         & \texttt{[\textasciicircum abc]} \\
               & \texttt{Escape}         & \texttt{\textbackslash d}, \texttt{\textbackslash w}, \texttt{\textbackslash s}, ... \\
               & \texttt{Char}           & a single literal \\
\hline
Atomic / special & \texttt{Carat}          & \texttt{\textasciicircum} \\
               & \texttt{Dollar}         & \texttt{\textdollar} \\
               & \texttt{Dot}            & \texttt{.} \\
               & \texttt{WordBoundary}   & \texttt{\textbackslash b}, \texttt{\textbackslash B} \\
               & \texttt{Eps}            & $\varepsilon$ \\
               & \texttt{Never}          & $\emptyset$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{The 19 \texttt{ExtPat} variants, grouped by role. Of the
six in the last group, \texttt{Carat}, \texttt{Dollar}, and
\texttt{WordBoundary} are parsed but rejected by \texttt{translate};
\texttt{Dot} lowers to the working alphabet (or its
\texttt{\textbackslash n}-free complement);
\texttt{Eps} and \texttt{Never} lower to the core
\texttt{Regex::Eps} and \texttt{Regex::Phi}.}
\label{tab:extpat}
\end{table}

Two properties of \texttt{ExtPat} matter for the rest of the chapter.
First, the quantifier variants are single-level: \texttt{r*} carries
its atom, but not a chain. Chained quantifiers such as \texttt{a**}
are not produced by the parser, since PCRE treats them as syntax
errors. Second, the anchor and word-boundary variants are
first-class. Parsing does not reject them; it produces an
\texttt{ExtPat::Carat} or \texttt{ExtPat::WordBoundary} and leaves
the rejection to \texttt{translate}. The reason is diagnostic: a
pattern that fails at translation with the message ``anchor is not
supported'' is more informative to a corpus analyst than one that
fails at parse time with ``unexpected \texttt{\textasciicircum}''.

Rejection at parse time covers two distinct cases. The first is
constructs the parser cannot even represent: backreferences and
lookaround assertions have no \texttt{ExtPat} constructor, so the
parser has no tree to produce for them. The second is POSIX classes,
which \emph{are} representable --- as the literal characters
\texttt{[}, \texttt{:}, \texttt{a}, and so on --- but whose silent
misinterpretation would change the pattern's language. Their
rejection is a correctness measure, described in
Section~\ref{sec:frontend-parse-rejected}.

The deliberate asymmetry between parse and translate --- parse
succeeds on more than translate accepts --- is what makes a
per-rejection-cause breakdown of a corpus possible at all. Without
it, a pipeline could only say how many patterns failed, not why.

\section{Parsing: String to ExtPat}
\label{sec:frontend-parse}

Parsing is a single recursive-descent pass, one function per level of
the grammar. Precedence is fixed by the call chain.

\subsection{Precedence and associativity}

Three levels, from loosest to tightest binding:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item alternation (\texttt{|}), left-associated;
  \item concatenation (implicit), left-associated;
  \item postfix quantifiers (\texttt{?}, \texttt{+}, \texttt{*},
        \texttt{\{m,n\}}), at most one per atom.
\end{enumerate}

So \texttt{abc*} parses as \texttt{ab(c*)} and \texttt{a|b|c} parses
as \texttt{(a|b)|c}. The parser never relies on these associations for
correctness --- \texttt{translate} produces the same \texttt{Regex}
for any equivalent bracketing of an associative operator --- but the
association is observable in the resulting \texttt{Regex} tree, and
Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}'s growth measurements depend on it.

A trailing \texttt{?} after a quantifier (\texttt{r*?}, \texttt{r+?},
\texttt{r??}, \texttt{r\{m,n\}?}) is accepted and discarded. Laziness
is a backtracking-engine concept: it governs the order in which a
search explores matches, and it becomes observable only when that
order changes an externally visible result --- which substring a
search returns, or what a capture group holds. Neither channel exists
here. The core \texttt{Regex} has no capture structure, and the
parsers of Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} decide membership rather than
search, so no operation ever returns a substring. The marker is
therefore discarded during parsing, before any \texttt{ExtPat} is
built: \texttt{r*} and \texttt{r*?} produce the same
\texttt{ExtPat::Star}.

This is a separate concern from the disambiguation policy the parsers
apply. A source pattern such as \texttt{(a+ab)(b+\textbackslash
varepsilon)} admits more than one valid parse tree for the same input,
and which tree a parser returns is decided by the parser's own policy
(POSIX leftmost-longest for the derivative-based parsers, greedy
leftmost for the partial-derivative ones), not by anything in the
source quantifier syntax. The \texttt{?} marker is not the mechanism
by which a PCRE author selects between those policies; it is a hint
that only a backtracking engine reads, and there is no backtracking
engine here to read it.

\subsection{Accepted surface syntax}

Beyond the core constructs already listed in
Table~\ref{tab:extpat}, the parser accepts several forms from the PCRE
family.

\textbf{Groups.} \texttt{( ... )} and \texttt{(?: ... )} both parse;
the distinction is preserved in \texttt{ExtPat} but erased at
translation. Named groups \texttt{(?<name>...)} and
\texttt{(?P<name>...)} are accepted with the name dropped. The name is
not recoverable from the resulting \texttt{Regex}, since
\texttt{Regex} has no capture structure, and the parser does not
attempt to preserve it.

\textbf{Character classes.} A class body is read as a sequence of
members. A \texttt{-} between two literal characters denotes a range;
a leading or trailing \texttt{-} is a literal member, and so is a
leading \texttt{]}. Shorthand escapes inside a class
(\texttt{\textbackslash d}, \texttt{\textbackslash w},
\texttt{\textbackslash s}, and their capitalised negations) expand
inline as separate members. A shorthand cannot be a range endpoint:
\texttt{[a-\textbackslash d]} is rejected.

\textbf{Escapes outside a class.} \texttt{\textbackslash n},
\texttt{\textbackslash t}, \texttt{\textbackslash r} produce the
corresponding control characters; \texttt{\textbackslash xHH} produces
the byte given by two hex digits; \texttt{\textbackslash 0} produces an
octal escape (up to two further octal digits). A single digit
\texttt{\textbackslash 1}--\texttt{\textbackslash 9} is rejected, since
PCRE resolves those as backreferences using a capture-group count this
parser does not track.

\textbf{Octal escapes inside a class.} Inside a class, a
\texttt{\textbackslash} followed by an octal digit reads up to three
octal digits. This is a real-corpus concern: SpamAssassin rules use
patterns such as \verb|[\042\223\224\262\263\271]| to match Windows
codepage punctuation, and misreading \verb|\042| as the three literal
characters \texttt{0}, \texttt{4}, \texttt{2} would silently change
the pattern's language. \texttt{\textbackslash 8} and
\texttt{\textbackslash 9} are not octal digits and are read as literals
\texttt{8} and \texttt{9}, matching PCRE's own behaviour.

\textbf{Explicit $\varepsilon$ and $\emptyset$.} The two Unicode tokens
are accepted as \texttt{ExtPat::Eps} and \texttt{ExtPat::Never}. They
are extensions for the worked examples of earlier chapters, not part
of the PCRE family.

\subsection{Rejected surface syntax}
\label{sec:frontend-parse-rejected}

Rejection falls into two categories: constructs the grammar cannot
represent, and constructs the frontend chooses not to represent.

\textbf{Not representable.} Backreferences
(\texttt{\textbackslash 1}--\texttt{\textbackslash 9},
\texttt{(?P=name)}), lookahead \texttt{(?=...)} and \texttt{(?!...)},
and lookbehind \texttt{(?<=...)} and \texttt{(?<!...)} are rejected at
parse time. None of them is a regular-language construct, and the
external AST has no constructor for them. Real corpus examples:

\begin{verbatim}
/^(?P<addr1>.{4})(?P<addr2>.{4})(?P=addr2)(?P=addr1)/Rsi
    (Snort, named backreference)
/(?=(?:(0x[a-f0-9]{2}|0+\d{1,3})\s*?,\s*?)*?\d{1,3}).../Rsi
    (Snort, chained lookahead)
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{Representable but rejected.} Word boundaries
\texttt{\textbackslash b} and \texttt{\textbackslash B}, and anchors
\texttt{\textasciicircum} and \texttt{\textdollar}, parse successfully
into \texttt{ExtPat} but are rejected by \texttt{translate}. Both are
position-dependent: their meaning depends on the boundary between the
matched substring and the surrounding buffer, which a full-string
matcher has no way to model. Rejecting them at parse time would have
worked just as well, but the parse-time success lets the reject
message name the construct. Corpus examples:

\begin{verbatim}
/\bsolusvmc-node\b/          (Snort, word boundary)
/\.ida$/iU                   (Snort, end anchor)
/^Argument\s+\//smi          (Snort, start anchor + m flag)
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{POSIX classes.} A class of the form
\texttt{[:name:]} inside a bracket expression is rejected with a
specific message. Without this check, \verb|[[:alpha:]]| would parse
as the five literal characters \texttt{[}, \texttt{:}, \texttt{a},
\texttt{l}, \texttt{p}, followed by \texttt{h}, \texttt{a}, and a
closing bracket --- a pattern that denotes a completely different
language and that would have been accepted silently. The rejection is
a correctness measure, not a stylistic choice.

\section{PCRE Flags}
\label{sec:frontend-flags}

A pattern passed to \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} may be wrapped in a
\texttt{/PATTERN/FLAGS} form. The frontend reads the flag suffix and
sorts the characters into three groups: honoured, rejected, and
ignored.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Flag & Group & Effect \\
\hline
\texttt{i} & honoured  & case-fold every literal letter \\
\texttt{s} & honoured  & \texttt{.} includes newline \\
\texttt{m} & rejected  & would change anchor meaning this frontend lacks \\
\texttt{x} & rejected  & would change whitespace meaning this parser lacks \\
\texttt{R} & ignored   & positioned a search this frontend does not perform \\
\texttt{A} & ignored   & anchored a search this frontend does not perform \\
\texttt{E} & ignored   & tuned a \texttt{\textdollar} this frontend rejects \\
\texttt{U}, \texttt{H}, \texttt{P}, \texttt{C}, ... & ignored & named a buffer or normalisation strategy \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{The three flag groups. ``Ignored'' means the pattern parses
exactly as it would without the flag.}
\label{tab:flags}
\end{table}

The distinction between ``honoured'' and ``ignored'' is whether the
flag changes what the pattern denotes. A buffer selector such as
\texttt{U} says which region of the input to test against, not what
the pattern itself matches; under full-string matching there is only
one region. A relative-position flag \texttt{R} says how to position
a subsequent search, which does not exist in this frontend. Neither
of these changes the language the pattern denotes, so both are
harmless to ignore. An honoured flag such as \texttt{i} does change
the language, so it must be applied.

Honoured flags are applied between parse and translate, as
preprocessing passes over the \texttt{ExtPat}. This is the natural
place: the passes are structural rewrites that do not need the
pattern's own scope information --- \texttt{case\_fold} turns every
literal letter into a two-element \texttt{Any}, and
\texttt{strip\_dot\_newline} turns every \texttt{Dot} into
\texttt{NoneOf(\textbackslash n)} --- so they can run once on the
whole tree.

\section{Translation: ExtPat to Regex}
\label{sec:frontend-translate}

Translation is a second structural pass, this time over the whole
\texttt{ExtPat}, producing a \texttt{Regex}. Most variants have a
direct counterpart: \texttt{Concat}, \texttt{Or}, \texttt{Opt},
\texttt{Plus}, and \texttt{Star} map to the corresponding core
constructors or a one-line desugaring of them. Three parts of the
translation are less direct and are described below.

\subsection{Character classes and the working alphabet}
\label{sec:frontend-alphabet}

An \texttt{ExtPat::Any(cs)} lowers to an alternation over
\texttt{cs}: one \texttt{Alt} node per element, folded left.
\texttt{NoneOf(cs)} first takes the complement of \texttt{cs} against
the working alphabet and then does the same. \texttt{Dot} lowers to
\texttt{Any} over the full alphabet when the \texttt{s} flag is set,
and has already been rewritten to \texttt{NoneOf(\textbackslash n)}
otherwise. Shorthand escapes (\texttt{\textbackslash d},
\texttt{\textbackslash w}, \texttt{\textbackslash s}, and their
capitalised forms) lower through the same path.

The consequence is that \texttt{Dot}, the shorthand classes, and any
negated class cannot match a character outside the working alphabet.
The evaluation's corpus contains a few Snort patterns that include
non-ASCII literals; those are unaffected, because a direct literal
lowers to a single \texttt{Regex::Lit} that carries the full
\texttt{char}. The working alphabet only constrains the constructs
that need to enumerate a set.

\subsection{Bounds and unrolling}

\texttt{ExtPat::Bound} is the only quantifier without a direct core
construct, since \texttt{Regex} has no \texttt{\{m,n\}} form. It
lowers by unrolling: \texttt{r\{m,n\}} becomes \texttt{m} exact copies
of the translated body, followed by \texttt{n-m} optional copies.
\texttt{r\{m,\}} becomes \texttt{m} exact copies followed by a
\texttt{Star}. Both forms clone the body, so the resulting
\texttt{Regex} has size proportional to the bound.

Two caps guard this unrolling. A bound whose upper or lower endpoint
exceeds 1000 is rejected rather than unrolled. Real Snort patterns
sometimes carry a bound in the thousands ---
\verb|/(GET).\/%.{1586,}/i| is one such rule --- and unrolling 1586
copies would blow up the subsequent derivative computation for no
matching benefit. The cap is enforced before any translation, so the
rejection is immediate.

A second, coarser cap is applied further downstream, by the corpus
preparation of Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}, and rejects a pattern if
the translated \texttt{Regex} would exceed a node count. That cap does
not affect the frontend's own behaviour, only the pipeline's decision
to skip a pattern before generation.

\subsection{Anchor recovery}
\label{sec:frontend-anchor-recovery}

Translation is where anchors are rejected, with one exception. A
pattern whose top level is a single \texttt{Concat} beginning with
\texttt{Carat} and ending with \texttt{Dollar} has those two anchors
stripped before translation. The reasoning is that under full-string
matching they are provably redundant: consuming the whole input
already requires both endpoints, so removing them changes nothing
about what the pattern denotes.

The exception is narrow. A lone \texttt{\textasciicircum}, a lone
\texttt{\textdollar}, or either anchor nested inside an alternation
branch or under a quantifier is not stripped, and reaches
\texttt{translate} where it is rejected.

The recovery is not part of the frontend's own public interface.
Neither \texttt{parse\_pattern} nor \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule}
strips an anchor; both translate every anchor to a rejection. A
caller that has a reason to recover the redundant wrap can do so
before invoking \texttt{translate}, and the corpus preparation of
Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} is such a caller. The frontend's
behaviour is unchanged either way: it accepts every pattern that
\texttt{translate} accepts, and rejects every anchor on its own.

The evaluation classifies every accepted pattern by whether it
carried the recoverable wrap or had no anchor at all. Both classes
are generated for and benchmarked identically, since the anchors'
redundancy means the two patterns have the same \texttt{Regex}.

\section{Translation Size}
\label{sec:frontend-sizes}

Because classes expand to alternations and bounds unroll, the
translated \texttt{Regex} can be much larger than the source pattern.
The demo \texttt{demo\_frontend\_translation\_sizes} measures this
directly.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrlr}
\hline
Pattern & Nodes & Pattern & Nodes \\
\hline
\texttt{a*}         &   2 & \texttt{\textbackslash d}         &  19 \\
\texttt{abc}        &   5 & \texttt{\textbackslash d\{2\}}    &  41 \\
\texttt{a\{2,4\}}   &  15 & \texttt{[a-c]+\textbackslash d\{2\}} & 54 \\
\texttt{a+}         &   4 & \texttt{\textbackslash w}         & 125 \\
\texttt{a?}         &   3 & \texttt{[\textasciicircum a]}     & 193 \\
\texttt{a\{2\}}     &   5 & \texttt{.}                        & 195 \\
\texttt{a\{2,\}}    &   8 & \texttt{\textbackslash w+}        & 252 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Translated size in \texttt{Regex} nodes for representative
patterns.}
\label{tab:translation-sizes}
\end{table}

The right-hand side of the table shows the cost of the 98-character
alphabet. A single \texttt{\textbackslash d} contributes 19 nodes: one
for each of the ten digits, plus nine \texttt{Alt} wrappers to chain
them. \texttt{\textbackslash w} is 125 nodes for 63 characters.
\texttt{[\textasciicircum a]} is 193 nodes: one for every alphabet
character except \texttt{a}, plus the chain.

Two consequences follow. First, patterns with many distinct classes
can produce large translations even when the source is short, which
is why a downstream pipeline carries a node-count cap. Second, the
constant factor between ``one class'' and ``one literal'' is roughly
two orders of magnitude, so a pattern written as an explicit
alternation over literal characters (\texttt{(a|b|c|...)}) is not
equivalent, size-wise, to the same class written as
\texttt{[abc...]}. Both lower to the same \texttt{Regex}, but the
class notation makes the size cost visible only after translation.

Case folding adds to this cost linearly. For \texttt{/abc/i}, the
translated pattern grows from 5 nodes (for \texttt{/abc/}) to 11, an
increase of 6 nodes, i.e.\ 2 per letter. Each letter is replaced by a
two-element class, and each such class contributes one extra
\texttt{Alt} wrapper.

\section{Summary and Bridge to Evaluation}
\label{sec:frontend-summary}

The frontend sits between two formalisms. On one side is the surface
syntax that real pattern authors write; on the other is the
six-constructor \texttt{Regex} the four parsers of the previous
chapters can consume. The bridge between them is a two-pass design
--- parse to \texttt{ExtPat}, then translate to \texttt{Regex}, with
flag-driven preprocessing in between --- whose every choice has a
consequence in the evaluation that follows.

Three consequences are worth naming explicitly.

\textbf{Every rejection is a frontend decision.} None of the four
parsers rejects a pattern. Every pattern that reaches a parser has
already been through the frontend's filter, and the reasons a pattern
is filtered out are collected in
Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}'s rejection table. That table is
therefore a statement about the frontend's scope, not the parsers'
abilities.

\textbf{The corpus is bounded by the working alphabet.} On the
patterns the evaluation uses, this bound is not restrictive: the
corpus is text-oriented, and every pattern that survives translation
matches only characters the alphabet contains. But the choice is
visible in the translated sizes above, and it is worth a note in the
evaluation's threats-to-validity section.

\textbf{Translation size is not parser cost.} A pattern that lowers
to 250 nodes is larger to hold in memory than one that lowers to 5,
but the two parsers that handle it may still perform similarly. The
evaluation separates the two: the frontend's own cost is what
Table~\ref{tab:translation-sizes} measures, and the parsers' cost is
what the timing and memory benchmarks measure. Conflating the two is
easy --- a pattern's ``complexity'' can mean either --- so the
distinction is maintained throughout.
