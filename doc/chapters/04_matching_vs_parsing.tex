\chapter{Matching versus Parsing}
\label{sec:matching-vs-parsing}

Chapter~\ref{sec:derivatives} established matching with a Boolean
answer: whether $w \in L(r)$. A matcher therefore answers only whether
the input is accepted by the regular expression. Parsing goes beyond
this membership question by identifying the derivation for the match.

This distinction is important because a regular expression can admit
more than one way of matching the same word. A parser must therefore
represent the choices made during a match. These choices can be
represented by \emph{parse trees}, which provide structured witnesses
for membership in the language.

%

\section{The Matching Problem}
\label{sec:matching-problem}

Consider the regular expression
\[
r = (a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)
\]
and the input word
\[
w = \mathtt{ab}.
\]

The matcher can establish that $w \in L(r)$ using the matching
procedures of Chapter~\ref{sec:derivatives}. There are, however, two
ways in which the match can be obtained. In the first, the left
subexpression matches $\mathtt{a}$ and the right subexpression matches
$\mathtt{b}$:
\[
\underbrace{a}_{a+ab}\;
\underbrace{b}_{b+\varepsilon}.
\]

In the second, the left subexpression matches the entire input and the
right subexpression matches the empty word:
\[
\underbrace{\mathtt{ab}}_{a+ab}\;
\underbrace{\varepsilon}_{b+\varepsilon},
\]

Both choices therefore establish the same membership result,
\[
\mathtt{ab} \in L\bigl((a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)\bigr),
\]
but assign the input differently to the subexpressions of $r$.
The Boolean result of matching does not distinguish these cases.

Parsing makes this additional structure explicit. A parser must represent
which alternatives were chosen and which parts of the input were
consumed by the corresponding subexpressions. 
A \emph{parse tree} provides a structured witness for such a derivation,
recording the choices made by the expression and the corresponding
consumption of the input.

%

\section{Parse Trees as Matching Evidence}
\label{sec:parse-trees-as-evidence}

Sulzmann and Lu view regular expressions as types and parse trees as
their inhabitants \citep{sulzmannlu2014}. A parse tree $v$ records the
structural choices made while matching an expression $r$.

A judgment $\vdash v : r$ states that $v$ is a valid parse tree for $r$.
The judgment is defined by:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:typing-rules}
\begin{array}{ll}
\vdash () : \varepsilon
&
(\mathsf{T}_{\varepsilon})
\\[1.0em]

\dfrac{l \in \Sigma}{\vdash l : l}
&
(\mathsf{T}_{\mathrm{lit}})
\\[1.4em]

\dfrac{\vdash v_1 : r_1 \qquad \vdash v_2 : r_2}
      {\vdash (v_1,v_2) : r_1 r_2}
&
(\mathsf{T}_{\mathrm{seq}})
\\[1.4em]

\dfrac{\vdash v_1 : r_1}
      {\vdash \mathit{Left}\ v_1 : r_1 + r_2}
&
(\mathsf{T}_{+}^{L})
\\[1.4em]

\dfrac{\vdash v_2 : r_2}
      {\vdash \mathit{Right}\ v_2 : r_1 + r_2}
&
(\mathsf{T}_{+}^{R})
\\[1.4em]

\dfrac{}{\vdash [\,] : r^{*}}
&
(\mathsf{T}_{*}^{0})
\\[1.4em]

\dfrac{\vdash v : r \qquad \vdash vs : r^{*}}
      {\vdash (v:vs) : r^{*}}
&
(\mathsf{T}_{*})
\end{array}
\end{equation}

The rules correspond to the constructors of regular
expressions. A concatenation uses a pair of parse trees, an alternation
records the chosen branch with a tagged injection, and a Kleene star
records its iterations as a list.

The typing judgment describes the structure of a match, but a parse tree
must also be related to the word that it matches. For this purpose, they
define a \emph{flattening} function $\lvert v\rvert$ that maps a parse
tree to the word represented by its structure:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:flatten}
\begin{aligned}
\lvert ()\rvert
  &= \varepsilon,
\\
\lvert l\rvert
  &= l,
\\
\lvert [\,]\rvert
  &= \varepsilon,
\\
\lvert (v_1,v_2)\rvert
  &= \lvert v_1\rvert\lvert v_2\rvert,
\\
\lvert \mathit{Left}\ v\rvert
  &= \lvert v\rvert,
\\
\lvert \mathit{Right}\ v\rvert
  &= \lvert v\rvert,
\\
\lvert v:vs\rvert
  &= \lvert v\rvert\lvert vs\rvert.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The flattening function connects parse trees to the language of a regular
expression:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-5}
L(r)
=
\{\,\lvert v\rvert \mid \vdash v : r\,\}.
\end{equation}

Thus, a parse tree $v$ provides a structured \emph{witness} for
membership in $L(r)$, recording both the match and the structural
choices by which $r$ produces the word.



%

\paragraph{Unambiguous example.}
Consider $r = ab$ and $w = \mathtt{ab}$. The sequence rule requires a
parse tree for $a$ followed by a parse tree for $b$. Since each
subexpression is a literal, the literal rule gives the only possible
derivation:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-6}
\dfrac{
  \dfrac{a \in \Sigma}{\vdash a : a}\;(\mathsf{T}_{\mathrm{lit}})
  \qquad
  \dfrac{b \in \Sigma}{\vdash b : b}\;(\mathsf{T}_{\mathrm{lit}})
}{
  \vdash (a,b) : ab
}\;(\mathsf{T}_{\mathrm{seq}})
\end{equation}

Thus the unique parse tree is $v=(a,b)$. Its flattening recovers the
input word:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-1}
\begin{aligned}
\lvert (a,b)\rvert
  &= \lvert a\rvert\,\lvert b\rvert 
  &= \mathtt{a}\mathtt{b}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

When the structure of the expression permits different choices, a word
may have multiple parse trees. The first two examples are adapted from
the running examples of \citet{sulzmannlu2014}, but are developed here
in more detail to establish the derivations used later in the discussion
of the parser implementations.

%

\paragraph{Ambiguous example 1: concatenation.}
The example from the introduction can now be revisited using parse trees.
For
$r_1 = (a + ab)(b + \varepsilon)$ and $w = \mathtt{ab}$, there are two
possible matches, corresponding to two distinct parse trees.

The first consumes $\mathtt{ab}$ with the second alternative of $a+ab$
and then chooses $\varepsilon$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-7}
\dfrac{
  \dfrac{\vdash a : a \quad \vdash b : b}
        {\vdash (a,b) : ab}
}{
  \vdash \mathit{Right}(a,b) : a + ab
}
\quad
\dfrac{
  \vdash () : \varepsilon
}{
  \vdash \mathit{Right}() : b + \varepsilon
}
\quad
\Rightarrow
\quad
\vdash
\bigl(\mathit{Right}(a,b),\mathit{Right}()\bigr)
:
(a+ab)(b+\varepsilon).
\end{equation}

Thus,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-8}
v =
\bigl(\mathit{Right}(a,b),\mathit{Right}()\bigr),
\qquad
\lvert v\rvert = \mathtt{ab}.
\end{equation}

The second consumes $\mathtt{a}$ with the first alternative of $a+ab$
and $\mathtt{b}$ with the first alternative of $b+\varepsilon$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-9}
\dfrac{\vdash a : a}
      {\vdash \mathit{Left}\ a : a + ab}
\quad
\dfrac{\vdash b : b}
      {\vdash \mathit{Left}\ b : b + \varepsilon}
\quad
\Rightarrow
\quad
\vdash
\bigl(\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ b\bigr)
:
(a+ab)(b+\varepsilon).
\end{equation}

Thus,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-10}
v' =
\bigl(\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ b\bigr),
\qquad
\lvert v'\rvert = \mathtt{ab}.
\end{equation}

Hence the same expression and word have two distinct parse trees,
$v \neq v'$, both of which flatten to $\mathtt{ab}$.


%

\paragraph{Ambiguous example 2: repetition.}
The same example from \citet{sulzmannlu2014} also illustrates ambiguity
arising from repetition. Consider
$r_2 = (a+b+ab)^{*}$ against $w = \mathtt{ab}$, with the alternation
nested as $a+(b+ab)$. There are two possible matches: one iteration can
consume the entire word using the $ab$ alternative, or two iterations
can consume $\mathtt{a}$ and $\mathtt{b}$ separately.

The first match is given by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-16}
\dfrac{
  \dfrac{
    \dfrac{\vdash a : a \quad \vdash b : b}
          {\vdash (a,b) : ab}
  }{
    \vdash \mathit{Right}(a,b) : b+ab
  }
}{
  \vdash \mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}(a,b) : a+(b+ab)
}
\quad
\Rightarrow
\quad
\vdash
[\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}(a,b)]
:
(a+(b+ab))^{*}.
\end{equation}

Thus,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-17}
v_2 =
[\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}(a,b)],
\qquad
\lvert v_2\rvert = \mathtt{ab}.
\end{equation}

The second match uses two iterations, one for each character:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-18}
\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{\vdash a : a}
      {\vdash \mathit{Left}\ a : a+(b+ab)}
&\qquad
\dfrac{
  \dfrac{\vdash b : b}
        {\vdash \mathit{Left}\ b : b+ab}
}{
  \vdash \mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Left}\ b : a+(b+ab)
}
\\[1.2em]
&\qquad\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\vdash
[\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Left}\ b]
:
(a+(b+ab))^{*}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


Thus,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-19}
v_2' =
[\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Left}\ b],
\qquad
\lvert v_2'\rvert = \mathtt{ab}.
\end{equation}

Hence $v_2 \neq v_2'$ are two distinct parse trees for the same
expression and word. Here the ambiguity comes from the number of
iterations of the Kleene star.

%

\paragraph{Ambiguous example 3: duplicate alternatives.}
A different form of ambiguity arises when an alternation contains the
same alternative twice. Consider $r_3 = (a+a)^{*}$ against
$w = \mathtt{aa}$. Each iteration can choose either copy of $a$, so
each occurrence of $\mathtt{a}$ gives two choices:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-20}
\begin{aligned}
v_3^{LL} &= [\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ a], &
v_3^{LR} &= [\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Right}\ a],\\
v_3^{RL} &= [\mathit{Right}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ a], &
v_3^{RR} &= [\mathit{Right}\ a,\mathit{Right}\ a].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Each is derivable using $\mathsf{T}_{+}^{L}$ or
$\mathsf{T}_{+}^{R}$ for each iteration and $\mathsf{T}_{*}$ to form
the list, and all four flatten to the same word:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-21}
\lvert v_3^{LL}\rvert
=
\lvert v_3^{LR}\rvert
=
\lvert v_3^{RL}\rvert
=
\lvert v_3^{RR}\rvert
=
\mathtt{aa}.
\end{equation}

Thus the four parse trees differ only in which syntactically distinct
copy of $a$ is selected. The ambiguity is therefore caused by duplicate
alternatives rather than by different ways of partitioning the input.

%

\paragraph{Ambiguous example 4: nested repetition.}
A further source of ambiguity is nested repetition. Consider
$r_4 = (a^{*})^{*}$ against $w=\mathtt{aa}$. The word can be matched
by a single outer iteration whose inner repetition consumes both
characters, or by two outer iterations, each containing one inner
iteration.

The first match uses one outer iteration and two inner iterations:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-22}
\vdash
\bigl[
  [a,a]
\bigr]
:
(a^{*})^{*},
\end{equation}

with parse tree

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-23}
v_4 = [[a,a]],
\qquad
\lvert v_4\rvert = \mathtt{aa}.
\end{equation}

The second uses two outer iterations, each containing one inner
iteration:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-24}
\vdash
\bigl[
  [a],
  [a]
\bigr]
:
(a^{*})^{*},
\end{equation}

with parse tree

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-25}
v_4' = [[a],[a]],
\qquad
\lvert v_4'\rvert = \mathtt{aa}.
\end{equation}

Thus $v_4 \neq v_4'$ are two distinct parse trees for the same
expression and word. The ambiguity here arises from the interaction of
nested repetitions: the same input can be partitioned differently
between the iterations of the inner and outer Kleene stars.

\section{Disambiguation Policies}
\label{sec:disambiguation-problem}

The parse-tree judgment identifies all valid witnesses for a match, but
does not determine which witness a parser should return. The last two
examples also show that not every ambiguity needs to be resolved by the
parser itself. Duplicate alternatives, as in $r_3$, can be removed by
simplifying the expression, while nested repetition, as in $r_4$, can
likewise be rewritten without changing the language. However, the first two
examples are different. Their parse trees represent genuinely different
ways of matching the same input, so simplifying the expression does not
by itself determine which match should be selected.

This raises the question of how a parser should choose between such
valid matches. A \emph{disambiguation policy} to make this choice.
Given a regular expression $r$ and word $w$, it selects one parse tree
$v$ satisfying

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-26}
\vdash v : r
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\lvert v\rvert = w.
\end{equation}

The two policies considered here make this choice according to
different principles. Greedy matching follows the structural priority
of the expression, whereas POSIX matching gives priority to longer
leftmost submatches. Their difference becomes clear when they are
applied to the same ambiguous expression.

%

\subsection{Greedy Leftmost Matching}
\label{sec:greedy-leftmost}

The policy associated with conventional backtracking matchers is
\emph{Greedy}: alternatives are considered from left to right, and the
first one leading to an overall match is selected; within a Kleene star,
another iteration is preferred whenever it can still lead to an overall
match \citep{frischcardelli2004}. 

Both preferences are structural, because they
are decided by the position of a subexpression in $r$, and never by how
much input a candidate consumes.

%

\paragraph{Applied to $r_1$.}
For $r_1=(a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$ and $w=\mathtt{ab}$, Greedy selects the
left alternative of $a+ab$, consuming $\mathtt{a}$, followed by the
left alternative of $b+\varepsilon$, consuming $\mathtt{b}$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-greedy-r1}
v_1^{G}
=
\bigl(\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ b\bigr).
\end{equation}

%

\paragraph{Applied to $r_2$.}
For $r_2=(a+(b+ab))^{*}$ and $w=\mathtt{ab}$, Greedy first consumes
$\mathtt{a}$ with the left alternative, then continues the star and
matches $\mathtt{b}$ with its second alternative:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-greedy-r2}
v_2^{G}
=
[\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Left}\ b].
\end{equation}

%

\paragraph{Applied to $r_3$.}
For $r_3=(a+a)^{*}$ and $w=\mathtt{aa}$, both alternatives consume one
character, so the left alternative is selected at each iteration:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-greedy-r3}
v_3^{G}
=
[\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ a].
\end{equation}

%

\paragraph{Applied to $r_4$.}
For $r_4=(a^{*})^{*}$ and $w=\mathtt{aa}$, the preference for continuing
a repetition applies first to the inner star, since that is the star the
first character is consumed by. Having consumed $\mathtt{a}$, the inner
star takes a second iteration instead of closing its list, so the first
outer iteration consumes both characters:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-greedy-r4}
v_4^{G}
=
[[a,a]].
\end{equation}

The alternative $[[a],[a]]$ closes the inner star after one character and
opens a second outer iteration. Greedy reaches the outer star only with
the input the inner star has declined, so this tree is not selected.

%

\paragraph{Procedural versus declarative view.}
The description above gives a procedural intuition for Greedy matching.
Alternatives are considered from left to right, and repetition continues
when doing so can still lead to a complete match. The same policy can be
stated declaratively as an ordering on the parse trees admitted by $r$ and
$w$, with the selected tree being the maximal one
\citep{frischcardelli2004}. This declarative view is useful for comparing
Greedy with other disambiguation policies because it makes the preferred
parse tree an explicit object of comparison.

These two views describe the same policy but
are not the same computationally. Frisch and Cardelli formulate the
ordering independently of any particular matching algorithm and show how
it can be computed without backtracking.

Written as inference rules, the Greedy ordering $v_1 \succ_r v_2$ is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:greedy-order}
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{(C1)} &
\dfrac{
  v_1 = v_1'
  \quad
  v_2 \succ_{r_2} v_2'
}{
  (v_1,v_2) \succ_{r_1 r_2} (v_1',v_2')
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(C2)} &
\dfrac{
  v_1 \succ_{r_1} v_1'
}{
  (v_1,v_2) \succ_{r_1 r_2} (v_1',v_2')
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(G1)} &
\dfrac{
}{
  \mathit{Left}\ v_1
  \succ_{r_1+r_2}
  \mathit{Right}\ v_2
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(A3)} &
\dfrac{
  v_2 \succ_{r_2} v_2'
}{
  \mathit{Right}\ v_2
  \succ_{r_1+r_2}
  \mathit{Right}\ v_2'
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(A4)} &
\dfrac{
  v_1 \succ_{r_1} v_1'
}{
  \mathit{Left}\ v_1
  \succ_{r_1+r_2}
  \mathit{Left}\ v_1'
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(G2)} &
\dfrac{
}{
  v:vs
  \succ_{r^{*}}
  [\,]
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(K3)} &
\dfrac{
  v_1 \succ_r v_2
}{
  v_1:vs_1
  \succ_{r^{*}}
  v_2:vs_2
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(K4)} &
\dfrac{
  v_1 = v_2
  \quad
  vs_1 \succ_{r^{*}} vs_2
}{
  v_1:vs_1
  \succ_{r^{*}}
  v_2:vs_2
}
\end{array}
\end{equation}

Rules (C1) and (C2) propagate comparisons through concatenation. Rule
(C2) gives priority to the first component whenever it differs, and
(C1) compares the second component when the first components are equal,
so earlier subexpressions have priority over later ones. Rules (G1) and
(G2) are the two structural preferences of the procedural description:
(G1) prefers the left alternative outright, and (G2) prefers any
non-empty list of iterations to stopping. Neither has a premise, so
neither consults what the competing trees consume. Rules (A3), (A4),
(K3) and (K4) are congruence rules: they carry a comparison into a
position where both candidates have already made the same choice, first
comparing the heads of two iteration lists and then their tails.

For example, consider the two parse trees
\[
v=(\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ b),
\qquad
v'=(\mathit{Right}(a,b),\mathit{Right}()) 
\]
for $r_1=(a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$. Since the first components differ,
(C2) reduces the comparison to
\[
\mathit{Left}\ a
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\mathit{Right}(a,b)
\]
within $a+ab$. Rule (G1) immediately gives
\[
\mathit{Left}\ a
\succ_{a+ab}
\mathit{Right}(a,b),
\]
without comparing the lengths of the two matches. Thus the structural
priority of the left alternative determines the result before the
second component is considered.

This distinction is relevant for the parser implementations considered
later. The partial-derivative parsers do not enumerate all parse trees and
then select a maximal one, but compute a kind of Greedy result directly. This
also matters when a maximal parse tree does not exist, as the following
example demonstrates.

%

\subsection{The Infinite Empty-Match Chain Problem}
\label{sec:infinite-empty-chain}

Greedy's preference for continuing a repetition creates a problem when
the repeated expression can match the empty word. Consider
$\varepsilon^{*}$ against the empty input. The following parse trees are
all valid:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-28}
v_0 = [\,],
\qquad
v_1 = [()],
\qquad
v_2 = [(),()],
\qquad \ldots
\end{equation}

Each additional iteration still flattens to $\varepsilon$, and Greedy
prefers every additional iteration:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-29}
v_0 \prec_{\varepsilon^{*}} v_1 \prec_{\varepsilon^{*}}
v_2 \prec_{\varepsilon^{*}} \cdots.
\end{equation}

There is therefore no maximal parse tree. The language itself is not
problematic: $\varepsilon$ is a valid match. The problem is that the
Greedy ordering does not identify a largest witness when empty iterations
can be added indefinitely.

A less degenerate example is $(\varepsilon+a)^{*}$ against
$\mathtt{a}$. The parse trees

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-30}
\begin{aligned}
v_0 &= [\mathit{Right}\ a],\\
v_1 &= [\mathit{Left}\ (),\mathit{Right}\ a],\\
v_2 &= [\mathit{Left}\ (),\mathit{Left}\ (),\mathit{Right}\ a],\\
&\quad\vdots
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

all flatten to $\mathtt{a}$, while Greedy again prefers each additional
empty iteration.

The same phenomenon occurs whenever a star's body is nullable. For
$(a^{*})^{*}$ against $\mathtt{aaa}$, for example, the inner star can
match $\varepsilon$, so additional empty outer iterations can always be
added:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-31}
[[a,a,a]]
\;\prec_{(a^{*})^{*}}\;
[[a,a,a],[\,]]
\;\prec_{(a^{*})^{*}}\;
[[a,a,a],[\,],[\,]]
\;\prec_{(a^{*})^{*}}\;
\cdots.
\end{equation}

Thus the Greedy ordering may contain an infinite ascending chain without
a maximal element. Engines that search directly for such a maximal match
can encounter the familiar difficulties of nested nullable repetition
\citep{cox2007}. The frontier-based Greedy parsers of
Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}, by contrast, compute the
match without searching for an ordering maximum.

Two premise-free rules of Equation~\eqref{eq:greedy-order} produce these
chains. (G2) prefers a non-empty iteration list to the empty one without
considering what it consumes, so an iteration flattening to $\varepsilon$
can always be added again. In the second example, (G1) instead
prefers the $\mathit{Left}\ ()$ branch of $\varepsilon+a$ regardless of
length, so (K3) ranks each tree above the one with one fewer empty
iteration. In both cases, the deciding rule has no premise and therefore
cannot detect that the added iteration contributes nothing.

This exposes a limitation of the Greedy ordering: on such an expression
a maximal parse tree does not exist, so the ordering alone does not
specify an answer. Frisch and Cardelli resolve this by restricting the
specification to parse trees in which no iteration flattens to
$\varepsilon$, and show that such a tree exists whenever the word is
matched at all \citep{frischcardelli2004}. The POSIX ordering instead
settles these comparisons within the rules themselves, as
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost} shows.

%

\subsection{POSIX Longest-Leftmost Matching}
\label{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}

POSIX uses a different disambiguation policy. Subpatterns are compared
according to the length of the substrings they match, with subpatterns
that occur earlier in the regular expression taking priority. Section
9.1 of the POSIX Base Definitions states that, consistent with the whole
match being the longest of the leftmost matches, each subpattern, from
left to right, shall match the longest possible string, and that for
this purpose a null string is considered longer than no match at all
\citep{posix_ere}.

Sulzmann and Lu specify this policy by reusing the Greedy ordering of
Equation~\eqref{eq:greedy-order} and substituting for its two structural
preferences \citep{sulzmannlu2014}. The congruence rules (A3), (A4),
(K3) and (K4) and the concatenation rules (C1) and (C2) are kept
unchanged. In their place, (G1) becomes the length-sensitive pair

\begin{equation}\label{eq:posix-alternative-rules}
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{(A1)} &
\dfrac{
  \operatorname{len}(\lvert v_2\rvert)
  >
  \operatorname{len}(\lvert v_1\rvert)
}{
  \mathit{Right}\ v_2
  \succ_{r_1+r_2}
  \mathit{Left}\ v_1
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(A2)} &
\dfrac{
  \operatorname{len}(\lvert v_1\rvert)
  \geq
  \operatorname{len}(\lvert v_2\rvert)
}{
  \mathit{Left}\ v_1
  \succ_{r_1+r_2}
  \mathit{Right}\ v_2
}
\end{array}
\end{equation}

and (G2) becomes the pair

\begin{equation}\label{eq:posix-star-rules}
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{(K1)} &
\dfrac{
  \lvert v:vs\rvert = \varepsilon
}{
  [\,]
  \succ_{r^{*}}
  v:vs
}
\\[1.2em]

\text{(K2)} &
\dfrac{
  \lvert v:vs\rvert \neq \varepsilon
}{
  v:vs
  \succ_{r^{*}}
  [\,]
}.
\end{array}
\end{equation}

Both substitutions replace a premise-free rule with a pair of rules
whose premises read a flattening, which is what makes the ordering
length-sensitive. Rule (A1) prefers the branch consuming more input, and
(A2) breaks an equal-length tie in favor of the left branch, so the left
branch still wins whenever length does not decide. Rule (K2) agrees with
(G2) whenever the iterations consume something, so the two policies
differ on repetition only when a list of iterations flattens to
$\varepsilon$, and there (K1) reverses (G2) by preferring the empty
list. Between them, (A1) and (K1) close every chain of
Section~\ref{sec:infinite-empty-chain}: (K1) settles the comparisons
that (G2) drove, and (A1) settles the one that (G1) drove, since an
empty iteration consumes less than a non-empty one. No restriction on
the admissible parse trees is needed.

The ordering compares only parse trees that flatten to the same word.
Sulzmann and Lu make this explicit in the associated non-strict
relation,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:posix-geq}
v_1 \succeq_r v_2
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
v_1 = v_2
\quad\text{or}\quad
\bigl(v_1 \succ_r v_2
\ \text{ and }\
\lvert v_1\rvert = \lvert v_2\rvert\bigr),
\end{equation}

and call $v_1$ the POSIX parse tree for $r$ when $\vdash v_1 : r$ and
$v_1 \succeq_r v_2$ holds for every $v_2$ with $\vdash v_2 : r$ and
$\lvert v_2\rvert = \lvert v_1\rvert$. The length restriction matters
because $\succ_r$ by itself relates trees with different flattenings:
(A1) compares two branches by length, so it would rank a tree consuming
more input above one consuming less even when the shorter tree is the
only one matching $w$. Equation~\eqref{eq:posix-geq} confines the
comparison to the candidates that are answers to the same parsing
problem.

%

\paragraph{Applied to $r_1$.}
For
$r_1=(a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$ and $w=\mathtt{ab}$, consider

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r1-trees}
v
=
\bigl(\mathit{Right}(a,b),\mathit{Right}()\bigr),
\qquad
v'
=
\bigl(\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ b\bigr).
\end{equation}

By (C2), the comparison reduces to the first components. The right
alternative consumes two characters and the left alternative one, so
(A1) gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r1-choice}
\mathit{Right}(a,b)
\succ_{a+ab}
\mathit{Left}\ a.
\end{equation}

Thus POSIX selects

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r1}
v_1^{P}
=
\bigl(\mathit{Right}(a,b),\mathit{Right}()\bigr),
\end{equation}

the opposite of the Greedy result.

%

\paragraph{Applied to $r_2$.}
For
$r_2=(a+(b+ab))^{*}$ and $w=\mathtt{ab}$, consider

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r2-trees}
v_2 =
[\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}(a,b)],
\qquad
v_2' =
[\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Left}\ b].
\end{equation}

By (K3), the comparison is determined by the first iterations. The
right alternative consumes two characters while the left consumes one,
so (A1) gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r2-choice}
\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}(a,b)
\succ_{a+(b+ab)}
\mathit{Left}\ a.
\end{equation}

Hence POSIX selects the single two-character iteration,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r2}
v_2^{P}
=
[\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}(a,b)],
\end{equation}

whereas Greedy selects two one-character iterations.

%

\paragraph{Applied to $r_3$.}
For $r_3=(a+a)^{*}$ and $w=\mathtt{aa}$, both alternatives consume
exactly one character. Thus (A1) does not apply, and (A2) breaks the
tie in favor of the left alternative:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r3-choice}
\mathit{Left}\ a
\succ_{a+a}
\mathit{Right}\ a.
\end{equation}

Applying (K3)--(K4) to both positions gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r3}
v_3^{P}
=
[\mathit{Left}\ a,\mathit{Left}\ a].
\end{equation}

POSIX therefore agrees with Greedy: the alternatives have equal length,
so the POSIX tie-break coincides with Greedy's left preference.

%

\paragraph{Applied to $r_4$.}
For $r_4=(a^{*})^{*}$ and $w=\mathtt{aa}$, consider

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r4-trees}
[[a,a]]
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
[[a],[a]].
\end{equation}

The first outer iterations are $[a,a]$ and $[a]$, which differ, so (K4)
does not apply and the comparison is made by (K3) between these two
iterations under $\succ_{a^{*}}$. Both are non-empty lists whose first
elements are the literal $a$, so (K3) does not apply at this inner level
either, and (K4) passes the comparison to the tails $[a]$ and
$[\,]$. Since $\lvert [a]\rvert=\mathtt{a}\neq\varepsilon$, rule (K2)
gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r4-inner}
[a]
\succ_{a^{*}}
[\,],
\end{equation}

so (K4) yields $[a,a]\succ_{a^{*}}[a]$, and (K3) lifts this to the outer
star:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r4-choice}
[[a,a]]
\succ_{(a^{*})^{*}}
[[a],[a]].
\end{equation}

Thus POSIX selects

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-r4}
v_4^{P}=[[a,a]],
\end{equation}

the same parse tree as Greedy. The agreement has a different source from
the one on $r_3$. There the two policies coincided because an alternation
tied in length, so (A2) reproduced (G1). Here no alternation rule is
consulted at all: the comparison uses (K2), (K3) and (K4), and the only
step where the two policies could diverge is the appeal to (K2), which
agrees with (G2) because $[a]$ does not flatten to $\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{Applied to the infinite-chain examples.}
The repetition rules also resolve the empty-match chains of
Section~\ref{sec:infinite-empty-chain}. For $\varepsilon^{*}$ against
$\varepsilon$, (K1) gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-empty-star}
[\,]\succ_{\varepsilon^{*}}[()],
\end{equation}

and therefore prefers the empty iteration list to every list of empty
iterations.

For $(\varepsilon+a)^{*}$ against $\mathtt{a}$, (K3) reduces the
comparison between

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-nullable-star}
[\mathit{Right}\ a]
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
[\mathit{Left}\ (),\mathit{Right}\ a]
\end{equation}

to their first iterations. Since $1>0$, (A1) gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-nullable-choice}
\mathit{Right}\ a
\succ_{\varepsilon+a}
\mathit{Left}\ (),
\end{equation}

so the tree without the additional empty iteration is preferred.

Likewise, for $(a^{*})^{*}$ against $\mathtt{aaa}$, (K4) reduces

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-nested-nullable}
[[a,a,a]]
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
[[a,a,a],[\,]]
\end{equation}

to their tails, where (K1) gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvp-posix-nested-choice}
[[a,a,a]]
\succ_{(a^{*})^{*}}
[[a,a,a],[\,]].
\end{equation}

Thus POSIX selects $[[a,a,a]]$ rather than extending the match with
empty outer iterations. Unlike the Greedy ordering, which admits the
infinite chains described in Section~\ref{sec:infinite-empty-chain},
the POSIX rules explicitly settle these empty-match comparisons.

The contrast between the two policies is therefore clear: Greedy gives
structural priority to earlier alternatives and prefers continuing
repetition, whereas POSIX prefers longer leftmost submatches and uses
the structural order to break ties.
