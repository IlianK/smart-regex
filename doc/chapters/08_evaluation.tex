\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates the derivative and partial-derivative parsers
developed in Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} 
and \ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}. 
Using two datasets, 
it compares their execution time, memory consumption, and translation cost, 
as well as their match decisions and parse-tree shapes. 
All measurements were taken on an AMD Ryzen~5~5600 six-core processor
with 16\,GB of RAM, running Pop!\_OS 24.04 LTS.

%
%
%

\section{Candidates}
\label{sec:eval-candidates}

A candidate is a parser $p$ mapping an \emph{entry} $(r,w)$, 
consisting of a translated \texttt{Regex} $r$ 
and an input \texttt{String} $w$, 
to an optional \texttt{ParseTree} $pt$. 
All candidates share the interface:
\[
p :: (\mathit{Regex}, \mathit{String}) \to
     \mathit{Option\langle ParseTree\rangle}
\]
Candidates (Table~\ref{tab:eval-candidates})
are grouped into two families according 
to their disambiguation strategy.
All five parsers are expected to agree on membership.
So given an entry, they must either all accept or reject it.
\emph{Within family} the parsers are also expected to agree on parse-tree shape.
\emph{Across families} parse tree shape agreement is possible, but not required.

\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular*}{\textwidth}{@{\extracolsep{\fill}} llll}
\hline
Family & Candidate & Description & Section \\
\hline

          & \ref{sec:deriv-std-parser} \\
          & \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} & iterative standard derivative parser

Both standard derivative variants are collectively
referred to as \texttt{deriv\_std}.
Similarly, \texttt{deriv} and \texttt{pderiv}
refer to the derivative and partial-derivative
families, respectively.

\section{Evaluation Criteria}
\label{sec:eval-criteria}

The evaluation considers \emph{performance}, 
measured in terms of execution time, memory consumption, and translation cost, 
and \emph{agreement} on membership decisions and parse-tree selection.

%
%
%

\subsection{Measurement Setup}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-setup}

Each entry runs in a separate worker subprocess,
allowing the operating system to terminate it by signal
in cases such as stack overflow or out-of-memory failure.
This avoids the lack of safe thread termination in stable
Rust~\citep{rust-thread-joinhandle} and ensures that
the worker's resources can be reclaimed independently
of the benchmark harness.
Each worker is subject to a $12\,\mathrm{GiB}$ memory cap
and a default timeout of $60\,\mathrm{s}$,
which can be overridden for individual pattern tests.
The harness monitors these limits and terminates workers
that exceed their allotted execution time.
Runs are classified as \emph{measured} if the worker
exits successfully and reports a result,
\emph{timed out} if the timeout is exceeded,
or \emph{crashed} if the worker exits non-zero
or is terminated by a signal.

%

\subsection{Timing}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-timing}

Each entry $(r,w)$ is timed once, without warm-up or repetition.
Translation is measured separately
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-translation}).
Execution is divided into four phases across all five candidates.
Together, the four phases account for the complete
measured execution. Since setup differs between
implementations, total timings provide the common
basis for comparison.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Setup} covers initialization before derivation.
    For \texttt{deriv\_bc}, it measures
    \texttt{internalize}, which converts a \texttt{Regex} into \texttt{ARegex}
    (Section~\ref{sec:internalize}).
    This is necessary because \texttt{deriv\_bc}
    keeps \texttt{Alt} nodes whose branches must
    be tagged beforehand. Later \texttt{fuse}
    operations in \texttt{deriv\_bc} and \texttt{simp}
    only transfer existing bits and are not part
    of this initial encoding.

    In contrast, \texttt{pderiv\_bc} splits
    alternations into separate states, assigning
    branch bits during derivation via \texttt{flatten\_alt}
    (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-impl}).
    Its setup includes the initial clone of $r$
    and the accumulated cost of all \texttt{flatten\_alt}
    calls, although these occur during derivation.
    Otherwise, setup would cover only the initial clone,
    as no separate encoding is needed.

    For \texttt{pderiv\_std}, setup consists only
    of the initial clone, while the
    \texttt{deriv\_std}'s have zero setup cost,
    as they derive $r$ directly without
    initializing a separate state or representation.

    \item \emph{Derive} measures per-character
    derivative steps for all five candidates.

    \item \emph{Reconstruct} produces the final
    \texttt{ParseTree}. The bit-coded parsers
    decode their accumulated bits, while
    \texttt{pderiv\_std} applies composed injection
    closures through \texttt{Inj::apply}.
    Both \texttt{deriv\_std}'s perform
    backward injection, constructing the tree
    directly rather than applying a separately
    composed closure.

    \item \emph{Teardown} measures the cost of
    destroying the returned \texttt{ParseTree}
    and releasing its allocated memory.
    It is timed uniformly by the benchmark harness
    after the parser returns.
\end{itemize}

Four timing statistics are reported.
Aggregation determines whether measurements are combined
per pattern or across individual entries,
with the method depending on the dataset:

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-memory}

Memory is measured with a counting allocator in each worker,
reset immediately before $p(r,w)$ to isolate allocations made during the parser call. 
It tracks requested and live bytes:
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\emph{Max live peak} & Highest live-byte count during the call; peak heap demand. \\
\emph{Total allocated} & Sum of requested bytes over the call, a proxy for allocator pressure. \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Memory metrics collected by the counting allocator.}
\label{tab:eval-memory-metrics}
\end{table}

Total allocated is always at least the peak, 
since it includes memory freed during the call. 
Frequent allocation and deallocation can therefore increase total allocation 
and timing costs even when peak usage remains low.

\subsection{Translation}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-translation}

Translation depends only on the source pattern, 
and is independent of the input,
so each distinct pattern is translated once in the main process. 
The caps: \emph{Bound cap}, \emph{Depth cap} and \emph{Node cap} 
bind translation time and memory by rejecting patterns 
that would require excessive recursion or unrolling. 
Translation cost is measured using two quantities:

\subsection{Stack Depth}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-stack-depth}

This measurement determines the input lengths
at which stack exhaustion occurs, as well as
the translation limits.
Both standard derivative parsers use the default
thread stack ($8\,\mathrm{MiB}$ on Linux),
but consume it differently:
\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} keeps intermediate
derivatives in recursive call frames, whereas
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} stores them in a
heap-allocated \texttt{Vec}.
The probe timeout was reduced to $15\,\mathrm{s}$,
as this is sufficient to identify runs limited by expensive derivation.
These time out, whereas runs that reach the stack limit crash.
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}).
Timeouts are treated as negative controls,
indicating that derivation cost prevents
the stack limit from being reached.

A doubling search is used to determine 
at which input length a stack overflow is reached. 
The search starts from a small input length 
and doubles the input after each successful probe until the worker fails. 
The search brackets the last successful value and the first failure. 
The same method is used to find the two translation caps
\emph{Bound cap} and \emph{Depth cap}.

\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Target} & \textbf{Quantity doubled} & \textbf{Failure condition} \\
\hline
\emph{Parser stack} & Input length & Parser fails to complete \\
\emph{Bound cap} & Repetition bound & Translating the bound exhausts the stack \\
\emph{Depth cap} & Nesting depth of \texttt{(?:...)} groups & Translating the pattern exhausts the stack \\
\end{tabularx}

\subsection{Parse Agreement}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-agreement}

Parse agreement is evaluated on entries $(r,w)$
extended with an expected match result
$e \in \{\mathit{true},\mathit{false}\}$, giving $(r,w,e)$.
Within a family, the parsers are alternative implementations of the
same disambiguation policy and agree with each other by construction;
the comparison therefore uses one representative per family,
\texttt{deriv\_bc} for POSIX and \texttt{pderiv\_bc} for non-POSIX,
to check the one place the two policies can legitimately diverge.
\emph{Membership} is \texttt{deriv\_bc} and \texttt{pderiv\_bc} each
returning a tree exactly when $e = \mathit{true}$.
The two disagreeing with \emph{each other} on membership would
indicate a bug, since whether a string matches at all does not
depend on which disambiguation policy selects its parse tree.
Both agreeing with each other but against $e$ instead points at the
entry's expected label, not at either parser; this case is reported
separately below rather than folded into either outcome.
\emph{Shape} is compared only once membership is settled, i.e.\ when
$e = \mathit{true}$ and both return a tree: since their disambiguation
policies differ, distinct trees there indicate ambiguity rather than
a bug.
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\emph{Timeout or crash} & The candidate exceeded the timeout or was killed by a signal. \\
\emph{Membership} & Match ($e = \mathit{true}$) or no-match ($e = \mathit{false}$) decision. \\
\emph{Parse tree} & The parse tree returned, when $e = \mathit{true}$. \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Results recorded for each candidate during parse-agreement testing.}
\label{tab:eval-agreement-fields}

\subsection{S2ELab}
\label{sec:eval-s2elab}

The dataset by \citet{s2elab-regexeval-dataset} 
contains 762 human-authored patterns. 
Each pattern has a natural-language description 
and lists of labeled matching and non-matching inputs. 
The number of each varies per pattern, 
so the corpus is not balanced between the two.
The source patterns conventionally use \texttt{\textasciicircum} 
and \texttt{\textdollar} as beginning- and end-of-input anchors. 
A pattern surrounded by anchors requires a full-string match, 
where a pattern without them is intended for substring matching. 
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:surface-rejected},
substring matching is not supported and anchors are rejected constructs.
However, fully anchored patterns can still be used
by stripping their anchors.
Since the candidates perform full-string matching,
this preserves the original pattern's matching semantics.
So the benchmarks only use the 463 accepted patterns after anchor stripping.
Figure~\ref{fig:s2elab-pipeline} shows this 
as part of the data preparation, that turns one example pattern into an $entry$.

\subsection{Synthetic Stress Set}
\label{sec:eval-pathological}

The synthetic set consists of 112 patterns generated
with the assistance of Mistral AI and manually verified.
It is designed to stress the parsers and examine
how their costs scale with input length and pattern size.
All patterns are intended for full-string matching.
Each row defines a base pattern and input
parameterized by input length $n$, pattern size $p$, or both.
A \emph{sweep} varies one parameter over a range of values,
recording a measurement at each step.
Rows with IDs beginning with \texttt{P} fix the pattern
and increase $n$ to measure scaling with input length,
while \texttt{E} rows fix the input and increase $p$
to measure scaling with pattern size.
Table~\ref{tab:sweep-samples} illustrates one row of each type
at two sweep values, using their matching inputs.

    & $[\,\texttt{a}^{1000}\,]$ \\

The 112 patterns are grouped by the complexity mechanism they test. 
Each category has its own sweep range.
Each swept value contributes two entries, 
one matching and one non-matching,
allowing costs to be measured for both outcomes.
The latter uses a pattern-specific
near-miss formed by adding or removing one boundary character.
Entries are pooled across each category's patterns,
and every entry is run once, without repeated trials. 
Table~\ref{tab:patho-categories}
gives the resulting pattern and entry counts. 
For example, category A has 160 entries from 16 patterns, each swept
over $n \in \{10, 20, 30, 1\,000, 10\,000\}$ at $p=100$ and tested
with both outcomes ($16 \times 5 \times 2 = 160$).

The patterns in these categories were generated using
prompts specifying the intended stress
mechanisms and alphabets, as summarized in

Both parsers keep all $n$ intermediate derivatives
for backward reconstruction, but store them differently.
The recursive form uses one stack frame per character,
whereas the iterative form uses a heap-allocated \texttt{Vec}.
P01 and P30 serve as negative controls, using
a $15\,\mathrm{s}$ timeout to stop expensive derivations
before stack exhaustion can be observed.
P01 times out at $n=16$ due to near-exponential
derivative growth from nested quantifiers,
while P30 times out at $n=64$ due to polynomial
growth from ten adjacent \texttt{(\textbackslash w+)} groups.
In both cases, derivation becomes too expensive
before the stack limit is reached.
E51 isolates stack usage using the simple pattern
\texttt{x} and inputs consisting entirely of \texttt{a}.
The immediate mismatch keeps the residual fixed,
making derivative computation cheap.
The recursive parser crashes at $n=32\,768$,
confirming its stack-depth limitation.
The iterative form avoids this limitation by storing
intermediate derivatives on the heap,
whose memory costs are examined separately.
For subsequent evaluations, only
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} is reported
as \texttt{deriv\_std}, acting as the representative of both.

simplification required by \texttt{deriv\_bc}.

and 38 of 80 in E, due to growing
residual expressions and saving the derivation sequence.

\paragraph{Category C}
tests bounded repetition around quantified groups,

\texttt{pderiv} variants, with a median of
70.76\,ms against 195.34 and 178.74\,ms.

processing at every input character, while
\texttt{deriv\_bc} simplifies a single residual expression.

\paragraph{Category D}
shows the strongest advantage for \texttt{deriv\_bc}.

remaining suffixes differ as the input is consumed.

a single residual expression.

more effective residual simplification
rather than bit-coding itself.

\paragraph{P60 and P61}
Both rows use the pattern \texttt{(a|b|ab)*},
with P60 repeating \texttt{a} and P61 repeating \texttt{ab}.

examined by \citet[Figs.~7(a) and 7(b)]{sulzmannlu2014}.

Figures~\ref{fig:patho-fig7a}

A ratio of $1.0$ denotes the fastest candidate,

\texttt{deriv\_bc} and \texttt{pderiv\_std}

on P61, reflecting the accumulated cost of
\texttt{flatten\_alt} during derivation
rather than initial pattern preparation.

\clearpage
These reveal distinct costs
associated with repeated and changing residual structures.
For \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{internalize}
annotates the pattern as
$[0](a \mathbin{|} b) \oplus [1](a \cdot b)$
under the Star.
The outer alternation identifies the branch
($[0]$ for \texttt{a|b}), the inner alternation
identifies the literal ($[0]a \oplus [1]b$),
and the Star records whether repetition continues
($[0]$) or terminates ($[1]$).

Two operations contribute differently to
the cost of each derivative step.
The cheaper operation applies when
\texttt{a|b} is already nullable.
The nullable-Seq rule
$\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$
(Equation~\eqref{eq:bderiv})
extracts the completed path through
\texttt{mkEpsBC} and attaches it to
the Star continuation $X$ through \texttt{fuse}.
This processes a fixed three-bit annotation.
The more expensive operation derives the
bare Star body, constructing a new \texttt{Seq}
node from its alternatives instead of
reusing the existing residual structure.

On P60, this structural expansion occurs
only for the first \texttt{a}.
Thereafter, \texttt{a|b} is already nullable
at the start of each step, so subsequent
derivatives follow the same residual structure:
the \texttt{b} branch vanishes,
the \texttt{a} branch resolves directly,
and \texttt{fuse} extends the annotations
without rebuilding the Star body.
P60 therefore reaches a steady state after
its initial derivative.

On P61, each \texttt{a}
follows a \texttt{b} that leaves
\texttt{a|b} non-nullable.
Consequently, every \texttt{a} requires
the structural expansion performed only
once on P60.
The following \texttt{b} resolves the
corresponding branch and commits its
two-bit annotation, returning the residual
to a state that requires expansion again
on the next \texttt{a}.
So P61 repeatedly alternates between
expansion and collapse.

The main difference is therefore the frequency
of structural reconstruction, rather than
the length of the accumulated bit annotations.
While P60 has the expensive expansion only
once, P61 repeats it on every other character.
This explains the higher measured derivation
cost of \texttt{deriv\_bc} on P61
(Tables~\ref{tab:patho-phases-p60}
and~\ref{tab:patho-phases-p61}),
despite its shorter accumulated bit lists.

The \texttt{pderiv\_bc} traces show a
related distinction.
On P60, the state set reaches $S_1$
after the first character and remains there.
Each subsequent \texttt{a} produces the
same two-residual structure, with one
continuation discarded as empty.
On P61, however, the state set alternates
between $S_0$ and $S_1$.
Each \texttt{a} expands $S_0$ into $S_1$,
while each \texttt{b} collapses it again.
Unlike P60's elimination of an empty residual,
this collapse produces two independently
derived residuals with the same structure.
The \texttt{nub2} operation must identify
and remove the duplicate, introducing
additional deduplication work on P61.
Despite these differences,
\texttt{pderiv\_std} remains faster than
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} at the largest sampled
lengths of both rows:
28.36\,s versus 33.21\,s on P60,
and 52.26\,s versus 54.26\,s on P61
(Tables~\ref{tab:patho-p60-all-parsers}
and~\ref{tab:patho-p61-all-parsers}).
Thus, bit-coding provides no measured
speed advantage for the partial-derivative
implementation on these inputs.
The relative cost of bit annotations
and injection closures cannot be determined
from wall time alone;

\paragraph{P01 and P09}
Both rows use repeated \texttt{a} inputs but
introduce ambiguity through different constructions.
P01, \texttt{(a+)+}, nests quantified repetitions,
allowing multiple divisions into outer iterations.
P09, \texttt{(a|aa)+}, instead uses overlapping
alternatives under a single repetition.
Both admit multiple decompositions,
but differ in how these occur during derivation.

Figures~\ref{fig:patho-p01}
and~\ref{fig:patho-p09} show the measured
wall times and ratios to the fastest parser
at each sampled input length.
Their behaviour differs substantially.
On P01, \texttt{deriv\_bc} briefly leads
at $n=15$ and $n=20$, but its cost grows
much faster than that of the \texttt{pderiv} variants.
By $n=1\,000$, it is nearly three times slower,
and at $n=50\,000$ its ratio reaches
approximately $156\times$.
It then times out at $n=200\,000$,
while both \texttt{pderiv} variants complete
the sweep through $n=10\,000\,000$.

On P09, \texttt{deriv\_bc} instead maintains
approximately linear scaling and completes
the entire sweep.
It remains roughly $1.4$--$2.4\times$ slower
than the fastest \texttt{pderiv} variant,
without the widening gap observed on P01.
Thus, although both patterns introduce ambiguity,
nested repetition causes a pronounced
scaling disadvantage for \texttt{deriv\_bc}.

The contrasting scaling traces to one operation in \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s
derivative rule for \texttt{Seq}: whenever its left operand is already
nullable, the rule calls \texttt{mk\_eps\_bc}
(Figure~\ref{fig:mkepsbc-rust}) to extract that operand's accumulated
bits, then \texttt{fuse}s (Figure~\ref{fig:fuse-rust},
Section~\ref{sec:fuse}) the result onto the right -- costing time
proportional to the left operand's bit-list length, not a fixed
amount per character. On P01, \texttt{(a+)+} desugars to
\texttt{Seq(a+, (a+)*)}, and once the inner \texttt{a+} matches one
\texttt{a} it stays nullable for the rest of the input, so this rule
fires every remaining character; the list \texttt{mk\_eps\_bc}
materializes grows by one bit per character -- 1 bit at $n=2$, 999 at
$n=1\,000$, 19\,999 at $n=20\,000$, measured directly -- integrating
to the quadratic-looking total in Figure~\ref{fig:patho-p01}:
competitive with the \texttt{pderiv} pair through $n\approx 20$,
nearly $3\times$ slower by $n=1\,000$, roughly $156\times$ by
$n=50\,000$. \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s own expression stays a fixed eight
\texttt{ARegex} nodes throughout; the cost grows in the bits those
nodes carry, not the tree's shape.

On P09, \texttt{(a|aa)+} never settles into that state: the rule
fires only in the first couple of characters, never again, so
\texttt{deriv\_bc} never pays a growing cost here -- every
character's work stays the same size regardless of input consumed so
far, consistent with the flat per-character cost and the roughly
constant $1.4$--$2.4\times$ slowdown relative to the \texttt{pderiv}
pair throughout.

Both \texttt{pderiv} variants handle both patterns without failures
and stay close in performance. Neither assembles one combined
expression: a partial-derivative frontier keeps one state per
residual, deduplicated by hashing (\texttt{nub2}/\texttt{nub\_tree}).
The cost depends not on ambiguity's presence but on whether it
re-triggers a growing bit-list materialization.

\paragraph{E37 and E38}
Unlike the preceding input-length sweeps, E37 and E38 vary pattern
size while the input stays a short, fixed literal -- the pattern's
own first alternative, matched outright, rather than a string whose
length tracks $n$ or $p$. Both are drawn from category J (``large
pattern, short input''), whose mechanism is the per-node cost of a
very large pattern, and use $p$-way alternations to examine how the
five parsers' timing phases (Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-timing})
scale with pattern size on matching inputs. Alternation was chosen to
grow a pattern's node count by a single parameter $p$ while leaving
the input unchanged.
E37 consists of distinct four-letter alternatives, matched by the
literal \texttt{"aaaa"}
(Figure~\ref{fig:patho-e37},
Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e37}),
while E38 uses numbered placeholder words, matched by the literal
\texttt{"w000001"}
(Figure~\ref{fig:patho-e38},
Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e38}).
Both sweep $p \in \{100, 500, 1\,000,
2\,000, 5\,000, 10\,000\}$.
The node cap was raised from 20\,000 to
200\,000 (Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-translation})
to accommodate these large patterns.
At $p = 10\,000$, E38's seven-character words each translate to 13 nodes
(seven literals chained by six concatenations), and joining all
$10\,000$ of them adds $9\,999$ more nodes for the alternation itself:
$10\,000 \times 13 + 9\,999 = 139\,999$ nodes in total.

tagging pass.
\texttt{Reconstruct} stays under $2$\,ms for every parser but
\texttt{deriv\_std}, whose backward injection pass over
an $O(p)$-long derivation sequence grows to $161.73$\,ms by
$p=10\,000$ -- the single largest contributor to its own
\texttt{Total} at that point.
\texttt{Derive} dominates \texttt{Total} for the three
non-recursive bit-coded/partial-derivative parsers, and at $p=10\,000$
the ratio of \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s \texttt{Derive} to \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s
is about $2.5\times$: both parsers' branch deduplication is $O(p)$
(\texttt{deriv\_bc}'s own \texttt{dedup\_first}, discussed below,
hashes each branch's shape once, the same way \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s
\texttt{nub2} hashes each residual), so the gap between them reflects
a difference in constant factor, not in growth rate.

\texttt{Setup} and \texttt{Derive} are both higher here than on E37 for
the bit-coded pair, consistent with E38's longer words, and
\texttt{Reconstruct} again stays under $2$\,ms for every parser but
\texttt{deriv\_std}, whose own backward pass reaches
$258.29$\,ms. The ratio of \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s \texttt{Derive} to
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s is wider here than on E37, at about $2.1\times$.
\texttt{Setup} remains cheaper for \texttt{pderiv\_bc} than
\texttt{deriv\_bc}, as on E37.
On \texttt{Total}, the partial-derivative pair is cheapest,
consistent with being fastest almost everywhere
in the dataset (Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-pathological}).
\texttt{deriv\_bc} finishes fastest of the three non-standard parsers
at this point ($94.46$\,ms against $332.00$\,ms for
\texttt{deriv\_std}).

\textbf{Convergence.}
At $p=10\,000$, \texttt{deriv\_bc} is roughly $3.5\times$
faster than \texttt{deriv\_std}
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e38}).
Unlike \texttt{deriv\_std}, which never attempts to simplify the alternation
and so never pays for an attempt that could not have succeeded
(every one of the $p$ alternatives is genuinely distinct by construction),
\texttt{deriv\_bc} does attempt to, but now hashes each branch's shape
once rather than comparing every pair,
so the attempt itself no longer dominates its per-character cost at this scale.
The partial-derivative pair is cheapest at this point, between
roughly $1.6\times$ and $2.0\times$ faster than \texttt{deriv\_bc},
since neither ever holds the $p$ alternatives
as one combined expression to flatten and simplify in the first place --
a narrower gap than on \texttt{deriv\_std}, now that \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s
own deduplication is $O(p)$ rather than $O(p^2)$.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} still pays more on E38 than on E37 at every $p$.
\texttt{hash\_shape} (Figure~\ref{fig:hash-shape-ch5-rust},
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}) walks a word's
whole chain of \texttt{Seq} nodes to hash it, one recursive call per
character, so its cost tracks word length regardless of whether that
word shares a prefix with any other.
E38's numbered words are longer than E37's fixed four-letter ones,
so hashing each one costs more.

\texttt{dedup\_first} (Figure~\ref{fig:dedup-first-ch5-rust},
Section~\ref{sec:simp}) hashes each of the $p$ branches once
with \texttt{hash\_shape}, which
mirrors \texttt{same\_shape}'s own recursion arm for arm, so two
branches of the same shape always land in the same bucket; a hash
collision between differently shaped branches costs one extra,
correctly negative, \texttt{same\_shape} call within that bucket,
never a wrong merge, since bucket membership is only ever a pre-filter
for the real equality test. For $p$ genuinely distinct words, each
one's own characters make its hash distinct from the others, so
buckets stay singletons and \texttt{same\_shape} is almost never
called at all; the cost is $O(p)$ hashing, not an $O(p^2)$ comparison
paid for zero branches removed.
This implements the \texttt{nub} call 
in the choice case of
\citet[sect.~3, Fig.~6]{sulzmannlu2014}'s simplification.
\[
simp\ (bs@{\oplus}(ri : rsi)) = bs@{\oplus}\bigl(\mathit{nub}\
(\mathit{filter}\ (\neg\,\mathit{isPhi})\ ((simp\ ri) : \mathit{map}\
simp\ rsi))\bigr),
\]
Haskell's own \texttt{nub} is quadratic, 
since with only an \texttt{Eq} instance 
it keeps the first occurrence of each element 
by comparing it against every element already kept;
\texttt{dedup\_first} avoids that cost by hashing first and only
falling back to a pairwise check within one bucket.
\texttt{same\_shape} is the same equality test \texttt{nub} would use, 
made insensitive to bit-code history, 
so identical branches merge regardless of parse path.

The partial-derivative pair deduplicates via a related but distinct
mechanism: \texttt{nub2} and \texttt{nub\_tree} are also hash-based
and $O(p)$, but hash the whole residual directly rather than a
structural shape, differing only in what carries along with each one
(a bit vector in \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, an injection closure in
\texttt{pderiv\_std}). Since the mechanism is otherwise the same,
only \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s is shown, alongside \texttt{step\_frontier}
which calls it (Figure~\ref{fig:step-frontier-bc-rust},
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-state-set-impl}).

Before the alternation splits into separate residuals, the first call
must flatten the whole $p$-deep nested \texttt{Alt} into its $p$ leaves
(Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-impl}), each tagged with its own bit prefix,
and deduplicate them once via \texttt{nub2}. Measured directly, nearly
all of \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s cost on a wide alternation sits in this
first call. \texttt{step\_frontier}'s loop then calls \texttt{pderiv\_bc}
and \texttt{nub2} once per character, linear in the live residuals, and
E38's longer shared prefix repeats this cost more often than E37.
\texttt{deriv\_std} pays neither cost, making no cross-branch or
per-residual comparison at all.

\texttt{deriv\_bc} deduplicates its own branches the same way in
spirit, keyed on shape (Figure~\ref{fig:hash-shape-ch5-rust}) rather than
on the whole residual the way \texttt{nub2} and \texttt{nub\_tree} do,
since a combined annotated expression has branches to flatten and
deduplicate in the first place, where a partial-derivative frontier
already keeps one state per surviving residual.
Either way the dedup itself is $O(p)$, not $O(p^2)$, without changing
what it computes: a hash collision only ever costs one extra,
correctly negative, \texttt{same\_shape} call, never a wrong merge.

in close proportion to $p$ over the sweep -- exactly doubling
each time $p$ doubles in Table~\ref{tab:patho-mem-e38}, and
\texttt{deriv\_bc} does the same, with \texttt{deriv\_std}
close behind at just under a $2\times$ doubling -- well
short of the quadratic growth either worst case allows. This is
consistent with the flattened structure's sharing most of each
leaf's encoding with its neighbors rather than each leaf holding an
independent copy, though profiling would be needed to establish the
exact contribution. \texttt{pderiv\_std} has similar timing to
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}, and both are much faster than the other
three parsers (Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-representative}).
The trade-off is inherent in the representations, but at this
width it is modest: \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s own peak over
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s stays under $2.2\times$ throughout the sweep.

\section{Findings}
\label{sec:eval-discussion}

\subsection{Cost of the standard construction}

\texttt{deriv\_std}, the standard construction's representative
outside the dedicated stack-depth probe
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-timing}), is the most prone to
crashing and, on the entries that cost it the most, by far the
slowest and most memory-hungry of the four; on typical small entries
this is not the case, as the next section details. Its two-pass
structure retains every intermediate expression until the backward
pass completes. On \texttt{a*} input, this state grows linearly with
$n$, while the other three variants remain bounded over the measured
range.

\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} tracks \texttt{deriv\_std} closely
everywhere outside the stack-depth probe itself
(Sections~\ref{sec:eval-timing-s2elab}, \ref{sec:eval-timing-representative}):
under the heap-capped setting used there, both reach their memory
cap at the same inputs and report almost identical times, since
both are limited by retained derivation state rather than by
recursion depth.

The stack-depth test isolates that other failure mode directly:
\texttt{deriv\_std} survives roughly $26\times$ longer in wall
time there than \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} before the doubling search
reaches its own per-probe cap (Section~\ref{sec:eval-stack-depth}).
The limiting factor thus
depends on whether derivation cost or stack depth grows first, and
which of the two forms is used changes only which of those two
failure modes is reached, not whether the underlying growth happens
at all.

%
%
%

\subsection{Cost of bit-coding}
\label{sec:eval-cost-bit-coding}

Within the standard-derivative family, bit-coding's advantage on
the measured corpus is not in typical-case speed: on median,
\texttt{deriv\_std} is itself faster than \texttt{deriv\_bc}
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-s2elab}), since most corpus entries
are small enough that \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s own bookkeeping costs
more than it saves. The advantage shows up instead in peak memory
and in robustness. \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s peak allocation is
4.9\,MB, about $1\,906\times$ lower than \texttt{deriv\_std}'s
peak (9\,282\,MB). This comparison is not population-stable on
\texttt{deriv\_std}'s side: \texttt{deriv\_bc} never crashes or times
out on this corpus, so every one of its $11\,395$ entries
contributes to its own peak, while \texttt{deriv\_std}'s measured
population is bounded by the same $60$-second per-entry timeout
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}) and so depends on where that
boundary falls, crashing instead on a small fraction of entries
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-s2elab}).
Its forward pass retains the information needed for
single-pass reconstruction, avoiding repeated simplification
of redundant branches. Reconstruct adds little time, staying
under 2\,ms even at $p=10\,000$ alternatives. Setup depends
only on pattern size, reaching 10.45\,ms at $p=10\,000$
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e37}).
Already at the narrowest sampled widths \texttt{deriv\_bc} tracks
\texttt{deriv\_std}'s own cost closely, and from a couple of thousand
alternatives on it overtakes it rather than losing ground, as
discussed below.

The representative sweeps reinforce the robustness result.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} completes three of the four rows (P60, P61,
and P09), reaching ten million characters on each, while
\texttt{deriv\_std} crashes on all four. Its cumulative allocation
can nevertheless be high, reaching 52.43\,GB on P60 despite
a peak of 2.16\,GB. This illustrates why both cumulative
allocation and peak live bytes are reported
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-memory}).

%
%
%

\subsection{Cost of partial derivatives}
\label{sec:eval-cost-partial-derivatives}

The partial-derivative parsers are the fastest and use the
least memory on the S2ELab corpus. Representing the residual
as a set of states rather than one combined expression limits
the alternation growth seen with standard derivatives.

This difference is sharpest on wide alternations, where it also
separates \texttt{pderiv\_bc} from \texttt{deriv\_bc}, not only from
\texttt{deriv\_std}. \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s simplification pass removes
duplicate branches of a combined \texttt{Alt} expression with
\texttt{dedup\_first} (Section~\ref{sec:simp-impl}), which compares
every pair of flattened branches and costs $O(p^2)$ for $p$
alternatives. \texttt{pderiv\_bc} deduplicates its own flattened
frontier with \texttt{nub2} (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-impl}), a
hash-set membership test costing $O(p)$ for the same $p$ branches.
Both parsers flatten nested alternations into one list before
deduplicating it, avoiding a cost that would otherwise be repeated
once per nesting level; what still differs is the cost of the single
deduplication pass itself, pairwise for \texttt{deriv\_bc} against
hash-based for \texttt{pderiv\_bc}. Both parsers' derive time grows
with $p$ at a comparable rate: on E38, \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s stays
within a roughly $1.6\times$ to $2.1\times$ multiple of
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s across the whole sweep rather than widening
as $p$ grows, so the pairwise scan's larger constant factor, not a
diverging growth rate, is what separates them at this scale.

Within this pair, bit-coding now gives \texttt{pderiv\_bc} a small
advantage on the measured corpus.
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} has the lower figure on three of the four axes
measured: mean (0.706 against \texttt{pderiv\_std}'s 0.752\,ms),
median (0.109 against 0.114\,ms), and total allocation (5.19
against 5.68\,GB); \texttt{pderiv\_std} keeps the lower peak (2.5
against 3.8\,MB), the one axis where it still leads.
The two parsers implement the same nullable-\texttt{Seq} collapse
differently (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-tree-impl}):
\texttt{pderiv\_std} builds a differently shaped injection closure
whenever the derivative of $r_1$ is \texttt{Eps}, even if no
duplicate residual needs removing, while \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s
version of the same collapse only removes a wrapper. This
structural difference favours \texttt{pderiv\_bc} wherever such
collisions are rare, as they are in the real corpus, and it is
reflected in three of the four measured figures; \texttt{pderiv\_std}'s
lower peak is the one exception, and establishing what costs
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} more there specifically would need separate
profiling.

The representative rows show the corpus ranking does not generalize
in full. At the largest commonly reached input,
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} is slower than \texttt{deriv\_bc} on both
P60 (a ratio of 1.023 at ten million characters) and P61 (1.054,
also at ten million, which both now reach without crashing;
Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-representative}). It is faster on P09
(0.558) and, by a wide margin, on P01 (0.007 at $n=50\,000$, the
largest length \texttt{deriv\_bc} itself reaches there before
timing out). \texttt{deriv\_bc} itself is no longer uniformly ahead
of \texttt{pderiv\_std} either: \texttt{pderiv\_std} is faster on
P60, P09, and (where \texttt{deriv\_bc} has data) P01, but on P61 it
is \texttt{deriv\_bc} that is marginally faster, by about
1.5\,\% (51\,493.84\,ms against 52\,259.70\,ms).
Within the partial-derivative pair itself, \texttt{pderiv\_std} is
the faster of the two on all four representative rows, by a margin
ranging from about 4\,\% (P61) to about 17\,\% (P60) -- the reverse
of its position on the S2ELab corpus, where \texttt{pderiv\_bc} now
leads on mean and median (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cost-bit-coding}).
Which of the two is faster therefore depends on the input: corpus-typical
entries favour \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, these four stress patterns favour
\texttt{pderiv\_std}.

The peak-memory ranking disagrees with the corpus on one row.
\texttt{pderiv\_std} has the lower peak of the partial-derivative
pair on three of the four representative rows, the same parser that
has the lower peak on the corpus: by around 6\,\% on P60, 6\,\% on
P61, and 1\,\% on P09 (Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory-representative}).
On P01 the ranking reverses and \texttt{pderiv\_bc} has the lower
peak instead, by about 18\,\%, even though \texttt{pderiv\_std}
remains the faster of the two there.
Speed and peak memory therefore no longer move together for this
pair on every row: P01 favours \texttt{pderiv\_bc} on memory but
\texttt{pderiv\_std} on time, the only row among these four where the
two axes disagree.

On wide patterns, the two bit-coded parsers differ in which
direction they diverge from the corpus ranking: here it is
\texttt{deriv\_bc}, not \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, that is consistently
slower, on both matching and non-matching input.
For a $p$-way alternation of equal-length words
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e37}), \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s derive time
is roughly $2.5\times$ \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s at $p=10\,000$ on
matching input; non-matching input shows a comparable gap in total
per-call time, $2.9\times$ at $p=10\,000$ (23.41\,ms against
7.98\,ms), though derive alone diverges further there (about
$7.5\times$: 14.02\,ms against 1.88\,ms) -- \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own
\emph{setup} there (6.10\,ms) makes up more of its total than
\texttt{deriv\_bc}'s does (9.39\,ms of 23.41\,ms), narrowing the gap
once setup and derive are combined into the total figure quoted
above. Rejecting
\texttt{"zzzz"} still requires deriving all $p$ alternatives, but
none survive, leaving nothing to deduplicate; \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s
branch deduplication (Figure~\ref{fig:dedup-first-ch5-rust}) accordingly
contributes little extra cost here, yet \texttt{pderiv\_bc} is
still the faster parser on this side too, so deduplication cost
alone does not account for the gap between them.
At this scale, \texttt{deriv\_bc} already tracks \texttt{deriv\_std}
closely at the narrowest sampled widths and overtakes it from a
couple of thousand alternatives on, rather than falling behind
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-representative}),
while \texttt{pderiv\_bc} remains well ahead of all three.
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own \emph{setup} figure is
smaller than \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s on both matching and
non-matching input, but this is
not a fair comparison of tagging cost: \texttt{pderiv\_bc}
builds no annotated state upfront, so its \emph{setup} entry
times only the initial clone plus its own flattening pass, measured
separately from \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s one-time tagging
by \texttt{internalize} though serving the same role
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-timing}).
The two bit-coded parsers' \emph{setup} figures are, on that basis,
already a fair comparison of tagging cost at this row: both grow
with $p$, \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s consistently the smaller of the two
(5.51 against \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s 10.45\,ms at $p=10\,000$).

Memory does not reflect this same cost difference. On the
widest swept alternations (Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory-representative}),
\texttt{pderiv\_std}'s own peak grows to 12.91\,MB on E38 and
7.63\,MB on E37 at $p=10\,000$, close to \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own
10.37 and 6.05\,MB there and well below \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s 21.57
and 12.93\,MB; all four parsers' peaks grow roughly in proportion
to $p$ over this sweep rather than diverging, including the two
whose per-leaf representation is sized by the leaf's own position
in the worst case
(\texttt{pderiv\_std}'s wrapped injection closures,
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-tree-impl}, and \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own
bit-prefixes, Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc}). Measured peak memory
is consistent with much of each leaf's representation being shared
with its neighbors rather than held as an independent copy, though
profiling would be needed to confirm this. The partial-derivative
pair's advantage in peak memory over the standard-derivative family
is, on this evidence, not conditional on the pattern's alternation
width staying bounded.

Both partial-derivative variants produce non-POSIX trees.
This reflects a difference in parsing policy, not a bug,
and motivates comparing the two parser families separately
in Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement}.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\textbf{The synthetic set.} Its 112 rows isolate individual
complexity mechanisms at controlled scales rather than
representing typical user patterns. Most categories vary
one parameter at a time, so combinations of stress
mechanisms remain untested.

\textbf{Agreement subset.} The \emph{Compared} subset
contains expected matches where both candidates completed
and agreed with the expected label. It excludes
0 of 5\,869 S2ELab matches and 20 of 443 synthetic matches
(about 4.5\%, 15 to timeouts and 5 to the category-C entries
that disagree with the expected label, discussed above), so
exclusion is no longer the
large share of either dataset it once was. What remains of it
on the synthetic set is concentrated in four categories --
F, I, J, and K lose entries to timeouts (12, 4, 2, and 10
respectively, out of each category's own total), the other
seven lose none. In
alternation-heavy categories, excluded cases may also
be more ambiguous, since shared prefixes can increase
both derivation cost and the number of possible parses.
This need not apply to categories such as I, where
timeouts arise from input length alone.

\textbf{Measurement model.} Memory measurements cover
heap allocations, not total process memory. Timing depends
on the test machine and may vary with CPU and cache.
A recorded \emph{crash} means the process was killed by
a signal, not that stack overflow was confirmed
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}). The separate
stack-depth test removes the memory cap and provides
direct evidence of stack overflow. Crashes in the
representative rows are instead consistent with the
memory cost of retaining intermediate derivatives.
