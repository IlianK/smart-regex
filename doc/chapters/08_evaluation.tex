\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates the four parser variants implemented in
Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}. The four candidates are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the standard derivative-based parser (\texttt{deriv\_std},
        direct and loop variants),
  \item the bit-coded derivative-based parser (\texttt{deriv\_bc}),
  \item the standard partial-derivative parser (\texttt{pderiv\_std}),
  \item the bit-coded partial-derivative parser (\texttt{pderiv\_bc}).
\end{itemize}

Three aspects are covered. 
Section~\ref{sec:eval-dataset} describes
how the corpus is prepared and how the verified inputs are generated
from real patterns. 
Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement} then checks, on the prepared corpus,
how often the POSIX parsers and the non-POSIX parsers agree on the
shape of the parse tree they build for the same pattern and input.
The main focus of the chapter, however, is
Section~\ref{sec:eval-performance}, which compares the four variants
on execution time and memory consumption, on both synthetic inputs and
the prepared corpus.

The chapter is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-dataset} describes the corpus and the pipeline
that produces the benchmark inputs.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement} verifies parser agreement.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-performance} presents timing and memory results.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-discussion} interprets them.

All measurements are produced by binaries under \texttt{benches/} and
\texttt{examples/}, run with a fixed \texttt{--release} profile.

\section{Dataset Preparation}
\label{sec:eval-dataset}

\subsection{Source selection}

Two rule corpora are used: Snort/Suricata and SpamAssassin. Both are
maintained upstream, their syntax is documented by the projects that
publish them, and their patterns are exercised in production. This makes
the corpus a reasonable proxy for the patterns a real parser will
encounter.

The corpus is the union of every rule file fetched as described in
\texttt{DATASET.md}: 47 Snort rule files under
\texttt{data/raw/snort}, totalling 16{,}774 extracted patterns, and 62
SpamAssassin rule files under \texttt{data/raw/spamAssassin}, totalling
549 extracted patterns.

The two sources differ in character, and this difference matters for the
rest of the chapter.

Snort signatures are byte-oriented and often written to defeat
obfuscation. A typical pattern mixes hexadecimal escapes, alternation
over syntactic variants, and a case-insensitive flag:

\begin{verbatim}
/Authorization\x3a\s*Basic\s*[a-zA-Z0-9]{255,}==/i
/eval\x28(String\x2EfromCharCode\x28
      |[a-z,0-9]{1,20}\x28String\x2EfromCharCode\x28)/i
\end{verbatim}

The first matches HTTP \texttt{Authorization: Basic} headers with an
unusually long base64 payload; the second matches two spellings of the
JavaScript \texttt{eval(String.fromCharCode(...))} idiom. Both are long
enough that a derivative parser's state grows visibly across the match.

SpamAssassin rules are keyword-oriented and much shorter. A typical
pattern is a single word with optional separators, plus a case-insensitive
flag:

\begin{verbatim}
/xan[ae]x/i
/l.{0,2}e.{0,2}v.{0,2}i.{0,2}t.{0,2}r.{0,2}a/i
/boundary="[\da-f]{8}(?:-[\da-f]{4}){3}-[\da-f]{12}"/
\end{verbatim}

The first matches the drug name \emph{Xanax}; the second matches the word
\emph{levitra} with up to two arbitrary characters between letters, a
common spammer evasion; the third validates a MIME boundary UUID. Their
short length is why the SpamAssassin corpus produces smaller but more
numerous per-pattern measurements.

\subsection{Extraction}

Extraction is the first stage of the pipeline. Each source file is read
line by line, and patterns are pulled out as described in
\texttt{src/data/extract.rs}: for Snort, every \texttt{pcre:"..."}
option paired with its \texttt{content:} fields, \texttt{msg}, and
\texttt{sid}; for SpamAssassin, every \texttt{header} and \texttt{body}
rule.

The extraction was run with:

\begin{verbatim}
cargo run --release --example dataset_prepare -- \
    suricata data/raw/snort/*.rules
cargo run --release --example dataset_prepare -- \
    spamassassin data/raw/spamAssassin/*.cf
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Input generation}
\label{sec:eval-input-generation}

Every accepted pattern needs inputs before it can be benchmarked: at
least $N/2$ matching and $N/2$ non-matching, where $N$ is the
\texttt{--inputs} target. The five sources differ in whether they
supply such inputs themselves, and if so, in what form.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Source & Given input? & Generation strategy \\
\hline
Suricata     & no                    & structural + content-derived \\
SpamAssassin & no                    & structural + corpus sample \\
RegexLib     & no                    & structural \\
S2ELab       & yes (labeled)         & given, with structural fill \\
Innovatorved & yes (unlabeled)       & given, with structural fill \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Source of candidate inputs per dataset.}
\label{tab:input-generation}
\end{table}

%

\paragraph{Suricata.}
A Snort rule is a conjunction of constraints; nothing in it is a
complete candidate string. The extractor takes the first
\texttt{pcre:"..."} option on the rule line and ignores any subsequent
one, so a rule that carries two distinct patterns contributes one to
the corpus, not two. The \texttt{content:} fields on the same line are
not input: they are fragments a triggering payload must contain, with
positional constraints (\texttt{distance}, \texttt{within},
\texttt{depth}, \texttt{offset}) that the pipeline parses but does not
enforce. Generation runs two strategies in parallel.
\texttt{generate\_sample} walks the translated \texttt{Regex} and
produces structural positives and negatives.
\texttt{generate\_from\_content\_fields} concatenates the extracted
\texttt{content:} bytes with a short filler between fields, producing
strings shaped like real payloads. Both feed the same verification
pass; only candidates that match the translated pattern (or fail late,
for negatives) survive. A Snort rule with \texttt{pcre:} but no
\texttt{content:} generates candidates only via the structural sampler;
a rule with \texttt{content:} but no \texttt{pcre:} is skipped entirely
by the extractor, since there is no pattern to translate.

%

\paragraph{SpamAssassin.}
Each \texttt{header} or \texttt{body} rule carries one pattern, and no
test messages are shipped with the rules. Generation is structural plus,
when a local ham/spam corpus is configured through \texttt{--corpus-dir},
a scan of that directory for messages whose entire content matches the
pattern. Under full-string matching the corpus scan finds little against
a real corpus, since most real messages are not themselves exact matches
of a short pattern; the structural sampler is what supplies the bulk of
the surviving inputs.

%

\paragraph{RegexLib.}
Structural generation only. No side input is available from the source.

%

\paragraph{S2ELab.}
Each entry ships with author-labeled \texttt{matches} and
\texttt{non\_matches}. Generation prefers these: they are wrapped as
candidates unchanged, deduplicated across both sides, and passed to
verification. If after verification either side has fewer than $N/2$
cases, structural sampling fills the gap, so a pattern with fewer given
examples than the target is still usable provided its own language
admits additional draws. A pattern whose given lists are empty on one
side \emph{and} whose language is too small to sample from cannot reach
the target and is excluded.

%

\paragraph{Innovatorved.}
Each entry ships with free-form test text -- a mix of matching and
non-matching lines with no marker saying which is which. Generation
prefers this text: it is split into lines, deduplicated, sampled down to
a cap, and each surviving line is classified against the real parser
(matching or not) before being handed to verification as any other
candidate. If after verification either side has fewer than $N/2$ cases,
structural sampling fills the gap, as for S2ELab. A pattern with no test
text, or with test text that yields too few surviving cases on one side
and a language too small to sample from, is excluded.

%

\paragraph{Common rule.}
In every case, candidates that do not match their claimed outcome under
the real parser are discarded, and negatives must additionally fail
\emph{late} -- past two thirds of the input -- to count as meaningful.
The balanced target of exactly $N/2$ matches and $N/2$ non-matches per
pattern, described in Section~\ref{sec:eval-worked-pipeline}, applies to
all five sources: whether an input comes from the source itself or from
structural fill, the balanced corpus carries the same count per pattern
and the same split.

\subsection{A worked example through the pipeline}
\label{sec:eval-worked-pipeline}

Before the aggregate counts, one pattern is traced from raw text to
prepared case, so that the later tables have a concrete referent.

Consider the Snort rule:

\begin{verbatim}
alert http $EXTERNAL_NET $HTTP_PORTS -> $HOME_NET any (
  msg:"ET WEB_CLIENT Possible IE URI Validation";
  content:"#|3A|../../"; content:"C|3A 5C|";
  nocase; within:50;
  pcre:"/\x2E\x2E\x2F\x2E\x2E\x2F.+C\x3A\x5C[a-z]/si";
  sid:2010798; rev:4;)
\end{verbatim}

\paragraph{Extract.} The extractor scans the line for \texttt{pcre:"...}
and \texttt{content:"..."}. It produces one \texttt{ExtractedRule} whose
\texttt{raw\_pattern} is \texttt{/\textbackslash x2E\textbackslash
x2E\textbackslash x2F.../si}, and whose source-specific context records
two \texttt{content:} fields (\texttt{\#:../../} and \texttt{C:\textbackslash}),
a \texttt{msg}, and a \texttt{sid}.

\paragraph{Translate.} \texttt{core\_regex\_tagged} strips the
\texttt{/.../si} wrapper, applies \texttt{case\_fold} for the \texttt{i}
flag, leaves \texttt{.} as the full alphabet since the \texttt{s} flag is
set, and translates the remaining pattern to a \texttt{Regex}. The result
carries \texttt{MatchIntent::UnanchoredAmbiguous}, since the pattern has
no top-level \texttt{\textasciicircum...\textdollar} wrap.

\paragraph{Generate.} Because the rule is Snort and the extractor
recorded two \texttt{content:} fields, generation runs in two ways. The
structural sampler walks the translated \texttt{Regex} and produces
strings from the pattern's own language. The content-derived sampler
joins the \texttt{content:} bytes with a short filler between them, and
produces strings that look like real HTTP requests:

\begin{verbatim}
#:../../C:\
#:../../xxC:\a
#:../../abC:\z
\end{verbatim}

The filler between the two fields is drawn from the pattern's own
language, so the generated string stays compatible with the pattern's
match constraint.

\paragraph{Verify.} Each candidate is checked against the real parser.
Structural positives and content-derived candidates are asserted to
match; structural negatives (produced by corrupting the last character of
a positive) are asserted not to match, and additionally asserted to fail
\emph{late} --- after at least two-thirds of the input. Candidates that
fail verification are discarded. Surviving candidates are written to
\texttt{data/processed/snort/prepared.jsonl} as one JSON line per case.

This worked example is the pattern the rest of the chapter refers to when
it discusses \emph{generated} versus \emph{verified} candidates.


Input generation is bounded by two distinct limits. The first is
sampling effort: a rich language such as \texttt{[a-z]+} admits
infinitely many matching strings, but the sampler draws them randomly
and can spend many attempts producing duplicates before reaching the
target count. This limit is practical, not fundamental, and is
addressed by retrying with a larger attempt budget. The second is the
language's own size. A pattern such as \texttt{a\{3\}} denotes exactly
one string, and no amount of sampling can produce ten distinct matching
inputs from it. This limit is fundamental: the pattern's language is
smaller than the target count, and it cannot be met by any generation
strategy. Patterns that fall into the second category are recorded as
unusable at generation and are not written to \texttt{prepared.jsonl},
since admitting a pattern with fewer than the standard number of inputs
would make its benchmark results incomparable to the rest of the
corpus.

\begin{table}[ht]

\hline

\end{tabular}
\caption{Rejection causes across both sources.}
\label{tab:corpus-rejections}
\end{table}

Four causes dominate. \emph{Anchor} (6406 patterns) is the largest. It
includes every pattern whose \texttt{\textasciicircum} or
\texttt{\textdollar} is not part of a top-level
\texttt{\textasciicircum...\textdollar} wrap: a lone start or end anchor,
or one nested inside an alternation branch or under a quantifier. The
frontend rejects these deliberately (Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}), since
removing them would change what the pattern denotes. Two concrete
examples:

\begin{verbatim}
/\.ida$/iU                 (Snort, end anchor only)
/^Argument\s+\//smi        (Snort, start anchor plus m flag)
\end{verbatim}

\emph{Multiline flag} (3982 patterns) is second. A Snort pattern ending
in \texttt{/smi} or similar carries the \texttt{m} flag, which is
rejected because anchors and newline-sensitive
\texttt{\textasciicircum}/\texttt{\textdollar} have no meaning in a
full-string matcher.

\emph{Backreference} (223 patterns) and \emph{lookaround} (148 patterns)
are both small relative to the corpus size, but they are the two most
interesting causes from a theoretical standpoint: they are the constructs
that place a pattern outside the regular languages. Example patterns:

\begin{verbatim}
/^(?P<addr1>.{4})(?P=addr2)(?P=addr1)/Rsi
/(?=(?:(0x[a-f0-9]{2}|0+\d{1,3})\s*?,?...))
\end{verbatim}

The first is a Snort rule with a named backreference; the second is a
Snort rule with a nested lookahead. Both are rejected before an
\texttt{ExtPat} exists, so no tree is available to recover.

The \emph{other} column collects patterns that fail for reasons not
covered by the six named causes. For Snort, this is almost entirely the
\texttt{MAX\_BOUND} cap of 1000 repetitions, triggered by patterns like
\texttt{/\{GET\}.{}/\%.\{1586,\}/}. For SpamAssassin, the same cap and a
handful of syntax errors.

These are the entries that need the parse step's own rejection mechanism,
since no tree exists to strip anchors from.

\subsection{Prepared cases}

The pipeline writes accepted cases to
\texttt{data/processed/\textless source\textgreater/prepared.jsonl}. Each
line is one verified (pattern, input, outcome) triple. The corpus
contains 6{,}285 accepted patterns, 87{,}283 generated candidates, and
61{,}712 verified candidates.

The gap between generated and verified is worth explaining, since it
appears in every benchmark that follows. Candidate generation produces
two kinds of input: positives, which \emph{claim} to match, and
negatives, which claim not to. Verification discards any candidate whose
claim does not hold under the real parser, and additionally discards any
negative that fails \emph{early} --- before two-thirds of the input ---
because an early failure is not a meaningful negative. For Snort,
85{,}997 candidates were generated and 60{,}426 survived (70\% survival);
for SpamAssassin, 1{,}286 generated and 1{,}286 survived (100\%). The
difference is that SpamAssassin's short, regular patterns generate
cleanly, while Snort's longer patterns occasionally produce a structural
sample that the case-folding or anchor-stripping steps have made
unmatchable.

A small fraction of accepted patterns contributes nothing to the
corpus. These are not sampler failures. Every non-matching input the
pipeline produces is a matching input with its last character replaced
by a character that lies outside the pattern's working alphabet, so a
pattern whose language covers the alphabet (any star expression, any
class-based pattern) still has a valid negative: the corrupted string
contains a character no member of its language could contain. What
remains are the patterns whose language is on one side empty: an
expression that translates to the empty-string language, such as
\texttt{\textasciicircum\textdollar} after anchor stripping or
\texttt{a\{0\}}, has no negative to draw; an expression that translates
to the empty language has no positive to draw. In both cases, one side
has zero members, and the pipeline excludes the pattern rather than
fabricating a member that would not be a real test. The count appears
as \emph{patterns below target} in the pipeline report and accounts for
the difference between \emph{accepted} and \emph{usable} patterns in
Table~\ref{tab:corpus-coverage}.

\section{Parser Agreement}
\label{sec:eval-agreement}

Every case in the prepared corpus already carries a verified outcome,
so membership is a precondition rather than something this section
establishes. The question here is the shape of the parse tree. On a
matching case, the POSIX parsers and the non-POSIX parsers may build
different trees --- the same input can admit more than one valid
decomposition --- but some patterns and inputs leave neither policy a
choice, forcing both onto the same structure. This section measures
how often that happens across the corpus.

%
%
%

\subsection{Method}

The check is implemented in \texttt{demo\_parser\_agreement}. For every
prepared case, all five parsers --- \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec},
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}, \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{pderiv\_std},
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} --- run on the same (pattern, input) pair.

Two conditions are checked as preconditions. First, every parser
returns the case's verified membership outcome. Second, within each
family the parsers agree with each other on the tree: the three POSIX
variants build the same tree, and the two non-POSIX variants build the
same tree. A violation of either condition is a bug, not a
measurement.

The measured quantity is cross-family tree agreement: of the cases
where both families matched and were internally consistent, how many
produced the same tree. The two families apply different
disambiguation policies, so a difference is expected where the input
admits several decompositions; a match where the input admits only one
forces both families onto the same structure.

Each parser call runs in a subprocess so that a stack overflow or an
infinite loop is contained. A 2-second wall-clock timeout kills the
process. Timeouts and crashes are counted separately from tree-shape
outcomes, since neither produces a result to compare.

\begin{verbatim}
cargo build --release --example demo_parser_agreement_worker
cargo run --release --example demo_parser_agreement
\end{verbatim}

\section{Performance Analysis}
\label{sec:eval-performance}

The four parsers are compared on two axes: execution time
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing}) and memory consumption
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory}). Both axes are measured on synthetic
inputs first, since synthetic families isolate a single cost, and on the
corpus second, since the corpus is the environment the parsers will
encounter in practice.

%
%
%

\subsection{Timing}
\label{sec:eval-timing}

\subsubsection{Synthetic benchmarks}

\texttt{bench\_parse} runs the five parsers on four families of
hand-constructed inputs. Each family targets one cost.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{small\_patterns}: fixed per-call overhead on four fixed
        cases (a literal, a star against empty input, against one
        character, against three).
  \item \texttt{scaling\_a\_star}: how cost grows with input length for
        \texttt{a*}.
  \item \texttt{deep\_expression}: how cost grows with expression size
        for a flat chain \texttt{a·a·...·a}.
  \item \texttt{ambiguous\_star\_a\_or\_aa}: how cost grows when the
        expression is genuinely ambiguous, using \texttt{(a|aa)*}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{verbatim}
cargo bench --bench bench_parse
BENCH_SIZE_LIMIT=200 cargo bench --bench bench_parse
BENCH_SAMPLE_SIZE=50 cargo bench --bench bench_parse
\end{verbatim}

Figures for each family to be added. The expected shape: \texttt{deriv\_bc}
and the two \texttt{pderiv} variants stay flat or nearly flat;
\texttt{deriv\_std} grows with input length.

%
%
%

\subsubsection{Corpus benchmarks}

\texttt{bench\_dataset} runs the same five parsers on the prepared
corpus. Two Criterion groups are produced.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{dataset\_\textless source\textgreater}: one combined pass
        over the entire admitted corpus for one source, per parser. This
        is a throughput figure, not a per-pattern one.
  \item \texttt{dataset\_per\_pattern}: each parser timed on each
        individual (pattern, input) entry, one row per entry. This
        reveals the per-pattern cost distribution.
\end{itemize}

\begin{verbatim}

\subsubsection{Allocation counters}

\texttt{bench\_memory} replaces the system allocator with a wrapper that
counts allocated bytes, and reports three figures per (parser, source)
pair:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{total bytes}: every allocation during a call, including

  \item \textbf{average bytes per entry}: total divided by the entry

Three patterns are visible.

First, on \texttt{a*} the \texttt{deriv\_std} column grows linearly in
$n$, while the other three are bounded. This is the cost of the two-pass
structure: the standard parser keeps every intermediate expression alive
until the backward pass reconstructs the tree, so its state size is a
function of input length. The bit-coded and partial-derivative variants
do not carry a history, so their state does not grow.

Second, on \texttt{(a+(b+ab))*}, all four grow, but at very different
rates. At $n=16$, \texttt{deriv\_std} has grown to 9179 nodes,
\texttt{deriv\_bc} to 2303, and the two partial-derivative variants to
2560 and 2048. The gap between the two derivative-based variants is the
cost of carrying reconstruction information through each step, which the
bit-coded variant does not.

Third, the two partial-derivative variants stay close to each other
across both expressions. Their live state is the frontier of residuals,
not a single expression; the accumulation is linear in the number of
distinct residuals reachable, which is bounded by the pattern's own size.

\texttt{demo\_simp\_branches} shows why simplification never fires its
$r + r = r$ rule on \texttt{(a+(b+ab))*}. Every step has \emph{distinct}
branches by bit-annotation even when the structural shape is identical.
This is a property of the bit-coded representation, not a deficiency of
the simplifier: the branches genuinely differ, and merging them would
lose the reconstruction information that makes single-pass decoding
possible.

\section{Discussion of Results}
\label{sec:eval-discussion}

\subsection{Cost of the standard construction}

The two standard derivative parsers (\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} and
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}) are consistently the slowest and
memory-heaviest of the four. This is a direct consequence of the two-pass
structure described in Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}: every
intermediate expression is stored, so the whole derivation sequence stays
alive until the backward pass completes. On \texttt{a*} input of length
$n$, the standard parser's state size grows linearly in $n$, while the
bit-coded and partial-derivative parsers stay constant.

The two standard variants also differ from each other by a large
constant. \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} recurses over the expression and pays
a deep call-stack cost on ambiguous patterns, while
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} drives the same computation iteratively. On the
small test run, \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} is roughly $5\times$ faster
than \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} on Snort and only $1.4\times$ faster on
SpamAssassin. The difference reflects pattern shapes: Snort patterns are
longer and more nested, so recursion overhead dominates; SpamAssassin
patterns are short enough that iteration and recursion cost similar
amounts.

%
%
%

\subsection{Cost of bit-coding}

The bit-coded derivative parser (\texttt{deriv\_bc}) is the second
fastest and second most memory-efficient on the small run. The trade-off
it makes is that the forward pass cannot simplify away redundant
branches without losing reconstruction information. The
\texttt{demo\_simp\_branches} output shows this clearly: the branch
counts grow as expected but the distinctness count stays at 1 after bit
stripping, meaning every branch is retained because it carries a
different bit annotation. The retention is not wasted: it is exactly what
makes single-pass reconstruction possible.

%
%
%

\subsection{Cost of partial derivatives}

The two partial-derivative parsers are the fastest and most memory-light
on the small run. They split the residual into a set of states rather
than combining into one expression, which keeps each state small and
avoids the alternation growth that the standard derivative parser
suffers. The bit-coded variant (\texttt{pderiv\_bc}) is consistently
faster than the standard variant (\texttt{pderiv\_std}), since it
replaces the reference-counted injection closures with flat bit-vectors.

The price of partial derivatives is that they produce a non-POSIX parse
tree rather than a POSIX one. This is a specification difference, not an
implementation deficiency, and it is the reason the two families are
compared separately in Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement}: within each
family, the two variants must agree, but between families they are
allowed to differ.

%
%
%

\subsection{When each parser is appropriate}

The four parsers are not interchangeable. Table~\ref{tab:choose-parser}
summarises the decision.

is a per-pattern trade-off, not a parser choice.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

Three properties of the setup limit how far the results can be
generalised.

\emph{Corpus scope.} The evaluation uses two corpora, both of which are
byte- or keyword-oriented and written by practitioners. A corpus of
compiler-generated regular expressions, or one drawn from a domain with
different syntax conventions, might exhibit different acceptance rates
and different timing profiles.

\emph{Synthetic families.} The synthetic benchmark families are
hand-constructed. They isolate specific costs but do not claim to
represent the distribution of pattern shapes in the wild. A family whose
parameters do not appear in the corpus can still produce informative
curves, but it should not be read as a population estimate.

\emph{Measurement model.} \texttt{bench\_memory} measures allocation
counts, not wall-clock memory pressure. On systems with a garbage
collector or a page cache, the figures would interact with those
subsystems in ways this evaluation does not model. The evaluator runs on
a single machine, and the wall-clock figures in
Table~\ref{tab:corpus-timing} are therefore CPU- and cache-specific.

%
%

\subsection{Summary}

On both timing and memory, the partial-derivative parsers dominate the
derivative parsers, and within each family the bit-coded variant
dominates the standard variant. The gap between the two families is large
enough that the choice of construction matters more than the choice of
variant. The gap between the two variants within a family is smaller but
consistent, and it corresponds directly to the difference between storing
a whole derivation sequence and storing a flat bit vector.

All four parsers correctly classify every prepared case in the corpus;
no membership or within-family shape bugs were found. Within-family tree
agreement was checked on every matching case, and the POSIX-vs-non-POSIX
difference, when it appears, is a specification difference rather than an
implementation bug.
