\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}
developed four parser combinations, \texttt{deriv\_std} (recursive and
loop variants), \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{pderiv\_std} and
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}, each claimed to compute either the POSIX or the
leftmost-first parse tree, by four structurally different constructions.
This chapter evaluates those four parsers against each other, first for
correctness and then for performance, on two kinds of input: the small,
hand-constructed patterns used throughout this thesis to isolate one
algorithmic property at a time, and a corpus of real-world regular
expressions assembled from the same three sources, Snort/Suricata,
SpamAssassin and RegexLib, that Mamouras et al.'s disambiguation-robustness
study \citep{mamouras2024} draws on. That paper studies exactly the
POSIX-versus-Greedy question this thesis studies, via real intrusion-detection
signatures, and finds concrete real-world patterns where the two policies
visibly diverge; it provides no public dataset artifact of its own, so
Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation} describes how this thesis's own
corpus was independently assembled to occupy the same role.

Section~\ref{sec:correctness-verification} checks the four parsers, and
the three Boolean matchers of Chapter~\ref{sec:derivatives}, against each
other and against the paper's own worked examples.
Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation} describes both input sources in
detail: the project's own synthetic patterns and the real-world corpus.
Section~\ref{sec:performance-analysis} measures where the four parsers'
costs diverge on each. Section~\ref{sec:results-discussion} draws the
two together.

\section{Parser Agreement}
\label{sec:correctness-verification}

Running the same expression through every parser at once, rather than
the two the test above picks, shows the full pattern the test's single

\begin{verbatim}
$ cargo run -- parse "(a|ab)(b|)" "ab" --parser all
Parser       | Policy    | Result
-------------+-----------+-------------------------------
DERIV_REC    | POSIX     | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
DERIV_LOOP   | POSIX     | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
DERIV_BC     | POSIX     | (Right (a, b), Right ()) -> "ab"
PDERIV_STD   | NON-POSIX | (Left a, Left b) -> "ab"
PDERIV_BC    | NON-POSIX | (Left a, Left b) -> "ab"

POSIX parsers agree
PDERIV parsers agree
NON-POSIX agrees with POSIX on membership
\end{verbatim}

\section{Dataset Preparation}
\label{sec:dataset-preparation}

Two kinds of input drive the measurements in this chapter, and they are

\subsection{Synthetic Patterns}
\label{sec:synthetic-patterns}

The synthetic patterns used throughout this thesis, \texttt{paper\_r1},
\texttt{paper\_r2}, the pathological $(a^{*})^{*}$-style expressions of
Section~\ref{sec:membership-overview}, and the benchmark families of
Section~\ref{sec:performance-analysis}, are deliberately small and
hand-constructed, each chosen to isolate one specific algorithmic
property at a time rather than to resemble real regex use:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.2cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.8cm}@{}}
\hline
Family & Pattern shape & Property isolated \\
\hline
\texttt{small\_patterns} & \texttt{a}, \texttt{a*} against \texttt{""}/\texttt{"a"}/\texttt{"aaa"} & fixed per-call overhead \\
\texttt{scaling\_a\_star} & \texttt{a*} against $\mathtt{a}^n$ & cost as a function of input length alone \\
\texttt{deep\_expression} & $\mathtt{a}\cdot\mathtt{a}\cdots\mathtt{a}$, depth $n$ & cost as a function of expression size \\
\texttt{ambiguous\_star} & $(a+aa)^{*}$ against $\mathtt{a}^n$ & branching from genuine ambiguity \\
\texttt{pathological}, \texttt{benign} & $(a^{*})^{*}$ vs.\ $(a+b)^{*}$ & the naive matcher's worst and ordinary case \\
\texttt{nesting\_depth} & $a^{*}$ nested $n$ times & cost as a function of nesting, not length \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

They say little on their own about how any of the four parsers behaves
on the regular expressions people actually write, which is what
Section~\ref{sec:mamouras-dataset} closes the gap on; kept separate,
each still isolates one variable the mixed real-world corpus cannot.

\subsection{The Mamouras Regex Dataset}
\label{sec:mamouras-dataset}

This thesis's own real-world corpus was independently assembled from
three families of real-world regular expression sources chosen to cover
different "shapes" of real regex use, not three samples of the same
thing:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Snort/Suricata} network intrusion-detection signatures
  (\texttt{pcre:"..."} rule options, sourced from the Emerging Threats
  Open ruleset), literal-heavy, moderate complexity, the closest of
  the three to "everyday" regex use.
  \item \textbf{SpamAssassin} email spam-filtering rules
  (\texttt{body}/\texttt{header} Perl-regex rules), content matching
  over unstructured text, a different shape than network signatures.
  \item \textbf{RegexLib} crowd-sourced general-purpose patterns
  (validators, extractors), the opposite end, where chained lookahead
  assertions are idiomatic, stress-testing the frontend's rejection path
  hardest of the three.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Extraction.} Each source embeds its patterns in a different
surrounding syntax that has nothing to do with the pattern itself and
has to be stripped away before the pattern can be parsed at all.
Figure~\ref{fig:extraction-pipeline} shows the three formats side by
side: Suricata's rule lines carry a \texttt{pcre:"/PATTERN/FLAGS";}
option; SpamAssassin's carry a bare
\texttt{body}/\texttt{header NAME =~ /PATTERN/FLAGS} without
Suricata's trailing-quote convention; RegexLib's sample file lists plain
patterns directly, one (possibly multi-line) entry per
blank-line-delimited block. A separate extraction routine reads each
format's own surrounding syntax and reduces it to the pattern text
underneath; despite three different starting shapes, all three converge
on one common \texttt{/PATTERN/FLAGS} PCRE delimiter convention once
extracted, and it is only that common form which is ever handed to the
frontend parser, including the case-insensitive \texttt{i} flag, applied
by case-folding every literal character in the translated
\texttt{ExtPat} to an \texttt{Any} of both cases (Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}'s
frontend) rather than as a runtime matching option, since
\texttt{Regex} itself has no case-insensitivity concept.


\paragraph{Filtering.} Filtering is not a separate step run over
already-extracted patterns; it is what happens when the frontend parser
itself rejects one. A regular-expression engine built on the
six-constructor \texttt{Regex} of Section~\ref{sec:regex-rust-implementation}
cannot represent every construct a real, PCRE-authored rule may use.
Backreferences and lookaround assertions in particular describe
languages that are not regular in the formal sense this thesis's entire
construction depends on, so \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule} rejects both
outright with a descriptive error rather than silently mis-parsing them
into something weaker than what the rule author wrote. Every rejection
is recorded by cause rather than discarded: \texttt{backreference} and
\texttt{lookaround} each name themselves, and \texttt{other} covers
everything else that still fails to parse, chiefly bound expressions
such as \texttt{\{1586,\}} that would unroll past this frontend's
repetition cap, and a handful of unsupported \texttt{(?...)}~group
syntaxes. Re-running this pipeline against the tracked raw sources
gives the current triage, read a row at a time: of 231 Suricata rules,
209 parse and 22 are rejected, split 5/11/6 across the three causes, and
so on for the other two formats.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\hline
Format & Extracted & Accepted & Backreference & Lookaround & Other \\
\hline
Suricata & 231 & 209 (90.5\%) & 5 & 11 & 6 \\
SpamAssassin & 82 & 69 (84.1\%) & 1 & 11 & 1 \\
RegexLib & 499 & 407 (81.6\%) & 8 & 55 & 29 \\
\hline
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{812} & \textbf{685 (84.4\%)} & \textbf{14} & \textbf{77} & \textbf{36} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Lookaround dominates the rejections and is concentrated almost entirely
in RegexLib, matching that family's idiomatic use of chained
assertions; Suricata and SpamAssassin reject mostly on the same handful
of negative-lookahead idioms (guarding against one specific following
word). The accepted 685 patterns, deduplicated across all three formats
into \texttt{data/corpus\_patterns.txt} (663 lines after deduplication),
are what Section~\ref{sec:mamouras-benchmark-results}'s dataset
benchmark actually runs against.

Real rules are \emph{written} as search patterns, fired on seeing a
match anywhere in a packet payload or message body, not on the payload
being exactly that pattern, so they are parsed through the frontend's
search entry points rather than through plain full-string equality.
Section~\ref{sec:frontend-substring-search} already gives the mechanism
that implements this, padding an unanchored side with $\Sigma^{*}$; the
practical effect on this corpus specifically is that a real dataset
pattern, run the way it has to be run, is measurably bigger than its
own text suggests, which Section~\ref{sec:mamouras-benchmark-results}
returns to.
