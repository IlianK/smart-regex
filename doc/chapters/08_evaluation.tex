\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates the derivative and partial-derivative parsers
developed in Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} 
and \ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}. 
Using two datasets, 
it compares their execution time, memory consumption, and translation cost, 
as well as their match decisions and parse-tree shapes. 
All measurements were taken on an AMD Ryzen~5~5600 six-core processor
with 16\,GB of RAM, running Pop!\_OS 24.04 LTS.

%
%
%

\section{Candidates}
\label{sec:eval-candidates}

A candidate is a parser $p$ mapping an \emph{entry} $(r,w)$, 
consisting of a translated \texttt{Regex} $r$ 
and an input \texttt{String} $w$, 
to an optional \texttt{ParseTree} $pt$. 
All candidates share the interface:
\[
p :: (\mathit{Regex}, \mathit{String}) \to
     \mathit{Option\langle ParseTree\rangle}
\]
Candidates (Table~\ref{tab:eval-candidates})
are grouped into two families according 
to their disambiguation strategy.
All five parsers are expected to agree on membership.
So given an entry, they must either all accept or reject it.
\emph{Within family} the parsers are also expected to agree on parse-tree shape.
\emph{Across families} parse tree shape agreement is possible, but not required.

\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular*}{\textwidth}{@{\extracolsep{\fill}} llll}
\hline
Family & Candidate & Description & Section \\
\hline

          & \ref{sec:deriv-std-parser} \\
          & \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} & iterative standard derivative parser

referred to as \texttt{deriv\_std}.
Similarly, \texttt{deriv} and \texttt{pderiv}
refer to the derivative and partial-derivative
families, respectively.

\section{Evaluation Criteria}
\label{sec:eval-criteria}

The evaluation considers \emph{performance}, 
measured in terms of execution time, memory consumption, and translation cost, 
and \emph{agreement} on membership decisions and parse-tree selection.

%
%
%

\subsection{Measurement Setup}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-setup}

Each entry runs in a separate worker subprocess,
allowing the operating system to terminate it by signal
in cases such as stack overflow or out-of-memory failure.
This avoids the lack of safe thread termination in stable
Rust~\citep{rust-thread-joinhandle} and ensures that
the worker's resources can be reclaimed independently
of the benchmark harness.
Each worker is subject to a $12\,\mathrm{GiB}$ memory cap
and a default timeout of $60\,\mathrm{s}$,
which can be overridden for individual pattern tests.
The harness monitors these limits and terminates workers
that exceed their allotted execution time.
Runs are classified as \emph{measured} if the worker
exits successfully and reports a result,
\emph{timed out} if the timeout is exceeded,
or \emph{crashed} if the worker exits non-zero
or is terminated by a signal.

%
%
%

\subsection{Timing}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-timing}

Each entry $(r,w)$ is timed once, without warm-up or repetition.
Translation is measured separately
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-translation}).
Execution is divided into four phases across all five candidates.
Together, the four phases account for the complete
measured execution. Since setup differs between
implementations, total timings provide the common
basis for comparison.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Setup} covers initialization before derivation.
    For \texttt{deriv\_bc}, it measures
    \texttt{internalize}, which converts a \texttt{Regex} into \texttt{ARegex}
    (Section~\ref{sec:internalize}).
    This is necessary because \texttt{deriv\_bc}
    keeps \texttt{Alt} nodes whose branches must
    be tagged beforehand. Later \texttt{fuse}
    operations in \texttt{deriv\_bc} and \texttt{simp}
    only transfer existing bits and are not part
    of this initial encoding.

    In contrast, \texttt{pderiv\_bc} splits
    alternations into separate states, assigning
    branch bits during derivation via \texttt{flatten\_alt}
    (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-impl}).
    Its setup includes the initial clone of $r$
    and the accumulated cost of all \texttt{flatten\_alt}
    calls, although these occur during derivation.
    Otherwise, setup would cover only the initial clone,
    as no separate encoding is needed.

    For \texttt{pderiv\_std}, setup consists only
    of the initial clone, while the
    \texttt{deriv\_std}'s have zero setup cost,
    as they derive $r$ directly without
    initializing a separate state or representation.

    \item \emph{Derive} measures per-character
    derivative steps for all five candidates.

    \item \emph{Reconstruct} produces the final
    \texttt{ParseTree}. The bit-coded parsers
    decode their accumulated bits, while
    \texttt{pderiv\_std} applies composed injection
    closures through \texttt{Inj::apply}.
    Both \texttt{deriv\_std}'s perform
    backward injection, constructing the tree
    directly rather than applying a separately
    composed closure.

    \item \emph{Teardown} measures the cost of
    destroying the returned \texttt{ParseTree}
    and releasing its allocated memory.
    It is timed uniformly by the benchmark harness
    after the parser returns.
\end{itemize}

Four timing statistics are reported.
Aggregation determines whether measurements are combined
per pattern or across individual entries,
with the method depending on the dataset:

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-memory}

Memory is measured with a counting allocator in each worker,
reset immediately before $p(r,w)$ to isolate allocations made during the parser call. 
It tracks requested and live bytes:
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\emph{Max live peak} & Highest live-byte count during the call; peak heap demand. \\
\emph{Total allocated} & Sum of requested bytes over the call, a proxy for allocator pressure. \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Memory metrics collected by the counting allocator.}
\label{tab:eval-memory-metrics}
\end{table}

Total allocated is always at least the peak, 
since it includes memory freed during the call. 
Frequent allocation and deallocation can therefore increase total allocation 
and timing costs even when peak usage remains low.

\subsection{Translation}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-translation}

Translation depends only on the source pattern, 
and is independent of the input,
so each distinct pattern is translated once in the main process. 
The caps: \emph{Bound cap}, \emph{Depth cap} and \emph{Node cap} 
bind translation time and memory by rejecting patterns 
that would require excessive recursion or unrolling. 
Translation cost is measured using two quantities:

\subsection{Stack Depth}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-stack-depth}

This measurement determines the input lengths
at which stack exhaustion occurs, as well as
the translation limits.
Both standard derivative parsers use the default
thread stack ($8\,\mathrm{MiB}$ on Linux),
but consume it differently:
\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} keeps intermediate
derivatives in recursive call frames, whereas
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} stores them in a
heap-allocated \texttt{Vec}.
The probe timeout was reduced to $15\,\mathrm{s}$,
as this is sufficient to identify runs limited by expensive derivation.
These time out, whereas runs that reach the stack limit crash.
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}).
Timeouts are treated as negative controls,
indicating that derivation cost prevents
the stack limit from being reached.

A doubling search is used to determine 
at which input length a stack overflow is reached. 
The search starts from a small input length 
and doubles the input after each successful probe until the worker fails. 
The search brackets the last successful value and the first failure. 
The same method is used to find the two translation caps
\emph{Bound cap} and \emph{Depth cap}.

\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Target} & \textbf{Quantity doubled} & \textbf{Failure condition} \\
\hline
\emph{Parser stack} & Input length & Parser fails to complete \\
\emph{Bound cap} & Repetition bound & Translating the bound exhausts the stack \\
\emph{Depth cap} & Nesting depth of \texttt{(?:...)} groups & Translating the pattern exhausts the stack \\
\end{tabularx}

\subsection{Parse Agreement}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-agreement}

Parse agreement is evaluated on entries
$(r,w,e)$, where $e \in
\{\mathit{true},\mathit{false}\}$ denotes
the expected match result.
Since implementations within each family
follow the same disambiguation policy,
one representative is compared per family:
\texttt{deriv\_bc} for POSIX and
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} for non-POSIX.
\emph{Membership} is correct when both parsers
return a tree exactly if $e=\mathit{true}$.
A membership disagreement between the parsers
indicates an implementation error, since
disambiguation does not affect acceptance.
\emph{Shape} is compared only when
$e=\mathit{true}$ and both parsers return
a tree.
Since their disambiguation policies differ,
distinct parse trees may reflect legitimate
ambiguity rather than an implementation error.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\emph{Timeout or crash} & The candidate exceeded the timeout or was killed by a signal. \\
\emph{Membership} & Match ($e = \mathit{true}$) or no-match ($e = \mathit{false}$) decision. \\
\emph{Parse tree} & The parse tree returned, when $e = \mathit{true}$. \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Results recorded for each candidate during parse-agreement testing.}
\label{tab:eval-agreement-fields}

\subsection{S2ELab}
\label{sec:eval-s2elab}

The dataset by \citet{s2elab-regexeval-dataset} 
contains 762 human-authored patterns. 
Each pattern has a natural-language description 
and lists of labeled matching and non-matching inputs. 
The number of each varies per pattern, 
so the corpus is not balanced between the two.
The source patterns conventionally use \texttt{\textasciicircum} 
and \texttt{\textdollar} as beginning- and end-of-input anchors. 
A pattern surrounded by anchors requires a full-string match, 
where a pattern without them is intended for substring matching. 
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:surface-rejected},
substring matching is not supported and anchors are rejected constructs.
However, fully anchored patterns can still be used
by stripping their anchors.
Since the candidates perform full-string matching,
this preserves the original pattern's matching semantics.
So the benchmarks only use the 463 accepted patterns after anchor stripping.
Figure~\ref{fig:s2elab-pipeline} shows this 
as part of the data preparation, that turns one example pattern into an $entry$.

\subsection{Synthetic Stress Set}
\label{sec:eval-pathological}

The synthetic set consists of 112 patterns generated
with the assistance of Mistral AI and manually verified.
It is designed to stress the parsers and examine
how their costs scale with input length and pattern size.
All patterns are intended for full-string matching.
Each row defines a base pattern and input
parameterized by input length $n$, pattern size $p$, or both.
A \emph{sweep} varies one parameter over a range of values,
recording a measurement at each step.
Rows with IDs beginning with \texttt{P} fix the pattern
and increase $n$ to measure scaling with input length,
while \texttt{E} rows fix the input and increase $p$
to measure scaling with pattern size.
Table~\ref{tab:sweep-samples} illustrates one row of each type
at two sweep values, using their matching inputs.

\begin{table}[H]

\hline

    & \texttt{(a+)+}
    & $[\,\texttt{a}^{1000}\,]$ \\
E36 & $p = 1000$ & $n = 20$ & 1000-way word Alt
    & \texttt{(?:w000001|\ldots|w001000)+}

The 112 patterns are grouped by the complexity mechanism they test. 
Each category has its own sweep range.
Each swept value contributes two entries, 
one matching and one non-matching,
allowing costs to be measured for both outcomes.
The latter uses a pattern-specific
near-miss formed by adding or removing one boundary character.
Entries are pooled across each category's patterns,
and every entry is run once, without repeated trials. 
Table~\ref{tab:patho-categories}
gives the resulting pattern and entry counts. 
For example, category A has 160 entries from 16 patterns, each swept
over $n \in \{10, 20, 30, 1\,000, 10\,000\}$ at $p=100$ and tested
with both outcomes ($16 \times 5 \times 2 = 160$).

The patterns in these categories were generated using
prompts specifying the intended stress
mechanisms and alphabets, as summarized in

Both parsers keep all $n$ intermediate derivatives
for backward reconstruction, but store them differently.
The recursive form uses one stack frame per character,
whereas the iterative form uses a heap-allocated \texttt{Vec}.
P01 and P30 serve as negative controls, using
a $15\,\mathrm{s}$ timeout to stop expensive derivations
before stack exhaustion can be observed.
P01 times out at $n=16$ due to near-exponential
derivative growth from nested quantifiers,
while P30 times out at $n=64$ due to polynomial
growth from ten adjacent \texttt{(\textbackslash w+)} groups.
In both cases, derivation becomes too expensive
before the stack limit is reached.
E51 isolates stack usage using the simple pattern
\texttt{x} and inputs consisting entirely of \texttt{a}.
The immediate mismatch keeps the residual fixed,
making derivative computation cheap.
The recursive parser crashes at $n=32\,768$,
confirming its stack-depth limitation.
The iterative form avoids this limitation by storing
intermediate derivatives on the heap,
whose memory costs are examined separately.
For subsequent evaluations, only
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} is reported
as \texttt{deriv\_std}, acting as the representative of both.

test ambiguous repetitions and overlapping alternatives,

to separate overlapping alternatives and deduplicate
identical residuals, avoiding some of the repeated
simplification required by \texttt{deriv\_bc}.
Both approaches complete all entries without failures.
In contrast, \texttt{deriv\_std} crashes on
88 of 160 entries in A, 82 of 150 in B,
and 38 of 80 in E, due to growing
residual expressions and saving the derivation sequence.

\paragraph{Category C}
tests bounded repetition around quantified groups,
whose unrolling creates up to ten copies of
an already ambiguous subexpression.
Here, \texttt{deriv\_bc} outperforms both
\texttt{pderiv} variants, with a median of
70.76\,ms against 195.34 and 178.74\,ms.

distinct partial derivatives, each requiring
processing at every input character, while
\texttt{deriv\_bc} simplifies a single residual expression.
All three complete every entry, whereas
\texttt{deriv\_std} crashes on 30 of 56.

\paragraph{Category D}
shows the strongest advantage for \texttt{deriv\_bc}.

164.27\,ms for \texttt{deriv\_bc}, compared with

remaining suffixes differ as the input is consumed.

structurally identical residuals, potentially retaining
many distinct states to process at each character.
In contrast, \texttt{deriv\_bc} repeatedly simplifies
a single residual expression.

more effective residual simplification
rather than bit-coding itself.

shows a similar, though less pronounced, advantage

category except D, while \texttt{deriv\_bc}
and both \texttt{pderiv} variants complete all
entries in A--E and H.

considerably faster.

sensitive to different pattern structures,

\paragraph{P60 and P61}
Both rows use the pattern \texttt{(a|b|ab)*},
with P60 repeating \texttt{a} and P61 repeating \texttt{ab}.

examined by \citet[Figs.~7(a) and 7(b)]{sulzmannlu2014}.

Figures~\ref{fig:patho-fig7a}

A ratio of $1.0$ denotes the fastest candidate,

\texttt{deriv\_bc} (32.47\,s) and
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} (33.21\,s).
On P61, \texttt{deriv\_bc} narrowly leads
(51.49\,s), ahead of \texttt{pderiv\_std}

and~\ref{tab:patho-phases-p61}

their reconstruction workloads differ.
For \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{mk\_eps\_bc}
flattens a relatively uniform annotation structure
built incrementally through \texttt{fuse}.
In contrast, \texttt{pderiv\_bc} must flatten
a history of nested \texttt{flatten\_alt}
annotations accumulated along the surviving path.
\texttt{pderiv\_std} instead reconstructs
through \texttt{Inj::apply}, evaluating a chain
of deferred injection closures.

Teardown is also more expensive for both
\texttt{pderiv} variants, particularly on P61.
There, their parse trees contain twice as many
Star iterations, selecting individual
\texttt{a} and \texttt{b} branches rather than
the \texttt{ab} iterations produced by
\texttt{deriv\_bc}.
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s reported setup
rises from 3.01\,ms on P60 to 2\,051.81\,ms
on P61, reflecting the accumulated cost of
\texttt{flatten\_alt} during derivation
rather than initial pattern preparation.

Tracing \texttt{pderiv\_bc} with $B$ for the
pattern \texttt{a|b|ab} and states $S_0 = \{B^{*}\}$,

\[
\begin{array}{r@{\ }l@{\qquad\qquad}r@{\ }l}
\multicolumn{2}{c}{\texttt{a}\text{-repeated}} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\texttt{ab}\text{-repeated}} \\[2pt]

\end{array}

\clearpage
These reveal distinct costs
associated with repeated and changing residual structures.
For \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{internalize}
annotates the pattern as
$[0](a \mathbin{|} b) \oplus [1](a \cdot b)$
under the Star.
The outer alternation identifies the branch
($[0]$ for \texttt{a|b}), the inner alternation
identifies the literal ($[0]a \oplus [1]b$),
and the Star records whether repetition continues
($[0]$) or terminates ($[1]$).

Two operations contribute differently to
the cost of each derivative step.
The cheaper operation applies when
\texttt{a|b} is already nullable.
The nullable-Seq rule
$\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$
(Equation~\eqref{eq:bderiv})
extracts the completed path through
\texttt{mkEpsBC} and attaches it to
the Star continuation $X$ through \texttt{fuse}.
This processes a fixed three-bit annotation.
The more expensive operation derives the
bare Star body, constructing a new \texttt{Seq}
node from its alternatives instead of
reusing the existing residual structure.

On P60, this structural expansion occurs
only for the first \texttt{a}.
Thereafter, \texttt{a|b} is already nullable
at the start of each step, so subsequent
derivatives follow the same residual structure:
the \texttt{b} branch vanishes,
the \texttt{a} branch resolves directly,
and \texttt{fuse} extends the annotations
without rebuilding the Star body.
P60 therefore reaches a steady state after
its initial derivative.

On P61, each \texttt{a}
follows a \texttt{b} that leaves
\texttt{a|b} non-nullable.
Consequently, every \texttt{a} requires
the structural expansion performed only
once on P60.
The following \texttt{b} resolves the
corresponding branch and commits its
two-bit annotation, returning the residual
to a state that requires expansion again
on the next \texttt{a}.
So P61 repeatedly alternates between
expansion and collapse.

The main difference is therefore the frequency
of structural reconstruction, rather than
the length of the accumulated bit annotations.
While P60 has the expensive expansion only
once, P61 repeats it on every other character.
This explains the higher measured derivation
cost of \texttt{deriv\_bc} on P61
(Tables~\ref{tab:patho-phases-p60}
and~\ref{tab:patho-phases-p61}),
despite its shorter accumulated bit lists.

The \texttt{pderiv\_bc} traces show a
related distinction.
On P60, the state set reaches $S_1$
after the first character and remains there.
Each subsequent \texttt{a} produces the
same two-residual structure, with one
continuation discarded as empty.
On P61, however, the state set alternates
between $S_0$ and $S_1$.
Each \texttt{a} expands $S_0$ into $S_1$,
while each \texttt{b} collapses it again.
Unlike P60's elimination of an empty residual,
this collapse produces two independently
derived residuals with the same structure.
The \texttt{nub2} operation must identify
and remove the duplicate, introducing
additional deduplication work on P61.
Despite these differences,
\texttt{pderiv\_std} remains faster than
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} at the largest sampled
lengths of both rows:
28.36\,s versus 33.21\,s on P60,
and 52.26\,s versus 54.26\,s on P61
(Tables~\ref{tab:patho-p60-all-parsers}
and~\ref{tab:patho-p61-all-parsers}).
Thus, bit-coding provides no measured
speed advantage for the partial-derivative
implementation on these inputs.
The relative cost of bit annotations
and injection closures cannot be determined
from wall time alone.

\paragraph{P01 and P09}
Both rows use repeated \texttt{a} inputs but
introduce ambiguity through different constructions.
P01, \texttt{(a+)+}, nests quantified repetitions,
allowing multiple divisions into outer iterations.
P09, \texttt{(a|aa)+}, instead uses overlapping
alternatives under a single repetition.
Both admit multiple decompositions,
but differ in how these occur during derivation.

Figures~\ref{fig:patho-p01}
and~\ref{fig:patho-p09} show the measured
wall times and ratios to the fastest parser
at each sampled input length.
Their behaviour differs substantially.
On P01, \texttt{deriv\_bc} briefly leads
at $n=15$ and $n=20$, but its cost grows
much faster than that of the \texttt{pderiv} variants.
By $n=1\,000$, it is nearly three times slower,
and at $n=50\,000$ its ratio reaches
approximately $156\times$.
It then times out at $n=200\,000$,
while both \texttt{pderiv} variants complete
the sweep through $n=10\,000\,000$.

On P09, \texttt{deriv\_bc} instead maintains
approximately linear scaling and completes
the entire sweep.
It remains roughly $1.4$--$2.4\times$ slower
than the fastest \texttt{pderiv} variant,
without the widening gap observed on P01.
Thus, although both patterns introduce ambiguity,
nested repetition causes a pronounced
scaling disadvantage for \texttt{deriv\_bc}.

The contrasting scaling behaviour can be traced
to the derivative rule for \texttt{Seq} in
\texttt{deriv\_bc}.
Whenever the left operand is already nullable,
the rule invokes \texttt{mk\_eps\_bc}
(Figure~\ref{fig:mkepsbc-rust}) to extract its
accumulated bits, then attaches them to the
right operand through \texttt{fuse}
(Figure~\ref{fig:fuse-rust},
Section~\ref{sec:fuse}).
The cost of this operation depends on the
length of the left operand's bit list,
rather than being constant per character.

On P01, \texttt{(a+)+} desugars to
\texttt{Seq(a+, (a+)*)}.
After matching the first \texttt{a},
the inner \texttt{a+} remains nullable,
causing the rule to fire on every subsequent
character.
Meanwhile, the bit list materialized by
\texttt{mk\_eps\_bc} grows by one bit per step:
direct measurements show 1 bit at $n=2$,
999 at $n=1\,000$, and 19\,999 at $n=20\,000$.
Repeatedly processing this growing list
produces the approximately quadratic scaling
observed in Figure~\ref{fig:patho-p01}.
While \texttt{deriv\_bc} remains competitive
with the \texttt{pderiv} variants up to
approximately $n=20$, it is nearly
$3\times$ slower at $n=1\,000$ and
roughly $156\times$ slower at $n=50\,000$.
Notably, its residual expression remains
fixed at eight \texttt{ARegex} nodes:
the increasing cost arises from the
annotations carried by these nodes,
not from growth in the expression tree itself.

P09, \texttt{(a|aa)+}, behaves differently.
Here, the nullable-Seq rule fires only
during the first few derivative steps
and does not recur as the input grows.
Consequently, \texttt{deriv\_bc} avoids
repeated materialization of an increasingly
long bit list, maintaining approximately
constant work per character.
This is consistent with its near-linear
scaling and relatively stable
$1.4$--$2.4\times$ slowdown against
the faster \texttt{pderiv} variant.
Both \texttt{pderiv} variants complete
the full sweeps and remain close in performance.
Rather than maintaining a single combined
expression, they track separate residuals
in a partial-derivative frontier,
deduplicated through hashing
(\texttt{nub2}/\texttt{nub\_tree}).
The different scaling behaviour is thus
largely explained by whether derivation
repeatedly materializes a growing bit list,
as on P01, or avoids this overhead, as on P09.

\paragraph{E37 and E38}
Unlike the preceding input-length sweeps, E37 and E38
vary pattern size while keeping the input fixed
to the short literal matching the first alternative.
Both belong to category J (``large pattern, short input'')
and use $p$-way alternations to examine how
the five parsers' timing phases
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-timing})
scale with pattern size on matching inputs.
Alternation allows the node count to grow with
a single parameter $p$, independently of input length.
E37 uses distinct four-letter alternatives,
matched by \texttt{"aaaa"}
(Figure~\ref{fig:patho-e37},
Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e37}),
while E38 uses numbered placeholder words,
matched by \texttt{"w000001"}
(Figure~\ref{fig:patho-e38},
Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e38}).
Both sweep $p \in \{100, 500, 1\,000,
2\,000, 5\,000, 10\,000\}$.
The node cap was raised from 20\,000 to
200\,000 (Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-translation})
to accommodate these large patterns.
At $p = 10\,000$, E38's seven-character words each translate to 13 nodes
(seven literals chained by six concatenations), and joining all
$10\,000$ of them adds $9\,999$ more nodes for the alternation itself:
$10\,000 \times 13 + 9\,999 = 139\,999$ nodes total.

On E37, \texttt{Setup} grows modestly with $p$.
It is consistently cheaper for
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} than for \texttt{deriv\_bc},
whose \texttt{internalize} performs more work
per node than cloning the expression and
assigning annotations through \texttt{flatten\_alt}.
\texttt{Reconstruct} remains below 2\,ms
for all parsers except \texttt{deriv\_std},
whose backward injection reaches 161.73\,ms
at $p=10\,000$, dominating its total time.

For the other three parsers, \texttt{Derive}
dominates the total.
At $p=10\,000$, \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s
derivation takes approximately $2.5\times$
that of \texttt{pderiv\_bc}.
Both use hash-based deduplication:
\texttt{deriv\_bc}'s \texttt{dedup\_first}
hashes branch structures, while
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s \texttt{nub2}
hashes residuals.
Both operations scale linearly with the
number of candidates, so their timing
difference is consistent with differing
per-candidate costs rather than growth rates.

On E38, \texttt{Setup} and \texttt{Derive}
are higher for both bit-coded parsers,
consistent with its longer alternatives.
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} again has lower setup
cost than \texttt{deriv\_bc}, while their
derivation-time ratio is approximately
$2.1\times$ at $p=10\,000$.
Reconstruction remains below 2\,ms
except for \texttt{deriv\_std},
whose backward pass reaches 258.29\,ms.
The partial-derivative variants achieve
the lowest total times, followed by
\texttt{deriv\_bc} at 94.46\,ms and
\texttt{deriv\_std} at 332.00\,ms.

%
%
%

At $p=10\,000$, \texttt{deriv\_bc} is approximately
$3.5\times$ faster than \texttt{deriv\_std}
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e38}).
Unlike \texttt{deriv\_std}, which keeps the
alternation without attempting simplification,
\texttt{deriv\_bc} attempts to deduplicate its branches.
Since all $p$ alternatives are distinct by construction,
no branches can be removed.
However, hash-based deduplication avoids comparing
every pair, keeping this otherwise unproductive
simplification step from dominating the runtime.

For \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{dedup\_first}
(Figure~\ref{fig:dedup-first-ch5-rust})
hashes each branch using \texttt{hash\_shape}
(Figure~\ref{fig:hash-shape-ch5-rust},
Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}).
The hash follows the same structural recursion
as \texttt{same\_shape} (Section~\ref{sec:simp-impl}),
but ignores bit-code history.
Branches with equal shapes therefore receive
equal hashes, while possible hash collisions
are resolved through \texttt{same\_shape}.
Hashing serves only as a prefilter, so
differently shaped branches are never merged
solely because their hashes coincide.

For $p$ distinct alternatives, hash-based
deduplication avoids potentially quadratic
pairwise comparisons, requiring expected
$O(p)$ time for fixed-length words.
However, the cost of hashing each branch
still depends on its size:
\texttt{hash\_shape} recursively traverses
each word's \texttt{Seq} chain.
E38's seven-character alternatives therefore
require more hashing work than E37's
four-letter words, contributing to the higher
\texttt{deriv\_bc} timings across the sweep.

This implements the \texttt{nub} operation
in the choice case of
\citet[sect.~3, Fig.~6]{sulzmannlu2014}:
\[
simp\ (bs@{\oplus}(ri : rsi)) =
bs@{\oplus}\bigl(
\mathit{nub}\
(\mathit{filter}\ (\neg\,\mathit{isPhi})\
((simp\ ri) : \mathit{map}\ simp\ rsi))
\bigr).
\]
Haskell's standard \texttt{nub} uses equality
comparisons against previously retained elements,
giving quadratic worst-case complexity.
In contrast, \texttt{dedup\_first} first groups
branches by structural hash and performs equality
checks only within matching buckets.
This preserves the intended deduplication semantics
while reducing comparisons between distinct branches.

The partial-derivative variants use a related
hash-based approach through \texttt{nub2}
and \texttt{nub\_tree}.
Unlike \texttt{dedup\_first}, which compares
branch shapes within a combined expression,
these functions deduplicate residual states directly.
The variants differ in their associated
reconstruction data: \texttt{pderiv\_bc}
carries bit annotations, whereas
\texttt{pderiv\_std} carries injection closures.
Their deduplication mechanisms are otherwise
similar, so Figure~\ref{fig:step-frontier-bc-rust}
(Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-state-set-impl})
shows only \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s
\texttt{step\_frontier} and its use of \texttt{nub2}.

Initially, \texttt{pderiv\_bc} must flatten
the nested $p$-way alternation into separate
residuals, assigning branch-bit prefixes
and removing duplicates through \texttt{nub2}
(Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-impl}).
Direct measurements place most of its processing
cost for these wide alternations in this first step.
Subsequent calls to \texttt{step\_frontier}
derive the active residuals and deduplicate
the resulting frontier.
E38's longer alternatives require additional
processing before the matching branch is resolved,
further increasing derivation cost relative to E37.

The partial-derivative variants are faster still,
by approximately $1.6$--$2.0\times$ relative to
\texttt{deriv\_bc} (Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e38}).
Both sides deduplicate in $O(p)$, so this remaining
gap is not about that complexity class but about
how often the work runs.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} holds the $p$ alternatives as
branches of one combined expression: \texttt{simp}
(Section~\ref{sec:simp}) re-applies \texttt{simp\_once}
to a fixpoint, calling \texttt{same\_shape} to compare
the result against the input and looping again
whenever they differ.
Measured directly on a wide alternation, every
derivative step takes exactly two \texttt{simp\_once}
passes, never one -- the first flattens, simplifies,
and deduplicates the $p$ branches; the second
re-walks and re-hashes what is left, and
\texttt{same\_shape} confirms the result is already
stable.
The \texttt{pderiv} variants instead keep one state
per surviving residual: \texttt{step\_frontier} has
no equivalent loop, deriving each residual once and
calling \texttt{nub2} once per character, with no
\texttt{same\_shape} check against a prior state.
What separates the two families is therefore not the
deduplication algorithm, which is $O(p)$ on both sides,
but how it is embedded -- hashing that runs twice per
character to fold one combined expression back to a
fixpoint, against hashing that runs once to fold a
frontier of independent residuals.

\paragraph{Category D}
shows the strongest allocation advantage

58\,747.9 and 50\,964.5\,MB for
the \texttt{pderiv} variants.

(3.6 versus 28.6 and 18.8\,MB).
The chains of bounded and optional repetitions
may produce many distinct residual suffixes
that cannot be merged by structural deduplication.
Maintaining and updating these separate states
could therefore increase allocation in the
\texttt{pderiv} variants, whereas
\texttt{deriv\_bc} benefits from simplifying
a single residual expression.

\paragraph{Category F}
exhibits a substantial allocator pressure
despite relatively low peak memory.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} allocates 107\,518.1\,MB
across its 58 completed entries,
yet reaches a peak of only 42.0\,MB.
Both \texttt{pderiv} variants complete all
70 entries and allocate considerably less
overall (18\,248.8 and 21\,523.5\,MB),
although their peaks are higher
(85.2 and 108.1\,MB).
The required final literal delays rejection
on non-matching inputs, forcing continued
derivative processing.
For \texttt{deriv\_bc}, repeated construction
and simplification of intermediate expressions
may account for the high cumulative allocation,
even though much of this memory is freed
before the peak can grow substantially.

\paragraph{Category H}
shows a smaller advantage for \texttt{deriv\_bc}.
Among the three parsers completing all
28 entries, it has the lowest total
(13\,823.9\,MB), compared with
17\,396.4 and 15\,758.0\,MB for
the \texttt{pderiv} variants.
Its peak is also lower
(2.1 versus 4.1 and 4.7\,MB).

potentially increasing the number of
distinct partial derivatives maintained.

may reduce the associated allocation costs.
In contrast, \texttt{deriv\_std} crashes
on half the entries and reaches a peak
of 1\,871.0\,MB among those completed.

\paragraph{Overall}
\texttt{deriv\_std} reaches the highest
live-memory peak in every category,
consistent with retaining intermediate
derivatives for backward reconstruction.

dominant because many entries are excluded

Among the other parsers, the lowest allocation
depends on the stress mechanism:
the \texttt{pderiv} variants generally
allocate less for overlapping alternatives,
whereas \texttt{deriv\_bc} benefits on
bounded repetitions and adjacent groups.

suggesting that the number and representation

time and allocation pressure.

Within the partial-derivative family,
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} has lower total allocation
in eight of eleven categories, but does not
consistently achieve lower peak memory.

cumulative allocation relative to injection
closures without necessarily reducing the
largest live-memory footprint.
Overall, the results reflect the combined effects
of residual construction, simplification,
and reconstruction-data representation,
rather than bit-coding alone.

their peaks approximately double
whenever $p$ doubles.
This is consistent with sharing between
the representations of neighboring
alternatives, although the aggregate peaks
do not establish the exact mechanism.

\section{Findings}
\label{sec:eval-discussion}

\subsection{Cost of the standard construction}

\texttt{deriv\_std}
is most crash-prone and incurs the highest
time and memory costs on demanding entries,
though not on typical small inputs.
Its two-pass design retains intermediate
derivatives for backward reconstruction,
causing linear memory growth with $n$
on \texttt{a*}, while the other variants
remain bounded over the measured range.
On wide alternations of distinct words,
such as E37 and E38, avoiding simplification
overhead can be beneficial, since few
redundant branches exist.
The stack-depth probe further exposes
the limitations of recursive derivation,
making iteration preferable for long inputs
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-stack-depth}).

%
%
%


\subsection{Cost of bit-coding}
\label{sec:eval-cost-bit-coding}

Within the standard-derivative family, bit-coding
does not improve typical-case speed:
\texttt{deriv\_std} has a lower median time
than \texttt{deriv\_bc} on the corpus
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-s2elab}),
suggesting that bookkeeping overhead dominates
on small entries.
Its main benefits are memory efficiency
and robustness.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} reaches a peak of 4.9\,MB,
roughly $1\,900\times$ below
\texttt{deriv\_std}'s 9\,282\,MB, and completes
all 11\,395 corpus entries without failure.
However, the peaks cover different completed
populations because \texttt{deriv\_std}
crashes on some entries.
Bit-coded reconstruction adds little overhead,
remaining below 2\,ms even at $p=10\,000$
alternatives, while setup reaches 10.45\,ms
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e37}).
At larger alternation widths,
\texttt{deriv\_bc} also overtakes
\texttt{deriv\_std} in execution time,
suggesting that its simplification and
representation costs become more favorable
as pattern width increases.
The representative sweeps reinforce its
robustness: \texttt{deriv\_bc} reaches
ten million characters on P60, P61, and P09,
whereas \texttt{deriv\_std} crashes on all four
input-length sweeps.
Nevertheless, \texttt{deriv\_bc} can incur
substantial allocator pressure, allocating
52.43\,GB cumulatively on P60 despite a
peak of only 2.16\,GB.
This highlights the distinction between
cumulative allocation and live memory
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-memory}).

%
%
%


\subsection{Cost of partial derivatives}
\label{sec:eval-cost-partial-derivatives}

The partial-derivative parsers achieve the lowest
execution times and memory usage overall on the
S2ELab corpus. By maintaining a set of residuals
rather than one combined expression, they can
avoid some of the expression growth associated
with standard derivatives.
This advantage is particularly evident on wide
alternations, where both partial-derivative
variants outperform \texttt{deriv\_bc}.
One possible explanation is the different
handling of residual alternatives.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} simplifies a combined
expression, whereas \texttt{pderiv\_bc}
deduplicates a frontier of residuals
(Sections~\ref{sec:simp-impl}
and~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-impl}).
Although both use structural hashing,
their representations and simplification
work differ.
The comparable scaling observed on the
alternation sweeps suggests that the measured
advantage reflects lower per-alternative
costs rather than a clearly different
growth rate at these widths.
The remaining performance gap on
non-matching inputs further suggests
that deduplication alone does not
explain the difference.
Within the partial-derivative family,
bit-coding provides a modest advantage
in corpus timing and cumulative allocation,
but not in peak memory.
This may partly reflect differences in
reconstruction bookkeeping:
\texttt{pderiv\_std} maintains injection
closures, whereas \texttt{pderiv\_bc}
records bit annotations
(Sections~\ref{sec:pderiv-tree-impl}
and~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc}).
However, the representative stress patterns
reverse the timing ranking, with
\texttt{pderiv\_std} outperforming
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} throughout.
Their peak-memory rankings also vary.
Thus, bit-coding does not provide
a uniform advantage across workloads.
The wide-alternation sweeps show
approximately linear peak-memory growth
for both partial-derivative variants,
despite their position-dependent
reconstruction representations.
This is consistent with substantial
structural sharing, although the
measurements do not establish its extent.
Overall, the partial-derivative approach
offers clear benefits on the measured corpus
and wide alternations, but its relative
cost depends on the residual structure
and reconstruction mechanism.
Both partial-derivative variants produce
non-POSIX trees. This reflects a different
parsing policy rather than an implementation
error and motivates comparing the two
parser families separately in
Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement}.

%
%


\subsection{Threats to validity}

\paragraph{Synthetic set}
The 112 pattern rows isolate specific complexity
mechanisms at controlled scales rather than
representing typical user patterns.
Most categories vary one parameter at a time,
leaving interactions between stress mechanisms
largely untested.

\paragraph{Agreement subset}
The \emph{Compared} subset includes expected matches
for which both parsers complete and agree with
the expected label.
No S2ELab matches are excluded, but 20 of
443 synthetic matches (4.5\%) are omitted:
15 due to timeouts and five due to label
disagreements in category C.
The timeouts occur in categories F, I, J,
and K, potentially biasing comparisons toward
less demanding entries.
In alternation-heavy categories, excluded cases
may also be more ambiguous, although this
does not necessarily apply to long-input cases.

\paragraph{Instrumentation}
Timing and memory instrumentation may introduce
measurement overhead and alter allocation or
execution behavior.
In particular, phase measurements and allocation
tracking provide useful cost breakdowns but
may not reproduce the exact costs of an
uninstrumented parser.
The results therefore characterize the measured
implementations under the evaluation setup,
rather than instrumentation-free performance.

\paragraph{Measurement model}
Memory measurements cover heap allocations,
not total process memory, while timing depends
on the hardware and execution environment.
A recorded \emph{crash} indicates termination
by a signal, not necessarily stack overflow
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}).
The separate stack-depth probe provides
direct evidence of recursive stack exhaustion,
whereas crashes in the representative sweeps
are consistent with retained-derivative
memory growth but do not establish its
precise failure mechanism.
