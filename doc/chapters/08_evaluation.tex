\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}
developed four parser combinations, \texttt{deriv\_std} (recursive and
loop variants), \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{pderiv\_std} and
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}, each claimed to compute either the POSIX or the
leftmost-first parse tree, by four structurally different constructions.
This chapter evaluates those four parsers against each other, first for
correctness and then for performance, on two kinds of input: 1. Small,
constructed patterns taken from the source paper to isolate one
algorithmic property at a time; 2. A corpus of real-world regular
expressions assembled from three sources: Snort/Suricata,
SpamAssassin and RegexLib, that Mamouras et al.'s disambiguation-robustness
study \citep{mamouras2024} uses. 

That paper studies exactly the
POSIX-versus-Greedy question this thesis studies, via real intrusion-detection
signatures, and finds concrete real-world patterns where the two policies
visibly diverge. It provides no public dataset artifact of its own, so
Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation} describes how the 
corpus was independently assembled and prepared.

Section~\ref{sec:correctness-verification} checks the four parsers, and
the three Boolean matchers of Chapter~\ref{sec:derivatives}, against each
other and against the paper's own worked examples.
Section~\ref{sec:dataset-preparation} describes how the real-world
corpus specifically is assembled and prepared into a benchmarkable
dataset; the small hand-constructed patterns used directly in
Section~\ref{sec:performance-analysis}'s own benchmark families need no
such preparation and are introduced there instead.
Section~\ref{sec:performance-analysis} measures where the four parsers'
costs diverge on each and the final Section~\ref{sec:results-discussion} draws the
two together.

\section{Dataset Preparation}
\label{sec:dataset-preparation}

This section builds the one dataset the measurements later in this
chapter run against: real-world regular expressions drawn from Suricata,
SpamAssassin, and RegexLib rule text, carried through extraction,
acceptance, input generation, and verification. The small
hand-constructed patterns used elsewhere in this thesis -- the
$(a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$ worked example, $(a^{*})^{*}$-style pathological
expressions, and similar -- are not part of it: each exists to isolate
one specific algorithmic property for one specific figure or trace, and
pooling them with a real-world corpus would blur exactly the property
each one is there to isolate. Only the real-world corpus is prepared as
a reusable, benchmarkable dataset; it is the sole input to everything
that follows in this chapter.

\subsection{The Real-World Corpus}
\label{sec:mamouras-dataset}

This thesis's corpus is independently assembled from three families of
real-world regular-expression sources, chosen to cover different
"shapes" of real regex use rather than three samples of the same thing:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Snort/Suricata} network intrusion-detection signatures
  (\texttt{pcre:"..."} rule options, sourced from the Emerging Threats
  Open ruleset), literal-heavy, moderate complexity, the closest of
  the three to "everyday" regex use.
  \item \textbf{SpamAssassin} email spam-filtering rules
  (\texttt{body}/\texttt{header} Perl-regex rules), content matching
  over unstructured text, a different shape than network signatures.
  \item \textbf{RegexLib} crowd-sourced general-purpose patterns
  (validators, extractors), the opposite end, where anchors and chained
  lookahead assertions are both idiomatic, stress-testing the frontend's
  rejection path hardest of the three.
\end{itemize}

Assembling a real-world corpus this way, rather than relying only on
hand-constructed patterns, follows the motivation in Mamouras et al.'s
disambiguation-robustness study \citep{mamouras2024}: that paper studies
exactly the POSIX-versus-Greedy question this thesis studies, via real
intrusion-detection signatures, and finds concrete real-world patterns
where the two policies visibly diverge. It publishes no dataset artifact
of its own, so the corpus used here was assembled independently.

\paragraph{Extraction.}
A separate extractor reads each source's native text format directly,
rather than a shared one assuming a structure the three formats do not
actually have in common. \texttt{extract\_suricata} scans each line of a
\texttt{.rules} file for a \texttt{pcre:"..."} option, pulling out the
pattern together with that rule's \texttt{content:} fields, \texttt{msg},
and \texttt{sid} as context input generation uses later.
\texttt{extract\_spamassassin} reads a \texttt{.cf} file's
\texttt{body NAME /pattern/flags} and
\texttt{header NAME Field =\textasciitilde{} /pattern/flags} lines; the
two genuinely differ in syntax, since a \texttt{body} rule carries no
\texttt{=\textasciitilde{}} operator at all, and requiring one, as an
earlier version of this extractor did, silently dropped every
\texttt{body} rule rather than just failing loudly on it.
\texttt{extract\_regexlib} reads RegexLib's comment-delimited export
format directly: a block of \texttt{\#}-prefixed description lines
followed by the bare pattern, one entry per blank-line-separated block.
All three produce the same \texttt{ExtractedRule}: a source tag, the raw
pattern text, and whatever source-specific context only that source
provides.

\paragraph{Acceptance.}
An extracted rule's raw pattern is handed to \texttt{core\_regex}
(\texttt{data/prepare.rs}), which repeats \texttt{parse\_pcre\_rule}'s own
steps (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchors}) -- delimiter stripping, the
\texttt{m}/\texttt{x} flag check, parsing, case-folding, \texttt{dotall}
-- rather than calling it directly, so that two further, pipeline-only
safety caps can run around translation: a pattern whose \emph{estimated}
translated depth already exceeds 2\,000 is rejected before translation is
attempted at all, and a pattern that translates to more than 20\,000
\texttt{Regex} nodes is rejected after. Both exist for the same reason
\texttt{MAX\_BOUND} does in Section~\ref{sec:frontend-translate}: a
character-class range can expand from a few source characters into
thousands of code points -- one RegexLib pattern in this corpus does
exactly this, with a range spanning from \texttt{-} to the en dash -- and
translating a pattern that large is itself the risk these caps exist to
avoid, independently of whether \texttt{translate} would otherwise
succeed on it. A pattern surviving every check \texttt{core\_regex}
applies -- the rejections Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchors} already
described, plus these two size caps -- is \emph{accepted}; every other
pattern is \emph{unusable} and takes no further part in the pipeline.
There is no accepted-but-approximate middle case left to report: a
pattern is either translated exactly or not translated at all.

The following reports both numbers against this project's own sample
corpus, \texttt{data/raw/\_Samples/}: six files split across the three
sources, small enough to check into the repository itself. The same
extraction and acceptance code applies unchanged at whatever scale the
corpus is eventually grown to; only the counts below are specific to
this sample.

expose, not a flaw in any one of them.

\paragraph{Generating Input.}
Every accepted pattern's \texttt{Regex} is handed to
\texttt{generate\_sample} (\texttt{data/generate.rs}), which samples up
to \texttt{count} distinct positive matches directly from the pattern's
own structure -- walking each constructor, choosing an \texttt{Alt}
branch by its relative leaf count so a large character class is sampled
uniformly rather than biased toward whichever side of a left-folded chain
happens to be smaller, and taking a star between zero and a fixed four
iterations -- plus up to \texttt{negative\_count} negatives, each a
positive sample with its last character replaced by a character outside
the working alphabet. This replaces the three-tier
Best/Neutral/Worst difficulty split used earlier in this project with one
plain setting: generating a realistic match is the goal here, not
adversarial growth, which Section~\ref{sec:performance-analysis}'s own
benchmark families handle separately and deliberately. \texttt{dataset\_prepare}
calls this with \texttt{count = negative\_count = 5} for every pattern.

Suricata patterns additionally draw on \texttt{generate\_from\_content\_fields},
which assembles a candidate from the rule's own \texttt{content:} bytes
with a short sampled filler between consecutive fields -- real evidence
of what a triggering payload contains, not a structural guess.
SpamAssassin patterns additionally draw on
\texttt{generate\_from\_corpus\_dir} when a local ham/spam corpus is
configured, keeping only messages whose \emph{entire} content matches; without
the search padding Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchors} removed, this
finds far fewer candidates against a real corpus than it once did; kept
for the rare whole-message pattern it still finds, not removed, since it
costs nothing when it finds nothing.

\paragraph{Verification.}
Nothing \texttt{generate\_sample} or its source-specific companions
produce is trusted as written: \texttt{verify\_candidate}
(\texttt{data/prepare.rs}) re-derives each candidate's claimed outcome
against the real bit-coded parser, bounded by a 100\,000-node cap so a
pathological candidate is dropped rather than left to run unbounded, and
keeps the candidate only if the claim holds. A claimed negative is held
to a second, stronger standard: its failure must fall at or past
two-thirds of the input, since a candidate that fails on its first
character exercises the frontend barely at all. Verification therefore
drops some fraction of what was generated, and that fraction is not the
same across sources. Against this project's sample corpus:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\hline
Source & Candidates Generated & Candidates Verified & Dropped \\
\hline
Suricata & 513 & 404 & 109 (21.3\%) \\
SpamAssassin & 440 & 440 & 0 (0.0\%) \\
RegexLib & 1\,026 & 987 & 39 (3.8\%) \\
\hline
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{1\,979} & \textbf{1\,831} & \textbf{148 (7.5\%)} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Suricata's much larger drop is not noise: it is the only source using
\texttt{generate\_from\_content\_fields}, whose candidates come from a
rule's raw \texttt{content:} bytes rather than from the translated
pattern's own structure, so nothing guarantees they satisfy it, unlike a
\texttt{generate\_sample} candidate, which is sampled from that structure
directly and almost always verifies. SpamAssassin's and RegexLib's
smaller, nonzero drops come from the same two sources as any
\texttt{generate\_sample}-only pipeline: a corrupted negative that
happens to fail too early, and, rarely, a candidate against a pattern
expensive enough to hit the node cap.
