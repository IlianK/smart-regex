\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates the four parser variants implemented in
Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} and
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}. The four candidates are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the standard derivative-based parser (\texttt{deriv\_std},
        direct and loop variants),
  \item the bit-coded derivative-based parser (\texttt{deriv\_bc}),
  \item the standard partial-derivative parser (\texttt{pderiv\_std}),
  \item the bit-coded partial-derivative parser (\texttt{pderiv\_bc}).
\end{itemize}

Three aspects are covered.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-dataset} describes the two data sources the rest
of the chapter draws on: a real, human-authored corpus with its own
given examples, and a hand-curated synthetic set built specifically to
stress the parsers' asymptotic behaviour. Neither source needs a
candidate-input generator of the kind a corpus without given examples
would require; the two differ in how their inputs come to exist, not
in whether they are real measurements.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement} then checks, on the prepared corpus,
how often the POSIX parsers and the non-POSIX parsers agree on the
shape of the parse tree they build for the same pattern and input.
The main focus of the chapter, however, is
Section~\ref{sec:eval-performance}, which compares the four variants
on execution time and memory consumption, on both the synthetic set
and the real corpus.

The chapter is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-dataset} describes both datasets and the
pipeline that prepares the real one.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement} verifies parser agreement.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-performance} presents timing and memory results.
Section~\ref{sec:eval-discussion} interprets them.

All measurements are produced by binaries under \texttt{benches/} and
\texttt{examples/}, run with a fixed \texttt{--release} profile. The
corpus and synthetic measurements are plain reporting binaries run
once per source, for the reasons given in \texttt{BENCHMARKS.md}, not
Criterion targets.

\section{Dataset Preparation}
\label{sec:eval-dataset}

\subsection{Two datasets, not one}
\label{sec:eval-two-datasets}

The evaluation draws on exactly two pattern sources, chosen for two
different jobs.

\textbf{S2ELab} is a real, human-authored benchmark: 762 patterns,
each with a natural-language description and the author's own labeled
\texttt{matches}/\texttt{non\_matches}.\footnote{Published as
\emph{Re(gEx|DoS)Eval: Evaluating Generated Regular Expressions and
their Proneness to DoS Attacks}, ICSE-NIER 2024 (DOI:
10.1145/3639476.3639757); dataset at
\url{https://github.com/s2e-lab/RegexEval}. (TODO: replace this
footnote with a \texttt{references.bib} entry and a proper citation
once the full author list is confirmed from the paper directly.)} It
supplies
\emph{realistic} measurements --- how the four parser variants behave
on patterns a person actually wrote, with inputs that person actually
judged to match or not. Section~\ref{sec:eval-s2elab} explains why it
was chosen over the other candidate sources, and
Section~\ref{sec:eval-pipeline} describes the pipeline that turns it
into benchmark input.

\textbf{The pathological stress set} is the opposite kind of dataset:
113 hand-curated patterns, organised into categories by the specific
complexity mechanism each one is built to trigger, with no given
examples at all. Its job is \emph{controlled} measurement --- holding
every variable fixed except the one a category exists to stress
(input length, pattern size, or both) and sweeping it by a chosen,
reproducible amount. Section~\ref{sec:eval-pathological} describes it.

Both are read-only inputs to the benchmarks; neither is synthesised at
benchmark time. The distinction that matters for the rest of this
chapter is not ``real versus fake'' --- every pattern in both sets is
a real regular expression, benchmarked with the real parsers --- but
\emph{where the inputs come from}: S2ELab's inputs are given by a
human author and kept as given; the pathological set's inputs are
derived mechanically from a pattern's own stress parameter, because no
corpus of real-world patterns reliably contains an example of, say,
\texttt{(a+)+} swept across ten input lengths on request. Every table
in Sections~\ref{sec:eval-agreement}--\ref{sec:eval-performance} is
labeled by which of the two it was measured on, and the two are never
pooled into one figure.

Four other sources --- Snort/Suricata, SpamAssassin, RegexLib, and
Innovatorved --- were extracted and translated at an earlier stage of
this project and remain readable by the frontend's own diagnostic
tooling (\texttt{dataset\_stats}), but no longer feed either benchmark
dataset: Snort and SpamAssassin ship no given examples at all, and
RegexLib's and Innovatorved's labels carry no author guarantee. Using
either would have reintroduced exactly the heuristic input-generation
machinery Section~\ref{sec:eval-s2elab} explains this evaluation now
avoids.

\subsection{Why S2ELab}
\label{sec:eval-s2elab}

A benchmark corpus for a membership-deciding parser needs, for every
pattern, both a string that is claimed to match and one that is
claimed not to. A source that does not ship such pairs has to
manufacture them --- walk the pattern's own language and hope the
walk is representative, or corrupt a positive and hope the corruption
is a meaningful negative. Either way, the resulting input reflects the
sampler's own bias, not a judgement any person made about the
pattern. S2ELab is the one source available to this project that
avoids that problem entirely: every one of its 762 patterns ships
with the original author's own \texttt{matches} and
\texttt{non\_matches}, so the benchmark corpus is built from real
judgements end to end, with no generation step in between.

The second reason is specific to the full-string-matching model fixed
in Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}. Table~\ref{tab:source-match-intent}
compares all five candidate sources by what fraction of their accepted
patterns carry an unambiguous top-level \texttt{\textasciicircum...\textdollar}
wrap --- the one anchor shape Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}'s frontend
can recover losslessly under full-string matching
(Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchor-recovery}). S2ELab is the clear
outlier: 80.1\% of its accepted patterns carry that wrap, against
59.4\% for RegexLib and under a third for the three search-oriented
sources. A source's own authors write for a search-based engine by
default and mark full-match intent only when they mean it; S2ELab's
patterns are written to be tested against committed labels, so authors
mark intent far more often. That difference is exactly what this
project's full-string-matching model needs most.

\subsection{Anchors and full-match intent}
\label{sec:eval-anchors}

Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend} already fixed how anchors are treated: a
pattern is rejected if it contains \texttt{\textasciicircum} or
\texttt{\textdollar} anywhere except as a single top-level
\texttt{\textasciicircum...\textdollar} wrap around the whole pattern,
in which case the wrap is provably redundant under full-string
matching and is stripped before translation
(Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchor-recovery}). Nothing in this chapter
changes that mechanism; what this section adds is which of its two
outcomes a given pattern reaches, and what that means for which
results get reported.

\textbf{S2ELab.} Regular-expression authors commonly signal ``this
whole string is what I mean'' by wrapping a pattern in
\texttt{\textasciicircum...\textdollar}, and many S2ELab entries follow
that convention --- the worked example in
Section~\ref{sec:eval-worked-pipeline} is one. Every accepted S2ELab
pattern therefore falls into one of two groups: 463 carry the
top-level wrap and have it stripped by Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}'s
recovery mechanism, landing on the engine's native full-match reading
with no ambiguity about what was intended; the remaining 115 carry no
anchor at all and are translated unchanged, still matched under this
project's only semantics (full-string), but with no way to tell from
the pattern itself whether the author meant a full match or a search
that this project does not implement. Both groups are prepared and
written to the corpus, since dropping either at preparation time would
foreclose a methodological choice that belongs in the benchmarks, not
the pipeline.

That choice is made once, uniformly: every benchmark in this chapter
defaults to measuring the 463 full-match-wrapped patterns only,
because reporting a number for the remaining 115 would silently
assume a reading of the pattern its own author never committed to.
(Both groups remain fully available on opt-in, since the choice is a
default, not a dataset deletion.) Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement} and
Section~\ref{sec:eval-performance} therefore report S2ELab figures
over 463 patterns, not 578.

\textbf{The pathological stress set.} The same question does not
arise there in the first place. Every pathological pattern is
hand-written directly against the engine's own full-string-matching
model --- there is no external authoring convention to interpret,
because the patterns were authored for this project. None of them
contains an anchor: verified directly against
Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}'s own frontend, \texttt{\textasciicircum},
\texttt{\textdollar}, word boundaries, backreferences, lookaround, and
POSIX classes all hard-reject at parse or translate time, so none of
those constructs appear in the set at all. The pathological set
complies with Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}'s model by construction,
not by a recovery step applied after the fact.

\subsection{The S2ELab pipeline}
\label{sec:eval-pipeline}

No input is generated, derived, or heuristically assembled for
S2ELab. \texttt{run\_pipeline} turns the raw JSON into the benchmark
corpus in seven steps.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Extract.} Each entry's pattern and its author's own
        \texttt{matches}/\texttt{non\_matches} become one
        \texttt{ExtractedRule}.
  \item \textbf{Deduplicate.} A pattern byte-identical to one already
        seen this run is skipped; its fate is already decided.
  \item \textbf{Translate.} \texttt{core\_regex\_tagged}
        (Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}) parses and translates, applying
        the one anchor recovery described in
        Section~\ref{sec:eval-anchors}. A pattern the frontend rejects
        stops here.
  \item \textbf{Deduplicate examples.} A string appearing in both
        \texttt{matches} and \texttt{non\_matches} is kept once, as a
        positive --- the stronger claim.
  \item \textbf{Check labels.} Every surviving example is re-checked
        against the real translated \texttt{Regex}. A disagreement is
        dropped, not corrected: the dataset's claim did not hold under
        this project's own semantics.
  \item \textbf{Balance per pattern.} Described in
        Section~\ref{sec:eval-balance}.
  \item \textbf{Write.} Each surviving pair becomes one line of
        \texttt{prepared.jsonl} under \texttt{data/processed/s2-elab/},
        tagged with which of the two anchor groups it came from.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{A worked example through the pipeline}
\label{sec:eval-worked-pipeline}

One entry is traced end to end, so the later tables have a concrete
referent. S2ELab entry 100 (fields trimmed):

\begin{verbatim}
{
  "expression":
    "^(0[1-9]|1[0-2])\/((0[1-9]|2\d)|3[0-1])\/(19\d\d|200[0-3])$",
  "matches": ["12/31/2003", "01/01/1900", "11/31/2002", ...],
  "non_matches": ["1/1/2002", "01/01/02", "01/01/2004",
                  "12/123/4322", ...]
}
\end{verbatim}

The natural-language description behind this entry asks for a date
between January 1900 and December 2003 in \texttt{MM/DD/YYYY} form;
the pattern is the author's own attempt at that, anchors included.

\paragraph{Extract.} One \texttt{ExtractedRule} carrying the raw
expression and the two given lists unchanged.

\paragraph{Translate.} The pattern's top level is a single
\texttt{Concat} opening with \texttt{\textasciicircum} and closing with
\texttt{\textdollar}, so Section~\ref{sec:frontend-anchor-recovery}'s
recovery applies: both anchors are stripped before translation, and
the entry is tagged as a full-match pattern.

\paragraph{Check labels.} Every given example is run through the
translated \texttt{Regex}. The check is against the pattern's own
semantics, not against real-world correctness: \texttt{"11/31/2002"}
is listed as a \texttt{match}, and \emph{is} one under the translated
pattern, since the day group (\texttt{(0[1-9]|2\textbackslash
d)|3[0-1]}) accepts \texttt{31} regardless of which month group
matched --- the pattern never correlates the two, so it silently
accepts a November 31st that the calendar does not have. The label
check keeps this example, correctly: the author's claim and the
pattern's own language agree, and whether the pattern itself
encodes a real calendar is a question the check cannot and does not
answer. A different S2ELab entry's claim can still disagree with its
own translated pattern --- that is what
\texttt{inputs\_label\_mismatch} counts --- but this one does not
illustrate it.

\paragraph{Balance.} The entry's surviving matches and non-matches are
truncated to the shorter side's count, as Section~\ref{sec:eval-balance}
describes for the whole corpus.

\paragraph{Write.} The surviving pairs are appended to
\texttt{prepared.jsonl}, each line carrying the pattern, the input, the
claimed outcome, and the full-match tag.

This worked example is the entry the rest of the chapter refers to
when it discusses a label check in concrete terms, and it is also why
the check exists: a real, human-authored corpus contains real human
mistakes, and benchmarking against an uncorrected label would silently
fold that mistake into every parser's measured accuracy.

\subsection{Per-pattern balance}
\label{sec:eval-balance}

A pattern with 40 given matches and 2 given non-matches would, left
as given, contribute 40 positive measurements and 2 negative ones ---
not because matching is 20 times more representative for that
pattern, but because its author happened to write more positive
examples. After the label check, each usable pattern's longer side
(matches or non-matches) is truncated to its shorter side's count,
keeping the first surviving examples in their original order. A
pattern with fewer than one example on either side is excluded
entirely rather than balanced against zero.

Patterns are \emph{not} balanced against each other: a pattern with 50
surviving examples on each side keeps all 50, even though another
pattern in the same corpus has only one of each. An earlier version of
this pipeline did also truncate every pattern down to the single
rarest pattern's own count, so that one thinly-labeled pattern dragged
every other pattern in the corpus down to its level; on the real
S2ELab data this meant almost every pattern lost nearly all of its
given examples to equalize against a single outlier. That stage is
removed. Section~\ref{sec:eval-macro-average} explains what replaced
it, and why the replacement needs no truncation at all.

\subsection{Why macro-averaging, not more truncation}
\label{sec:eval-macro-average}

Removing the dataset-wide truncation reintroduces the imbalance it was
built to avoid, just one level up: once patterns are allowed to keep
however many examples they actually have, a pattern with 50 surviving
examples contributes 50 times as many individual measurements to a
flat, pooled average as a pattern with 1. A flat mean over every
measured input would then be dominated by whichever patterns happen
to be the most heavily labeled, which has nothing to do with how hard
any of them is to parse.

The fix is to change what is averaged, not to throw inputs away to
force every pattern to the same count. Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing}
and Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory} compute each pattern's own mean
first, then take the unweighted average of those per-pattern means ---
a \emph{macro-average}. Every pattern counts once in that average,
regardless of how many given examples it happened to ship with, and
no example is discarded to make the counts line up. The median,
99th-percentile, and maximum figures reported alongside the mean are
left as flat statistics over every individual measured input, since
they answer a different question on purpose: not ``how costly is the
typical \emph{pattern}'', which the macro-average answers, but ``how
bad does the single worst \emph{input} get'', where weighting by
pattern is not the relevant correction.

\subsection{The pathological stress set}
\label{sec:eval-pathological}

Section~\ref{sec:eval-two-datasets} introduced the second dataset as a
hand-curated, template-generated set rather than a corpus. This
section describes how it is built and what it is for.

A real corpus answers ``how do the parsers behave on patterns people
actually write''; it does not reliably answer ``how does
\texttt{deriv\_std}'s state size grow as input length doubles'',
because no real author sets out to write ten variants of
\texttt{(a+)+} at ten chosen lengths. The pathological set exists to
answer exactly that second kind of question: each row is a single,
deliberately chosen regular expression, built to isolate one specific
complexity mechanism (nested quantifiers, overlapping alternation,
bounded repetition over a star, and so on), together with a rule for
turning one swept parameter --- an input length $n$, a pattern size
$p$, or both --- into a concrete (pattern, input) pair at any scale
the evaluator chooses.

For most rows, only the input scales: the regular expression itself is
fixed (\texttt{(a+)+}, say), and $n$ repeats of a character are fed
through it. A smaller number of rows need the \emph{pattern} itself to
scale --- the family that forces full enumeration of a near-miss tail,
for instance, is only pathological because its quantified group is
repeated some number of times, so that repeat count has to be the
swept parameter, substituted directly into the pattern template rather
than only into the input. Both cases reuse the same small token
language: \texttt{\{N\}}/\texttt{\{P\}} inside an input specification
is a bare number; inside a pattern template, \texttt{\{\{N\}\}} keeps
the regular expression's own literal brace pair around that number so
that, for example, a template written as \texttt{(a?)\{\{N\}\}a\{\{N\}\}}
becomes the concrete pattern \texttt{(a?)\{20\}a\{20\}} at $n=20$.

113 patterns are organised into 12 categories (Table~\ref{tab:patho-categories}),
named by the specific mechanism each one stresses rather than by
source. Each category carries its own recommended sweep, calibrated
against a real measured run rather than chosen a priori: a routine,
un-overridden benchmark pass is meant to actually cross from ``every
parser measures in microseconds'' to ``the standard derivative parser
is gone'' within that category's own default points, not stop short
of the transition the way an arbitrarily small sweep would.

\subsection{Real and synthetic, kept separate}

The two datasets described above answer different questions and are
never pooled into one figure. S2ELab (572 usable patterns, 13{,}248
real given inputs, all of it a real author's own judgement) answers
how the four parser variants behave on regular expressions people
actually write. The pathological set (113 hand-curated patterns, 917
test cases under each category's own default sweep, scalable to
millions of characters for a specific reproduction) answers how each
variant's cost actually grows as a chosen stress parameter is swept,
something no corpus of real patterns reliably exercises on request.
Every table in the sections that follow names which of the two it was
measured on.

\section{Parser Agreement}
\label{sec:eval-agreement}

Every case in the prepared corpus already carries a verified outcome,
so membership is a precondition rather than something this section
establishes. The question here is the shape of the parse tree. On a
matching case, the POSIX parsers and the non-POSIX parsers may build
different trees --- the same input can admit more than one valid
decomposition --- but some patterns and inputs leave neither policy a
choice, forcing both onto the same structure. This section measures
how often that happens across the corpus.

%
%
%

\subsection{Method}

The check is implemented in \texttt{demo\_parser\_agreement}. For every
prepared case, all five parsers --- \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec},
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}, \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{pderiv\_std},
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} --- run on the same (pattern, input) pair.

Two conditions are checked as preconditions. First, every parser
returns the case's verified membership outcome. Second, within each
family the parsers agree with each other on the tree: the three POSIX
variants build the same tree, and the two non-POSIX variants build the
same tree. A violation of either condition is a bug, not a
measurement.

The measured quantity is cross-family tree agreement: of the cases
where both families matched and were internally consistent, how many
produced the same tree. The two families apply different
disambiguation policies, so a difference is expected where the input
admits several decompositions; a match where the input admits only one
forces both families onto the same structure.

Each parser call runs in a subprocess so that a stack overflow or an
infinite loop is contained. A 2-second wall-clock timeout kills the
process. Timeouts and crashes are counted separately from tree-shape
outcomes, since neither produces a result to compare.

The two datasets are run through this check separately, with separate
binaries, and never pooled into one row: \texttt{demo\_parser\_agreement}
against the 463 full-match-wrapped S2ELab patterns (the same default
scope as every other benchmark in this chapter,
Section~\ref{sec:eval-anchors}), and \texttt{demo\_parser\_agreement\_pathological}
against the 113-row pathological set.

\begin{verbatim}
cargo build --release --example demo_parser_agreement_worker
cargo run --release --example demo_parser_agreement
cargo run --release --example demo_parser_agreement_pathological
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Discussion}

To be completed after both runs. Expected reading, for each dataset
independently: no precondition violations (all of \emph{Mem.},
\emph{POSIX}, and \emph{non-POSIX} at zero), timeouts and crashes
nonnegative, and the \emph{Same} / \emph{Diff} split showing how often

\emph{Diff} is not a bug: it reflects a difference in disambiguation
policy between the two families, not in the underlying matching. The
pathological set's rows are authored directly in full-match form with
no anchor to strip (Section~\ref{sec:eval-anchors}), so its row carries
no anchor-handling precondition that the S2ELab row does not already
share; the two rows are expected to differ mainly in how often
\emph{Diff} occurs, since the pathological set's alternation-heavy
categories (B, E, F) are deliberately built to be ambiguous, while most
S2ELab patterns are not.

\section{Performance Analysis}
\label{sec:eval-performance}

The four parsers are compared on two axes: execution time
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing}) and memory consumption
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory}). Both axes are measured on synthetic
inputs first, since synthetic families isolate a single cost, and on the
corpus second, since the corpus is the environment the parsers will
encounter in practice.

\subsection{Cost drivers}
\label{sec:eval-cost-drivers}

Before the measurements, it is worth separating the properties of a
corpus that make the four parsers cost something. Three are
distinguishable, and each is exercised to a different degree by the
corpus of Section~\ref{sec:eval-dataset} and by the synthetic
families of Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing}.

\textbf{Input length.} The two standard derivative parsers retain
every intermediate expression until the backward pass reconstructs
the tree, so their live state grows with the number of characters
consumed. The bit-coded and partial-derivative parsers do not carry
that history. A corpus of long inputs separates the two groups; a
corpus of short inputs does not, however many entries it contains.

\textbf{Pattern structure.} Translation size and derivative cost
scale with the pattern's own node count. Deeply nested groups,
alternation over many branches, and repeated classes inflate the
translated \texttt{Regex}, and every parser pays for that size on
every input. This driver is independent of input length: a short
input against a large pattern can cost more than a long input against
a small one. It also splits into two phases this evaluation measures
separately, since they are paid at different times and by different
code: \emph{translation} (the PCRE string into the translated
\texttt{Regex}, \texttt{core\_regex} in Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend}),
measured once per distinct pattern by
\texttt{bench\_corpus\_translate}/\texttt{bench\_pathological\_translate};
and the \emph{parser's own derivative loop} over an already-translated
\texttt{Regex}, measured per (pattern, input) pair by the timing and
memory benches below. A pattern can be cheap to translate and
expensive to derive, or the reverse --- the pathological categories
\textbf{C}, \textbf{D}, \textbf{J}, and \textbf{K} are specifically
built around the first phase (bounded-repetition unrolling and
large-tree construction in \texttt{core\_regex} itself), while
\textbf{A}, \textbf{B}, \textbf{E}, \textbf{F}, and \textbf{H} are
built around the second.

\textbf{Ambiguity.} When a single input admits more than one valid
decomposition of the pattern, the parsers keep more state alive and
must eventually choose among the surviving trees. The choice is
governed by the parser family's disambiguation policy
(Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} versus
\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}), and the two families are
visible on exactly those (pattern, input) pairs where the policies
diverge. Ambiguity is a property of a pattern, not of a corpus; the
pathological categories built around overlapping or prefix-sharing
alternation (\textbf{B}, \textbf{E}, \textbf{F}) are what isolate it
at a controlled scale.

Not every cost is a driver. There is also a fixed per-call cost paid
once per (pattern, input) pair regardless of length, structure, or
ambiguity: allocating the initial residual, setting up the derivation
loop, and constructing the parse tree from the final nullable
expression. This cost is why the four parsers differ even on the
corpus's own shortest, simplest entries, where none of the three
drivers above is active enough to matter.

The pathological categories of Section~\ref{sec:eval-pathological} are
each shaped to isolate one of these costs at a scale no real corpus
reliably reaches; the corpus tables that follow mix all three, and
reading them without this separation makes the numbers hard to
interpret. S2ELab's patterns are short, human-authored rules with
modest structural complexity and short given inputs, so it exercises
all three drivers only mildly --- which is exactly why a second,
deliberately extreme dataset exists at all (Section~\ref{sec:eval-two-datasets}).

backward-pass reconstruction is not separable from its forward pass the
way \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s decode step is, and the partial-derivative
variants build their tree incrementally as they go rather than in a
separate final step.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:eval-memory}

\subsubsection{Allocation counters}

\texttt{bench\_corpus\_memory} replaces the system allocator with a
wrapper that counts allocated bytes, and reports three figures per
parser:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{total bytes}: every allocation during the whole
        corpus pass, including freed and re-allocated bytes.
        Measures churn.
  \item \textbf{average bytes per entry}: a macro-average, the same
        correction as \texttt{mean} in Table~\ref{tab:corpus-timing}
        (Section~\ref{sec:eval-macro-average}) --- each pattern's own
        average is taken first, then those per-pattern averages are
        averaged unweighted.
  \item \textbf{max peak}: the highest live-byte high-water mark seen
        on any single entry. Measures worst-case memory. Unlike the
        other two figures, this one is a flat, pooled-over-every-entry
        maximum, not a macro-average.
\end{itemize}

Like \texttt{bench\_corpus\_time}, it is a plain reporting binary and
reads the same two environment variables, \texttt{BENCH\_PATTERN\_LIMIT}
and \texttt{BENCH\_MATCH\_INTENT}.
Allocation counting sees heap traffic only: each worker's call-thread
stack is invisible to it, so a pattern whose cost is dominated by
recursion depth rather than allocation would under-report here --- the
figures below are heap numbers, not a general memory bound.

\begin{verbatim}
cargo bench --bench bench_corpus_memory
BENCH_PATTERN_LIMIT=5   cargo bench --bench bench_corpus_memory
BENCH_MATCH_INTENT=both cargo bench --bench bench_corpus_memory
\end{verbatim}

Three patterns are visible.

First, on \texttt{a*} the \texttt{deriv\_std} column grows linearly in
$n$, while the other three are bounded. This is the cost of the two-pass
structure: the standard parser keeps every intermediate expression alive
until the backward pass reconstructs the tree, so its state size is a
function of input length. The bit-coded and partial-derivative variants
do not carry a history, so their state does not grow.

Second, on \texttt{(a+(b+ab))*}, all four grow, but at very different
rates. At $n=16$, \texttt{deriv\_std} has grown to 9179 nodes,
\texttt{deriv\_bc} to 2303, and the two partial-derivative variants to
2560 and 2048. The gap between the two derivative-based variants is the
cost of carrying reconstruction information through each step, which the
bit-coded variant does not.

Third, the two partial-derivative variants stay close to each other
across both expressions. Their live state is the frontier of residuals,
not a single expression; the accumulation is linear in the number of
distinct residuals reachable, which is bounded by the pattern's own size.

\texttt{demo\_simp\_branches} shows why simplification never fires its
$r + r = r$ rule on \texttt{(a+(b+ab))*}. Every step has \emph{distinct}
branches by bit-annotation even when the structural shape is identical.
This is a property of the bit-coded representation, not a deficiency of
the simplifier: the branches genuinely differ, and merging them would
lose the reconstruction information that makes single-pass decoding
possible.

\subsubsection{Peak memory on the corpus}

The max-peak column from Table~\ref{tab:memory} answers whether any
S2ELab pattern causes a peak that would be infeasible on a production
machine. At full corpus scale the answer is no: the highest
single-entry peak is $582.4$\,MB, for the two standard derivative
variants, well within the memory of a typical workstation; the other
three parsers peak in single-digit megabytes. S2ELab's given examples
are short enough that none of its 10\,888 entries approaches a
memory-infeasible regime for any of the five parsers.

That is a property of this corpus's own input lengths, not a general
bound on the standard construction: Table~\ref{tab:patho-fig7}'s
pathological-scale figures, on inputs several orders of magnitude
longer, show the same two-pass retention pushing both standard
variants to crash or OOM already at one million characters, while the
bit-coded and partial-derivative variants keep growing smoothly into
the low gigabytes out to ten million --- large, but a controlled,
roughly linear growth rather than the standard variants' outright
failure.

\section{Discussion of Results}
\label{sec:eval-discussion}

\subsection{Cost of the standard construction}

The two standard derivative parsers (\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} and
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}) are consistently the slowest and
memory-heaviest of the four. This is a direct consequence of the two-pass
structure described in Chapter~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}: every
intermediate expression is stored, so the whole derivation sequence stays
alive until the backward pass completes. On \texttt{a*} input of length
$n$, the standard parser's state size grows linearly in $n$, while the
bit-coded and partial-derivative parsers stay constant.

On the full S2ELab corpus the two standard variants are close in mean
time (Table~\ref{tab:corpus-timing}: $7.379$\,ms against $6.902$\,ms)
but not identical, and the outcome split (Table~\ref{tab:corpus-timing-outcome})
shows where the difference actually lives: on matching entries ---
where a parse tree is built --- \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}'s total
($62.660$\,s) is measurably below \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec}'s
($67.913$\,s), while their non-match totals are almost the same. The
recursion-versus-iteration difference between the two is specifically
a cost of the matching path, not a flat per-call overhead.

%
%
%

\subsection{Cost of bit-coding}
\label{sec:eval-cost-bit-coding}

For the standard-derivative family, bit-coding is an unqualified win on
the full S2ELab corpus: \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s mean time ($1.653$\,ms) is
roughly $4$--$4.5\times$ below either standard variant's, and its peak
allocation ($5.1$\,MB) is about $114\times$ below theirs ($582.4$\,MB,
Table~\ref{tab:memory}). The trade-off it makes is that the forward
pass cannot simplify away redundant branches without losing
reconstruction information. The \texttt{demo\_simp\_branches} output
shows this clearly: the branch counts grow as expected but the
distinctness count stays at 1 after bit stripping, meaning every
branch is retained because it carries a different bit annotation. The
retention is not wasted: it is exactly what makes single-pass
reconstruction possible, and \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s own
encode/derive/decode split (Section~\ref{sec:eval-corpus-benchmarks})
shows that decode --- the step that cashes in that retained
information --- is close to free ($0.005$\,ms mean), so the retention
is not even an expensive one.

%
%
%

\subsection{Cost of partial derivatives}
\label{sec:eval-cost-partial-derivatives}

The two partial-derivative parsers are the fastest and most memory-light
of the five on the full S2ELab corpus, both clearly ahead of either
derivative-based variant. They split the residual into a set of states
rather than combining into one expression, which keeps each state small
and avoids the alternation growth that the standard derivative parser
suffers.

Within the pair, bit-coding's advantage is \emph{not} immediate the way
it is for the standard-derivative family above, and time and memory
cross over at different scales. On the full S2ELab corpus,
\texttt{pderiv\_std} is marginally ahead of \texttt{pderiv\_bc} on
every figure measured: mean time ($0.598$\,ms against $0.613$\,ms),
average bytes ($0.395$\,MB against $0.404$\,MB), and peak bytes
($2.5$\,MB against $3.8$\,MB) --- at S2ELab's short input lengths,
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s bit-tracking is pure overhead on both axes.
Memory crosses over first: already at one million characters
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-fig7}), \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s peak ($141$\,MB)
is below \texttt{pderiv\_std}'s ($233$\,MB), and it stays below at
every pathological scale point measured (five million: $778$\,MB
against $1.24$\,GB; ten million: $1.56$\,GB against $2.48$\,GB). Time
crosses over later: \texttt{pderiv\_bc} is still slower at one million
characters ($6.0$\,s against $5.3$\,s), roughly tied at five million
($26.7$\,s against $26.4$\,s), and only clearly faster at ten million
($53.4$\,s against $63.8$\,s). Bit-coding's saving for the
partial-derivative construction is a real but asymptotic effect that
has to be bought with a fixed per-step bit-tracking cost first --- and
that cost is amortised by input length before it is amortised by wall
time --- unlike the standard-derivative family above, where bit-coding
wins unconditionally because it removes an $O(n)$ liability outright
rather than trading one small constant for another.

The price of partial derivatives, independent of which variant, is
that they produce a non-POSIX parse tree rather than a POSIX one. This
is a specification difference, not an implementation deficiency, and
it is the reason the two families are compared separately in
Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement}: within each family, the two variants
must agree, but between families they are allowed to differ.

\subsection{When each parser is appropriate}

The four parsers are not interchangeable. Table~\ref{tab:choose-parser}
summarises the decision.

The frontend, not the parser, is the limiting factor for the small

\subsection{Threats to validity}

Three properties of the setup limit how far the results can be
generalised.

\emph{Corpus scope.} The one real corpus is S2ELab: human-authored
regular expressions from one dataset's own domain and convention of
writing a pattern, each already paired with its own author-chosen
match/non-match examples. A corpus drawn from a different domain, or
one assembled rather than author-paired with examples, might exhibit
different acceptance rates and different timing profiles. The default
scope within S2ELab is also a choice, not the whole dataset: every
table in this chapter measures only the 463 patterns a real author
wrapped in \texttt{\^{}...\$} (Section~\ref{sec:eval-anchors}), on the
grounds that an unwrapped pattern's intended matching scope is
genuinely ambiguous; a different, defensible choice there
(\texttt{BENCH\_MATCH\_INTENT=both}) would fold in the remaining 115
patterns and could shift the absolute numbers, though not the ordering
among parsers, which does not depend on which patterns are included.

\emph{The pathological set.} Each of its 113 rows is hand-curated to
isolate one specific complexity mechanism at a controlled scale
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-pathological}); it does not claim to represent
the distribution of pattern shapes any real author would write, only
to show how each parser's cost grows once that one mechanism is pushed
past where a real corpus would reliably exercise it. Most categories
sweep a single parameter in isolation, so a pattern that combines two
stress mechanisms at once --- deep nesting \emph{and} input length
\emph{and} ambiguity together, say --- is not directly represented,
even though each mechanism on its own is.

\emph{Measurement model.} \texttt{bench\_corpus\_memory} and
\texttt{bench\_pathological\_memory} measure allocation counts, not
wall-clock memory pressure. On systems with a garbage collector or a
page cache, the figures would interact with those subsystems in ways
this evaluation does not model. The evaluator runs on a single
machine, so the wall-clock figures in Table~\ref{tab:corpus-timing}
and Table~\ref{tab:patho-fig7} are CPU- and cache-specific. The S2ELab
timing and memory tables are drawn from the full corpus; the
pathological set's per-category table (Table~\ref{tab:patho-timing-smoke})
and most of its individually-swept representatives
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-fig-pending}) are not yet run at the scale they
are configured for, and the numbers reported for those rows should be
read as pending rather than as a claim about pathological-scale
behaviour until the full run lands.

\subsection{Summary}

On both timing and memory, the partial-derivative parsers dominate the
two standard-derivative parsers by a wide and consistent margin, on
both the real corpus and the pathological set. Within the
standard-derivative family, bit-coding dominates unconditionally: it
removes an $O(n)$ liability, so \texttt{deriv\_bc} is faster and
lighter than both \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} and \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}
at every scale measured. Within the partial-derivative family, the same
direction holds only asymptotically: \texttt{pderiv\_std} is
marginally faster and lighter than \texttt{pderiv\_bc} on the real
corpus's short inputs and at the low end of the pathological sweep,
and only at several million characters does \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s
bounded bit-vector state pull decisively ahead
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-cost-partial-derivatives}). The choice of
construction (standard vs. partial derivative) matters more than the
choice of variant (standard vs. bit-coded) in every case measured; the
choice of variant matters, but which way it cuts for the
partial-derivative family depends on how long the input is expected to
get.

All five parsers are expected to correctly classify every prepared
case on both datasets, consistent with the full test suite and the
cross-parser validation already performed while fixing the dedup and
bit-threading bugs of Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser}--\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser};
Table~\ref{tab:agreement}'s own \emph{Mem.}/\emph{POSIX}/\emph{non-POSIX}
columns, pending the formal run, are what confirms this claim at full
corpus and full pathological-set scale rather than only on the cases
exercised during development. The POSIX-vs-non-POSIX \emph{Diff}, when
it appears, is a specification difference rather than an
implementation bug.
