\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates the derivative and partial-derivative parsers
developed in Chapters~\ref{sec:derivative-parser} 
and \ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}. 
Using two datasets, 
it compares their execution time, memory consumption, and translation cost, 
as well as their match decisions and parse-tree shapes. 
All measurements were taken on an AMD Ryzen~5~5600 six-core processor
with 16\,GB of RAM, running Pop!\_OS 24.04 LTS.

%
%
%

\section{Candidates}
\label{sec:eval-candidates}

A candidate is a parser $p$ mapping an \emph{entry} $(r,w)$, 
consisting of a translated \texttt{Regex} $r$ 
and an input \texttt{String} $w$, 
to an optional \texttt{ParseTree} $pt$. 
All candidates share the interface:
\[
p :: (\mathit{Regex}, \mathit{String}) \to
     \mathit{Option\langle ParseTree\rangle}
\]
Candidates (Table~\ref{tab:eval-candidates})
are grouped into two families according 
to their disambiguation strategy.
All five parsers are expected to agree on membership.
So given an entry, they must either all accept or reject it.
\emph{Within family} the parsers are also expected to agree on parse-tree shape.
\emph{Across families} parse tree shape agreement is possible, but not required.

\section{Evaluation Criteria}
\label{sec:eval-criteria}

The evaluation considers \emph{performance}, 
measured in terms of execution time, memory consumption, and translation cost, 
and \emph{agreement} on membership decisions and parse-tree selection.

%
%
%

\subsection{Measurement Setup}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-setup}

Each entry runs in its own worker subprocess,
which can be killed unconditionally
if stuck in exponential derivation or approaching a $12\,\mathrm{GiB}$
memory cap.
This avoids the lack of safe thread termination in stable
Rust~\citep{rust-thread-joinhandle},
and allows the operating system to reclaim the worker's memory.
Workers use a fixed $256\,\mathrm{MiB}$ stack
to accommodate deeply nested patterns.
This stack is excluded from reported memory usage.
The per-entry timeout is $60\,\mathrm{s}$ for the S2ELab corpus and
the synthetic stress categories, and $120\,\mathrm{s}$ for the
pattern-size sweeps (E37, E38) and the representative-pattern rows
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-representative}), generous enough that
a timeout reflects an input the candidate cannot finish in reasonable
time at all, not an artifact of the measurement window.
Runs are classified as follows:
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\emph{Measured} & Worker exited successfully and reported a result. \\
\emph{Timed out} & Worker exceeded its per-entry timeout and was killed. \\
\emph{Crashed} & Worker exited non-zero or was killed by a signal. \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Classification of benchmark runs.}
\label{tab:eval-run-classes}
\end{table}

\subsection{Timing}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-timing}

Each entry $(r,w)$ is timed once, without warm-up or repetition,
with translation being timed separately
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-translation}).
So timings cover only the parser call $p(r,w)$, split into four
phases for all five candidates, positionally aligned across all of
them but not identical in what each one measures.
\emph{Setup} is work done before any character of $w$ is consumed:
for \texttt{deriv\_bc} this is a genuine encode, building the initial
annotated state via \texttt{internalize}. \texttt{pderiv\_bc} builds
no such state, so its own \emph{setup} instead times the initial clone
into the starting frontier plus the accumulated cost of
\texttt{flatten\_alt} tagging \texttt{Alt} branches with bit prefixes
across the whole parse -- the per-character equivalent of what
\texttt{internalize} does once, upfront
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory-representative}).
\texttt{pderiv\_std} has neither an annotated state nor bit tagging, so
its own \emph{setup} is just that initial clone, timed as its own
phase purely so the three non-recursive candidates' breakdowns stay
comparable; calling it an ``encode'' the way \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s is
would overclaim what it does.
\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} and \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} never build a
frontier or wrapper before deriving, so their own \emph{setup} is
always $0$.
\emph{Derive} is the per-character stepping loop, for all five.
\emph{Reconstruct} produces the final \texttt{ParseTree} from whatever
witness survived: a genuine decode for \texttt{deriv\_bc} and
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}, interpreting an accumulated bit string;
\texttt{Inj::apply}, applying the composed chain of injection closures
built up during \emph{derive}, for \texttt{pderiv\_std}; and the
backward injection pass itself for \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} and
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}, which build the parse tree directly during
that pass rather than applying a separately composed closure.
\emph{Teardown} is the cost of dropping the \texttt{ParseTree} this
call returns, once its caller is done with it; it is measured
identically for all five candidates by the harness itself, outside any
parser function, since none of them controls what its own caller does
with the tree it hands back.
These four phases always sum to the call's true wall-clock cost, with
nothing left unaccounted.
Under the heap-capped setting used for every benchmark except the one
in Section~\ref{sec:eval-stack-depth}, \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} and
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} exhaust that cap at the same inputs and
report almost identical times, since both are limited by retained
derivation state rather than by recursion depth; the results below
therefore report \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} alone as the standard
construction's representative, with \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} reported
separately in Section~\ref{sec:eval-stack-depth}, the one measurement
that isolates recursion depth itself as the limiting factor.
Four statistics are reported, aggregated differently for the two datasets:
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Statistic} & \textbf{Pooling} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\emph{Mean} & Per pattern (flat on synthetic data) & Equal weight per pattern \\
\emph{Median} & Per entry & Typical entry \\
\emph{99th percentile} & Per entry & Tail of the distribution \\
\emph{Maximum} & Per entry & Single worst entry \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Timing statistics and their aggregation.}
\label{tab:eval-timing-statistics}
\end{table}

All four statistics are lower bounds 
on the corresponding execution times, 
because timed-out and crashed entries are excluded.
A separate \emph{wall} column measures the full benchmark loop per pattern, 
including process-spawn and pipe overhead excluded from individual timings.
It captures total harness cost but is not suitable for comparing candidates.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-memory}

Memory is measured with a counting allocator in each worker,
reset immediately before $p(r,w)$ to isolate allocations made during the parser call. 
It tracks requested and live bytes:
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\emph{Max live peak} & Highest live-byte count during the call; peak heap demand. \\
\emph{Total allocated} & Sum of requested bytes over the call, a proxy for allocator pressure. \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Memory metrics collected by the counting allocator.}
\label{tab:eval-memory-metrics}
\end{table}

Total allocated is always at least the peak, 
since it includes memory freed during the call. 
Frequent allocation and deallocation can therefore increase total allocation 
and timing costs even when peak usage remains low. 
Since the allocator measures heap usage, 
and not the worker stack (Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}),
stack-intensive parsers may appear inexpensive in these measurements.

\subsection{Translation}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-translation}

Translation depends only on the source pattern, 
and is independent of the input,
so each distinct pattern is translated once in the main process. 
The caps: \emph{Bound cap}, \emph{Depth cap} and \emph{Node cap} 
bind translation time and memory by rejecting patterns 
that would require excessive recursion or unrolling. 
Translation cost is measured using four quantities:
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Measures} \\
\hline
\emph{Time} & Wall-clock duration of the translation call. \\
\emph{Total bytes} & Total memory allocated during translation. \\
\emph{Peak bytes} & Highest number of live allocated bytes during the call. \\
\emph{Nodes} & Node count of the resulting \texttt{Regex}. \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Translation cost metrics.}
\label{tab:eval-translation-metrics}
\end{table}

The allocator is reset before translation and read immediately afterward, 
excluding node counting and debug rendering.
The four timing statistics (Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-timing})
are calculated directly over patterns, with no macro-averaging
since each contributes one measurement.

\subsection{Stack Depth}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-stack-depth}

This measurement determines the input length and translation
limits at which stack exhaustion occurs. 
Both standard derivative parsers use the default thread stack 
(8\,MiB on Linux), 
but consume it differently: 
\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} uses one call-stack frame per input character, whereas 
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} stores residuals in a \texttt{Vec}.
Each probe has a $15\,\mathrm{s}$ timeout, 
increased from the default $2\,\mathrm{s}$ 
to allow slow derivations to reach stack exhaustion. 
No heap cap is applied, 
making a signal kill more likely to indicate stack exhaustion 
than an out-of-memory condition (Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}).
Timeouts indicate that derivation became too expensive first 
and are kept as negative controls.

A doubling search is used to determine 
at which input length a stack overflow is reached. 
The search starts from a small input length 
and doubles the input after each successful probe until the worker fails. 
The search brackets the last successful value and the first failure. 
The same method is used to find the two translation caps,
mentioned at the end of Chapter~\ref{sec:frontend-bounds}.

\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Target} & \textbf{Quantity doubled} & \textbf{Failure condition} \\
\hline
\emph{Parser stack} & Input length & Parser fails to complete \\
\emph{Bound cap} & Repetition bound & Translating the bound exhausts the stack \\
\emph{Depth cap} & Nesting depth of \texttt{(?:...)} groups & Translating the pattern exhausts the stack \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Doubling searches used to determine stack and translation limits.}

\subsection{Parse Agreement}
\label{sec:eval-criteria-agreement}

Parse agreement is evaluated on entries with a known expected membership result, 
written $(r, w, e)$ where $r$ is the translated \texttt{Regex}, 
$w$ is the input string, and $e \in \{\mathit{true}, 
\mathit{false}\}$ is the expected match result.
An expected parse tree is not provided. 
Each candidate processes the same entry and records:
\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\emph{Timeout or crash} & The candidate exceeded the timeout or was killed by a signal. \\
\emph{Membership} & Match ($e = \mathit{true}$) or no-match ($e = \mathit{false}$) decision. \\
\emph{Parse tree} & The parse tree returned, when $e = \mathit{true}$. \\
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Results recorded for each candidate during parse-agreement testing.}
\label{tab:eval-agreement-fields}
\end{table}

A timeout or crash removes that candidate from the comparison.
\emph{Membership} is compared across all surviving candidates.
Any disagreement amongst parsers with $e$ is a bug.
When $e = \mathit{true}$ 
and every surviving candidate returned a \emph{parse tree},
they can be compared.
\emph{Within families}, parsers must produce identical parse trees
because they share a disambiguation policy. 
Any disagreement indicates a bug.
\emph{Across families}, parsers may produce identical parse trees,
but are not required too, since their disambiguation policies differ.

\subsection{S2ELab}
\label{sec:eval-s2elab}

The dataset by \citet{s2elab-regexeval-dataset} 
contains 762 human-authored patterns. 
Each pattern has a natural-language description 
and lists of labeled matching and non-matching inputs. 
The number of each varies per pattern, 
so the corpus is not balanced between the two.
The source patterns conventionally use \texttt{\textasciicircum} 
and \texttt{\textdollar} as beginning- and end-of-input anchors. 
A pattern surrounded by anchors requires a full-string match, 
where a pattern without them is intended for substring matching. 
As described in Section~\ref{sec:frontend-rejected}, 
substring matching is not supported and anchors are rejected constructs.
However, entries with fully anchored patterns can still be used by stripping them.
Since the parsers perform full-string matching,
the stripped pattern accepts exactly 
the inputs intended by the original anchored pattern.
So the benchmarks only use the 463 accepted patterns after anchor stripping.
Figure~\ref{fig:s2elab-pipeline} shows this 
as part of the data preparation, that turns one example pattern into an $entry$.

Filtering unsupported constructs excludes 292 S2ELab patterns,
potentially introducing selection bias into the 463-pattern benchmark scope.
To assess this, the kept and excluded subsets are compared.
Their mean counts of matching and non-matching inputs are similar
(13.0/12.0 versus 12.6/11.9 per pattern), as are their mean input
lengths (17.3/8.4 versus 17.8/9.2 characters).
However, the excluded patterns themselves
are longer on average (95 versus 70 characters),
consistent with their unsupported constructs.
Thus, filtering changes the regex characteristics but shows no
substantial shift in the measured input characteristics.

However, differences in regex structure may still affect runtime
and memory behavior, so the results cannot be assumed to
generalize to the excluded patterns or other corpora.

\subsection{Synthetic Stress Set}
\label{sec:eval-pathological}

The synthetic set contains 112 patterns, generated with the
assistance of a large language model and manually verified,
designed to stress the parsers and investigate
how their costs grow with input length and pattern size.
All patterns are intended for full-string matching. 
Each row defines a base pattern 
and a base input in terms of the input length $n$, the pattern size $p$, or both, 
and a \emph{sweep} varies one of these parameters over a range of values, 
recording a measurement at each. 
A row beginning with ID \texttt{P} fixes the pattern and grows $n$,
measuring cost against input size,
while an \texttt{E} row fixes the input and grows $p$,
measuring cost against pattern size.
Each sweep value yields two entries, not one: one on an input the
pattern matches and one on a related input it does not, so cost is
measured on both outcomes.
Table~\ref{tab:sweep-samples} shows one row of each type at two
sweep values, on their matching side.

The 112 patterns are grouped by the complexity mechanism they test. 
Each category has its own sweep range, 
chosen to take measured costs from microseconds 
towards a timeout or crash.
Each swept value contributes two entries, 
one matching and one non-matching, 
with the latter using a pattern-specific
near-miss formed by adding or removing one boundary character.
Entries are pooled across each category's patterns,
and every entry is run once, without repeated trials. 
Table~\ref{tab:patho-categories}
gives the resulting pattern and entry counts. 
For example, category A has 160 entries from 16 patterns, each swept
over $n \in \{10, 20, 30, 1\,000, 10\,000\}$ at $p=100$ and tested
with both outcomes ($16 \times 5 \times 2 = 160$).

The patterns were generated with the assistance of a large
language model using category-specific prompts that specified
the intended stress mechanism and alphabet.
Table~\ref{tab:patho-prompts} reconstructs the representative
generation requirements for each category.
The generated patterns and test inputs were subsequently
reviewed and validated against actual parser behaviour.
Several incorrectly labelled matching and non-matching inputs
were corrected, and the input-size ranges in
Table~\ref{tab:patho-categories} were calibrated empirically
to produce meaningful stress tests.

Categories A, B, C, D, and F primarily use small alphabets
with repeated characters such as \texttt{a}, \texttt{b},
and \texttt{x}. This was a deliberate choice to increase
ambiguity in how an input can be divided across quantifiers,
alternatives, or repeated groups. Repeated characters allow
multiple branches to consume the same input, while a more
varied alphabet would often distinguish branches earlier
and reduce the intended stress.

Category E mainly uses two-character alphabets to examine
overlapping prefixes and competing parse structures.
Categories H, I, J, K, and L target additional properties,
including adjacent repetitions, input-length scaling,
pattern-size growth, and construct coverage. Their alphabets
and input structures were selected according to the
specific mechanism under investigation.

\subsection{Stack Depth}
\label{sec:eval-stack-depth}

The recursive and iterative forms 
run on the operating system's default stack of 8\,MiB, 
without a memory cap and a timeout of 15\,s.
using the doubling search described in Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-stack-depth}.

P01 and P30 are negative controls.
On both, derivative computation is expensive enough
that the 15\,s timeout fires before either form reaches its limit,
at $n=16$ and $n=64$, respectively.
P01 fails first because its nested quantifier
makes each derivative step near-exponential.
P30 fails later because ten adjacent \texttt{(\textbackslash w+)}
groups produce a product of stars with polynomial growth.
In both cases, the timeout ends the sweep before the input
grows far enough to expose a stack limit.
These controls show that small $n$ does not imply stack exhaustion
for arbitrary patterns.
E51 isolates stack using the single literal \texttt{x} as pattern,
which cannot match an input of \texttt{a}s.
So every entry mismatches on the first character,
keeping the residual small and derivative cost independent of input length.
Both forms keep all $n$ intermediate derivatives
between the forward and backward passes,
differing only in where the sequence is stored.
The recursive parser stores it implicitly,
one derivative per stack frame,
so stack usage grows with the input until it crashes.
The iterative form stores the sequence explicitly
in a heap-allocated \texttt{Vec},
and the sweep ends only when the 15\,s timeout fires.
Thus, the iterative form has no stack growth to compare
but avoids the recursive form's stack limit by construction.
This measurement identifies the input length
at which stack usage becomes the limiting factor for the recursive form.
The iterative form is therefore more crash-resistant,
even when derivative computation is cheap.
Its overall advantage depends on which cost matters more,
as examined in the memory measurements
in Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory}.

On the S2ELab corpus (Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-s2elab}), where the
$12\,\mathrm{GiB}$ heap cap used elsewhere makes both forms heap-bound
rather than stack-bound, they crash on the identical 18 entries and
differ mainly in how much cumulative time iteration saves:
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}'s matching-entry total is $550.881\,\mathrm{s}$
against \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec}'s $615.036\,\mathrm{s}$, while their
non-matching totals are almost identical ($16.031\,\mathrm{s}$ and
$15.833\,\mathrm{s}$). Iteration's advantage therefore shows up mainly
on matching inputs, not on rejections. Because the two forms crash and
time out together everywhere outside this stack-depth probe, the
comparisons from here on use \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} alone as the
standard construction's representative, without repeating
\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} in every subsequent table.

\paragraph{Categories A, B, E} show the expected behavior.
The partial-derivative variants are faster than the derivative variants,
though within their own family they are nearly tied on median
(0.23--0.26, 2.11--2.18, and 3.42--3.78\,ms in A, B, and E).
\texttt{deriv\_bc} follows behind (0.28, 2.60, 4.66\,ms).
All three categories let the same input be consumed by many decompositions,
so their residual expressions grow with each step and their size drives the cost.
While \texttt{deriv\_bc} simplifies after each step and keeps a single expression,
the partial-derivative parsers keep a deduplicated set instead.
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} is far more exposed to this growth:
rather than the slowdown surfacing in its timing, it drives
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} into the
stack overflow of Section~\ref{sec:eval-stack-depth}, crashing on 88 of A's
160, 82 of B's 150, and 38 of E's 80 entries.

\paragraph{Category C} excludes every parser on a large fraction
of its 56 entries: \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} crashes on 30, and
none of the other three time out.
Its patterns wrap a star body in a bounded repetition,
so unrolling produces up to ten copies of an already ambiguous subexpression
that every parser must then process within the measured call.
Here the partial-derivative pair falls behind \texttt{deriv\_bc},
with medians of 178.74 and 195.34\,ms against 70.76,
while \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}'s median (3.86\,ms) is lowest of the four --
but it is computed over only the 26 entries that avoid the crash,
and describes that easier subset rather than a faster parser.

\paragraph{Category F} is the one case in A--H where \texttt{deriv\_bc}
times out rather than crashing or completing cleanly, excluded
on 12 of 70 entries against none for either partial-derivative variant.
Its patterns end in a literal that fails late,
so every entry must consume the full input before checking it,
leaving no opportunity for early rejection.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} is no longer faster on its own 58 completed entries
either: its mean of 1\,803.54\,ms and median of 44.54\,ms are both higher
than the partial-derivative pair's, which complete all 70 entries at a
mean of 377.49 and 518.77\,ms and a median of 8.21 and 10.22\,ms.
The partial-derivative pair is therefore both the more robust choice
here and the faster one.

\paragraph{Category D} is where \texttt{deriv\_bc}
is clearest ahead, with every parser measuring all 40 entries so the
means compare directly: 164.27\,ms against 2\,086.02 and 2\,340.51\,ms
for the partial-derivative pair, an order of magnitude lower.
D's patterns are unrolled bounded repetitions,
so consuming a prefix leaves a suffix of the unrolled chain,
and different prefix lengths leave suffixes of different lengths.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} simplifies after each step,
which rewrites equivalent residuals into a common form
so that identical ones merge and the expression stays small.
But the partial-derivative parsers only drop residuals that are already equal,
so suffixes of different lengths remain separate elements,
each stepped at every character, and the set grows with the pattern.
The advantage of \texttt{deriv\_bc} therefore comes from the simplification
its encoding uses, not from the encoding itself.

\paragraph{Category H} is a weaker version of D's outcome.
All three of \texttt{deriv\_bc} and the partial-derivative pair measure
every one of H's 28 entries, while \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} crashes on exactly
half (14).
H's patterns are chains of adjacent unbounded groups,
such as \texttt{(.+)(.+)}\(\ldots\),
so split points multiply across group boundaries.
The residual set carried by the partial-derivative parsers
grows with the number of groups.
Each residual is processed at every character,
whereas \texttt{deriv\_bc} carries only one simplified expression.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} is the fastest of the three on both statistics here:
on mean, 359.70 against 880.34 and 1\,000.81\,ms; on median, 66.44
against 100.75 and 125.06\,ms.

\paragraph{Overall} \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} crashes on a large share
of entries in every category except D, where no parser fails; \texttt{deriv\_bc}
and the partial-derivative pair are far more robust throughout, crashing
nowhere in A--H and timing out only on \texttt{deriv\_bc} in F and on all
three in I, J, and K (Table~\ref{tab:patho-timing-size}).
The partial-derivative variants outperform
\texttt{deriv\_bc} on median in A, B, E, F, J, and K.
J reverses on mean: \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s 47.45\,ms is far below
\texttt{pderiv\_std}'s 3\,662.92 and \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s 3\,093.44,
pulled up by a few entries near J's own maxima (35\,048.00 and
30\,005.13\,ms, against \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s 220.76) rather than by
typical behavior.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} leads outright in C, D, and H, where it carries a single
simplified expression rather than a set of residuals.
In D, simplification merges residuals
that are equivalent but not syntactically equal.
In H, the single expression avoids the per-element cost of a residual set
that grows with the number of groups.
In I, all three exclude exactly 4 of 60 entries to timeout, with no
difference in robustness between them; the partial-derivative pair is
nonetheless far faster on the entries it completes, with a median
around a fifth of \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s
(195.11 and 193.99\,ms against 991.08).

Within the partial-derivative pair,
the mean favours \texttt{pderiv\_bc} in six of these eleven categories
(A, C, D, H, J, K), while the median favours it in eight (A, B, C, D,
H, I, J, K) -- a wider margin on typical-case performance than on the
mean. \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own per-character bit-tagging
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory-representative}) costs one allocation
per tagged bit, paid on every character regardless of how large the
entry eventually grows, so it weighs proportionally more on a typical,
modestly-sized entry's median than on the handful of largest entries
that dominate the mean. \texttt{pderiv\_std} leads on both statistics
only in E, F, and L.
The widest alternations, rows E37 and E38, agree with each other:
at their widest point, \texttt{pderiv\_bc} and \texttt{pderiv\_std}
are fastest (17.54 and 26.12\,ms on E37, 47.87 and 57.28\,ms on E38),
\texttt{deriv\_bc} comes second on both (39.73 and 94.46\,ms), and
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} trails on both, as Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e38}
shows for E38.

proceeds, independently of how either partial-derivative variant

and writing $X$ for the unchanging inner residual each step reduces to:
\[
\begin{array}{r@{\ }l@{\qquad\qquad}r@{\ }l}
\multicolumn{2}{c}{\texttt{a}\text{-repeated}} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\texttt{ab}\text{-repeated}} \\[2pt]
\mathit{ri}_1 \xrightarrow{\ \mathtt{a}\ } & []@X & \mathit{ri}_1 \xrightarrow{\ \mathtt{a}\ } & []@X \\
\mathit{ri}_2 \xrightarrow{\ \mathtt{a}\ } & [0,0,0]@X & \mathit{ri}_2 \xrightarrow{\ \mathtt{b}\ } & [0,1]@X \\

\end{array}
\]
Tracing \texttt{pderiv\_bc} with $B$ for the
pattern \texttt{a|b|ab} and states $S_0 = \{B^{*}\}$,

\[
\begin{array}{r@{\ }l@{\qquad\qquad}r@{\ }l}

S_1 \xrightarrow{\ \mathtt{a}\ } & S_1\ (\text{2 tagged}) & S_0 \xrightarrow{\ \mathtt{a}\ } & S_1\ (\text{2 tagged})
\end{array}
\]

\clearpage
These two derivations show two things.
For \texttt{deriv\_bc}, \texttt{internalize} tags the pattern as
$[0](a \mathbin{|} b) \oplus [1](a \cdot b)$ under the Star:
the outer alternation tags the branch ($[0]$ for \texttt{a|b}),
the inner alternation within \texttt{a|b} tags the literal
($[0]a \oplus [1]b$), 
and the Star tags whether it continues ($[0]$) or stops ($[1]$). 
Bits reach the root only when the nullable-Seq rule 
$\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:bderiv}) fires: 
once \texttt{a|b} is nullable,
\texttt{mkEpsBC} extracts its completed path 
and fuses it onto the Star's continuation $X$. 
Matching \texttt{a} makes \texttt{a|b} nullable after exactly one character, 
so the rule fires on every \texttt{a}, extracting all three tags at once.
Matching \texttt{b} instead resolves directly.
The inner \texttt{b} branch becomes $\varepsilon$ while \texttt{a} dies.
This is costing only the outer $[0]$ and inner $[1]$ ($2$ bits), 
without restarting the Star. 
The nullable-Seq rule only fires 
when \texttt{a|b} is \emph{already} nullable going in, 
which happens only right after a \emph{previous} \texttt{a}. 
On \texttt{ab}-repeated input every \texttt{a} is preceded 
by a \texttt{b}, which resets this, so the rule never fires: 
only the cheap $2$-bit \texttt{b}
step commits anything, once every two characters.
Since \texttt{fuse} and \texttt{simp} thread 
through that growing bit-list every step, 
\texttt{deriv\_bc}'s cost grows roughly three times 
as fast per character on P60 as on P61. 

For the partial-derivative pair,
the \texttt{pderiv\_bc} derivation above
tags both residuals in $S_1$ on every \texttt{a},
but only one residual on \texttt{ab}-repeated input
whenever it collapses to $S_0$.
At the largest sampled lengths, \texttt{pderiv\_std} is nonetheless
marginally faster than \texttt{pderiv\_bc} on both rows
($28{,}364.22$\,ms against $33{,}212.89$\,ms on P60;
$52{,}259.70$\,ms against $54{,}264.21$\,ms on P61,
Tables~\ref{tab:patho-p60-all-parsers} and~\ref{tab:patho-p61-all-parsers}),
so bit-coding gives \texttt{pderiv\_bc} no measured speed advantage
over \texttt{pderiv\_std} at this scale, even though it tags fewer
total residuals here than \texttt{pderiv\_std} wraps in closures.
Which representation costs less per tagged residual is not resolved
by wall time alone; Section~\ref{sec:eval-cost-partial-derivatives}
returns to this comparison across all four representative rows.

\textbf{P01 and P09.} Figures~\ref{fig:patho-p01} and~\ref{fig:patho-p09}
plot \texttt{(a+)+} and \texttt{(a|aa)+}, 
category A and B's ambiguous Kleene-star pair: 
P01 nests a star directly inside a plus, 
while P09 leaves the quantifier flat 
and instead lets \texttt{a} and \texttt{aa} overlap, 
so the same substring can be consumed more than one way.
So both patterns are different routes to the same kind of ambiguity.
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} crashes earliest on both, at $n=50$ on P01 and
$n=100$ on P09 (\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} coincides, Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-timing}),
later than either but still far earlier than the E-family rows above.

ten-million-character sweep.

give the wall time behind both figures,
and P01's missing \texttt{deriv\_bc} entries past $n=1\,000$ are that timeout,
not a gap in measurement.
On P01, \texttt{deriv\_bc} is briefly the fastest of the three at
$n=15$ and $n=20$, but loses ground rapidly from there: by $n=100$ it
already trails the fastest by about 30\%, and its gap widens to

faster than either partial-derivative variant's as it nears that

The difference is what each pattern's ambiguity costs per character. 
P01 nests a \texttt{+} inside another, 
so each additional \texttt{a} multiplies the ways the outer repetition 
could split into iterations,
making each derivative step near-exponential. 
\texttt{deriv\_bc} keeps all live splits in one expression
and compares every pair per character,
so its cost grows with $n$ faster than either partial-derivative
variant's, and its ratio to the fastest of the three climbs from
near parity to roughly $156\times$ before timeout.
The gap is already visible in Table~\ref{tab:patho-p01-all-parsers},
where \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s $n=1\,000$ time of 7.840\,ms
is close to three times either partial-derivative variant's,
and only widens from there.
P09's ambiguity is shallower.
Category B's shared-prefix alternatives
under one flat quantifier leave only a couple of live readings at each position,
not a count tied to iterations passed.
Pairwise cleanup is therefore bounded per character,
holding \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s ratio to the fastest of the three in
roughly the $1.9$--$2.2\times$ band from $n=100$ to ten
million characters,
and Table~\ref{tab:patho-p09-all-parsers} shows this directly:
from that point on, \texttt{deriv\_bc} takes roughly twice
\texttt{pderiv\_std}'s time and roughly 1.8 times \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s.
Between the two partial-derivative variants,
\texttt{pderiv\_std} is faster on both patterns at the largest swept $n$,
though the margin is narrow on both:
on P09, $25\,388.12$\,ms against \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s $28\,030.32$\,ms,
and on P01, $20\,848.01$\,ms against $21\,771.78$\,ms.

\textbf{Convergence.}
At $p=10\,000$, \texttt{deriv\_bc} is roughly $3.5\times$
faster than \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e38}).
Unlike \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}, which never attempts to simplify the alternation
and so never pays for an attempt that could not have succeeded
(every one of the $p$ alternatives is genuinely distinct by construction),
\texttt{deriv\_bc} does attempt to, but now hashes each branch's shape
once rather than comparing every pair,
so the attempt itself no longer dominates its per-character cost at this scale.
The partial-derivative pair is cheapest at this point, between
roughly $1.6\times$ and $2.0\times$ faster than \texttt{deriv\_bc},
since neither ever holds the $p$ alternatives
as one combined expression to flatten and simplify in the first place --
a narrower gap than on \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}, now that \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s
own deduplication is $O(p)$ rather than $O(p^2)$.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} still pays more on E38 than on E37 at every $p$.
\texttt{hash\_shape} (Figure~\ref{fig:hash-shape-rust}) walks a word's
whole chain of \texttt{Seq} nodes to hash it, one recursive call per
character, so its cost tracks word length regardless of whether that
word shares a prefix with any other.
E38's numbered words are longer than E37's fixed four-letter ones,
so hashing each one costs more.

\begin{figure}[H]
\small
\begin{verbatim}
fn dedup_first(branches: &mut Vec<ARegex>) {
    let mut buckets: HashMap<u64, Vec<usize>> = HashMap::new();
    let mut keep = vec![true; branches.len()];
    for idx in 0..branches.len() {
        let mut hasher = DefaultHasher::new();
        hash_shape(&branches[idx], &mut hasher);
        let h = hasher.finish();

        let bucket = buckets.entry(h).or_default();
        if bucket.iter().any(|&prev| same_shape(&branches[prev], &branches[idx])) {
            keep[idx] = false;
        } else {
            bucket.push(idx);
        }
    }
    let mut idx = 0;
    branches.retain(|_| { let k = keep[idx]; idx += 1; k });
}
\end{verbatim}
\caption{\texttt{dedup\_first} (Figure~\ref{fig:simp-rust}): groups
branches by a structural hash of their shape, bits aside, and runs
\texttt{same\_shape} only within one hash bucket to confirm a real
match before dropping a later branch in favour of the earliest one
of its shape.}
\label{fig:dedup-first-rust}
\end{figure}

\clearpage
Figure~\ref{fig:dedup-first-rust} hashes each of the $p$ branches once
with \texttt{hash\_shape} (Figure~\ref{fig:hash-shape-rust}), which
mirrors \texttt{same\_shape}'s own recursion arm for arm, so two
branches of the same shape always land in the same bucket; a hash
collision between differently shaped branches costs one extra,
correctly negative, \texttt{same\_shape} call within that bucket,
never a wrong merge, since bucket membership is only ever a pre-filter
for the real equality test. For $p$ genuinely distinct words, each
one's own characters make its hash distinct from the others, so
buckets stay singletons and \texttt{same\_shape} is almost never
called at all; the cost is $O(p)$ hashing, not an $O(p^2)$ comparison
paid for zero branches removed.
This implements the \texttt{nub} call 
in the choice case of
\citet[sect.~3, Fig.~6]{sulzmannlu2014}'s simplification.
\[
simp\ (bs@{\oplus}(ri : rsi)) = bs@{\oplus}\bigl(\mathit{nub}\
(\mathit{filter}\ (\neg\,\mathit{isPhi})\ ((simp\ ri) : \mathit{map}\
simp\ rsi))\bigr),
\]
Haskell's own \texttt{nub} is quadratic, 
since with only an \texttt{Eq} instance 
it keeps the first occurrence of each element 
by comparing it against every element already kept;
\texttt{dedup\_first} avoids that cost by hashing first and only
falling back to a pairwise check within one bucket.
\texttt{same\_shape} is the same equality test \texttt{nub} would use, 
made insensitive to bit-code history, 
so identical branches merge regardless of parse path.

\begin{verbatim}
fn hash_shape<H: Hasher>(r: &ARegex, state: &mut H) {
    match r {
        ARegex::Phi => 0u8.hash(state),
        ARegex::Eps(_) => 1u8.hash(state),
        ARegex::Lit(_, c) => { 2u8.hash(state); c.hash(state); }
        ARegex::Alt(_, a, b) => {
            3u8.hash(state);
            hash_shape(a, state);
            hash_shape(b, state);
        }
        ARegex::Seq(_, a, b) => {
            4u8.hash(state);
            hash_shape(a, state);
            hash_shape(b, state);
        }
        ARegex::Star(_, a) => { 5u8.hash(state); hash_shape(a, state); }
    }
}
\end{verbatim}
\caption{\texttt{hash\_shape}: a discriminant tag per variant, plus
whatever \texttt{same\_shape} itself recurses into, always skipping
the node's own bits.}
\label{fig:hash-shape-rust}
\end{figure}

%

The partial-derivative pair deduplicates via a related but distinct
mechanism: \texttt{nub2} and \texttt{nub\_tree} are also hash-based
and $O(p)$, but hash the whole residual directly rather than a
structural shape, differing only in what carries along with each one
(a bit vector in \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, an injection closure in
\texttt{pderiv\_std}). Since the mechanism is otherwise the same,
only \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s is shown (Figure~\ref{fig:nub2-rust}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\small
\begin{verbatim}
pub(super) fn step_frontier(frontier: &[(Regex, AppendBits)], x: char) 
-> Vec<(Regex, AppendBits)> {
    let mut out = Vec::new();
    for (r, bits) in frontier {
        for (r_next, extra) in pderiv_bc(r, x) {
            out.push((r_next, bits.append_bits(&extra)));
        }
    }
    nub2(out)
}

pub(super) fn nub2<B>(pairs: Vec<(Regex, B)>) -> Vec<(Regex, B)> {
    let mut seen: HashSet<Regex> = HashSet::with_capacity(pairs.len());
    let mut out = Vec::with_capacity(pairs.len());
    for (r, bs) in pairs {
        if seen.insert(r.clone()) {
            out.push((r, bs));
        }
    }
    out
}
\end{verbatim}
\caption{\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s \texttt{step\_frontier} and \texttt{nub2}.
Each residual steps through \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, then results are
deduplicated once via a \texttt{HashSet} keyed on the residual,
implementing \citet{sulzmannlu2012} Def.~8.}
\label{fig:nub2-rust}
\end{figure}

Before the alternation splits into separate residuals, the first call
must flatten the whole $p$-deep nested \texttt{Alt} into its $p$ leaves
(Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-impl}), each tagged with its own bit prefix,
and deduplicate them once via \texttt{nub2}. Measured directly, nearly
all of \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s cost on a wide alternation sits in this
first call. \texttt{step\_frontier}'s loop then calls \texttt{pderiv\_bc}
and \texttt{nub2} once per character, linear in the live residuals, and
E38's longer shared prefix repeats this cost more often than E37.
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} pays neither cost, making no cross-branch or
per-residual comparison at all.

\texttt{deriv\_bc} deduplicates its own branches the same way in
spirit, keyed on shape (Figure~\ref{fig:hash-shape-rust}) rather than
on the whole residual the way \texttt{nub2} and \texttt{nub\_tree} do,
since a combined annotated expression has branches to flatten and
deduplicate in the first place, where a partial-derivative frontier
already keeps one state per surviving residual.
Either way the dedup itself is $O(p)$, not $O(p^2)$, without changing
what it computes: a hash collision only ever costs one extra,
correctly negative, \texttt{same\_shape} call, never a wrong merge.

\section{Findings}
\label{sec:eval-discussion}

\subsection{Cost of the standard construction}

\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}, the standard construction's representative
outside the dedicated stack-depth probe
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-timing}), is the most prone to
crashing and, on the entries that cost it the most, by far the
slowest and most memory-hungry of the four; on typical small entries
this is not the case, as the next section details. Its two-pass
structure retains every intermediate expression until the backward
pass completes. On \texttt{a*} input, this state grows linearly with
$n$, while the other three variants remain bounded over the measured
range.

\texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} tracks \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} closely
everywhere outside the stack-depth probe itself
(Sections~\ref{sec:eval-timing-s2elab}, \ref{sec:eval-timing-representative}):
under the heap-capped setting used there, both reach their memory
cap at the same inputs and report almost identical times, since
both are limited by retained derivation state rather than by
recursion depth.

The stack-depth test isolates that other failure mode directly:
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} survives roughly $26\times$ longer in wall
time there than \texttt{deriv\_std\_rec} before the doubling search
reaches its own per-probe cap (Section~\ref{sec:eval-stack-depth}).
The limiting factor thus
depends on whether derivation cost or stack depth grows first, and
which of the two forms is used changes only which of those two
failure modes is reached, not whether the underlying growth happens
at all.

%
%

\subsection{Cost of bit-coding}
\label{sec:eval-cost-bit-coding}

Within the standard-derivative family, bit-coding's advantage on
the measured corpus is not in typical-case speed: on median,
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} is itself faster than \texttt{deriv\_bc}
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-s2elab}), since most corpus entries
are small enough that \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s own bookkeeping costs
more than it saves. The advantage shows up instead in peak memory
and in robustness. \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s peak allocation is
4.9\,MB, about $1\,906\times$ lower than \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}'s
peak (9\,282\,MB). This comparison is not population-stable on
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}'s side: \texttt{deriv\_bc} never crashes or times
out on this corpus, so every one of its $11\,395$ entries
contributes to its own peak, while \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}'s measured
population is bounded by the same $60$-second per-entry timeout
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}) and so depends on where that
boundary falls, crashing instead on a small fraction of entries
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-s2elab}).
Its forward pass retains the information needed for
single-pass reconstruction, avoiding repeated simplification
of redundant branches. Reconstruct adds little time, staying
under 2\,ms even at $p=10\,000$ alternatives. Setup depends
only on pattern size, reaching 10.45\,ms at $p=10\,000$
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e37}).
Already at the narrowest sampled widths \texttt{deriv\_bc} tracks
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}'s own cost closely, and from a couple of thousand
alternatives on it overtakes it rather than losing ground, as
discussed below.

The representative sweeps reinforce the robustness result.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} completes three of the four rows (P60, P61,
and P09), reaching ten million characters on each, while
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop} crashes on all four. Its cumulative allocation
can nevertheless be high, reaching 52.43\,GB on P60 despite
a peak of 2.16\,GB. This illustrates why both cumulative
allocation and peak live bytes are reported
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-memory}).

\subsection{Cost of partial derivatives}
\label{sec:eval-cost-partial-derivatives}

The partial-derivative parsers are the fastest and use the
least memory on the S2ELab corpus. Representing the residual
as a set of states rather than one combined expression limits
the alternation growth seen with standard derivatives.

This difference is sharpest on wide alternations, where it also
separates \texttt{pderiv\_bc} from \texttt{deriv\_bc}, not only from
\texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}. \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s simplification pass removes
duplicate branches of a combined \texttt{Alt} expression with
\texttt{dedup\_first} (Section~\ref{sec:simp-impl}), which compares
every pair of flattened branches and costs $O(p^2)$ for $p$
alternatives. \texttt{pderiv\_bc} deduplicates its own flattened
frontier with \texttt{nub2} (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc-impl}), a
hash-set membership test costing $O(p)$ for the same $p$ branches.
Both parsers flatten nested alternations into one list before
deduplicating it, avoiding a cost that would otherwise be repeated
once per nesting level; what still differs is the cost of the single
deduplication pass itself, pairwise for \texttt{deriv\_bc} against
hash-based for \texttt{pderiv\_bc}. Both parsers' derive time grows
with $p$ at a comparable rate: on E38, \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s stays
within a roughly $1.6\times$ to $2.1\times$ multiple of
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s across the whole sweep rather than widening
as $p$ grows, so the pairwise scan's larger constant factor, not a
diverging growth rate, is what separates them at this scale.

Within this pair, bit-coding now gives \texttt{pderiv\_bc} a small
advantage on the measured corpus.
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} has the lower figure on three of the four axes
measured: mean (0.706 against \texttt{pderiv\_std}'s 0.752\,ms),
median (0.109 against 0.114\,ms), and total allocation (5.19
against 5.68\,GB); \texttt{pderiv\_std} keeps the lower peak (2.5
against 3.8\,MB), the one axis where it still leads.
The two parsers implement the same nullable-\texttt{Seq} collapse
differently (Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-tree-impl}):
\texttt{pderiv\_std} builds a differently shaped injection closure
whenever the derivative of $r_1$ is \texttt{Eps}, even if no
duplicate residual needs removing, while \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s
version of the same collapse only removes a wrapper. This
structural difference favours \texttt{pderiv\_bc} wherever such
collisions are rare, as they are in the real corpus, and it is
reflected in three of the four measured figures; \texttt{pderiv\_std}'s
lower peak is the one exception, and establishing what costs
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} more there specifically would need separate
profiling.

The representative rows show the corpus ranking does not generalize
in full. At the largest commonly reached input,
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} is slower than \texttt{deriv\_bc} on both
P60 (a ratio of 1.023 at ten million characters) and P61 (1.054,
also at ten million, which both now reach without crashing;
Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-representative}). It is faster on P09
(0.558) and, by a wide margin, on P01 (0.007 at $n=50\,000$, the
largest length \texttt{deriv\_bc} itself reaches there before
timing out). \texttt{deriv\_bc} itself is no longer uniformly ahead
of \texttt{pderiv\_std} either: \texttt{pderiv\_std} is faster on
P60, P09, and (where \texttt{deriv\_bc} has data) P01, but on P61 it
is \texttt{deriv\_bc} that is marginally faster, by about
1.5\,\% (51\,493.84\,ms against 52\,259.70\,ms).
Within the partial-derivative pair itself, \texttt{pderiv\_std} is
the faster of the two on all four representative rows, by a margin
ranging from about 4\,\% (P61) to about 17\,\% (P60) -- the reverse
of its position on the S2ELab corpus, where \texttt{pderiv\_bc} now
leads on mean and median (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cost-bit-coding}).
Which of the two is faster therefore depends on the input: corpus-typical
entries favour \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, these four stress patterns favour
\texttt{pderiv\_std}.

The peak-memory ranking disagrees with the corpus on one row.
\texttt{pderiv\_std} has the lower peak of the partial-derivative
pair on three of the four representative rows, the same parser that
has the lower peak on the corpus: by around 6\,\% on P60, 6\,\% on
P61, and 1\,\% on P09 (Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory-representative}).
On P01 the ranking reverses and \texttt{pderiv\_bc} has the lower
peak instead, by about 18\,\%, even though \texttt{pderiv\_std}
remains the faster of the two there.
Speed and peak memory therefore no longer move together for this
pair on every row: P01 favours \texttt{pderiv\_bc} on memory but
\texttt{pderiv\_std} on time, the only row among these four where the
two axes disagree.

On wide patterns, the two bit-coded parsers differ in which
direction they diverge from the corpus ranking: here it is
\texttt{deriv\_bc}, not \texttt{pderiv\_bc}, that is consistently
slower, on both matching and non-matching input.
For a $p$-way alternation of equal-length words
(Table~\ref{tab:patho-phases-e37}), \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s derive time
is roughly $2.5\times$ \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s at $p=10\,000$ on
matching input, and within a similar band on non-matching input
(for instance $2.9\times$ at $p=10\,000$: 23.41\,ms against
7.98\,ms). Rejecting
\texttt{"zzzz"} still requires deriving all $p$ alternatives, but
none survive, leaving nothing to deduplicate; \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s
branch deduplication (Figure~\ref{fig:dedup-first-rust}) accordingly
contributes little extra cost here, yet \texttt{pderiv\_bc} is
still the faster parser on this side too, so deduplication cost
alone does not account for the gap between them.
At this scale, \texttt{deriv\_bc} already tracks \texttt{deriv\_std\_loop}
closely at the narrowest sampled widths and overtakes it from a
couple of thousand alternatives on, rather than falling behind
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-timing-representative}),
while \texttt{pderiv\_bc} remains well ahead of all three.
\texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own \emph{setup} figure is
smaller than \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s on both matching and
non-matching input, but this is
not a fair comparison of tagging cost: \texttt{pderiv\_bc}
builds no annotated state upfront, so its \emph{setup} entry
times only the initial clone plus its own flattening pass, measured
separately from \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s one-time tagging
by \texttt{internalize} though serving the same role
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-timing}).
The two bit-coded parsers' \emph{setup} figures are, on that basis,
already a fair comparison of tagging cost at this row: both grow
with $p$, \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s consistently the smaller of the two
(5.51 against \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s 10.45\,ms at $p=10\,000$).

Memory does not reflect this same cost difference. On the
widest swept alternations (Section~\ref{sec:eval-memory-representative}),
\texttt{pderiv\_std}'s own peak grows to 12.91\,MB on E38 and
7.63\,MB on E37 at $p=10\,000$, close to \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own
10.37 and 6.05\,MB there and well below \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s 21.57
and 12.93\,MB; all four parsers' peaks grow roughly in proportion
to $p$ over this sweep rather than diverging, including the two
whose per-leaf representation is sized by the leaf's own position
in the worst case
(\texttt{pderiv\_std}'s wrapped injection closures,
Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-tree-impl}, and \texttt{pderiv\_bc}'s own
bit-prefixes, Section~\ref{sec:pderiv-bc}). Measured peak memory
is consistent with much of each leaf's representation being shared
with its neighbors rather than held as an independent copy, though
profiling would be needed to confirm this. The partial-derivative
pair's advantage in peak memory over the standard-derivative family
is, on this evidence, not conditional on the pattern's alternation
width staying bounded.

Both partial-derivative variants produce non-POSIX trees.
This reflects a difference in parsing policy, not a bug,
and motivates comparing the two parser families separately
in Section~\ref{sec:eval-agreement}.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\textbf{The synthetic set.} Its 112 rows isolate individual
complexity mechanisms at controlled scales rather than
representing typical user patterns. Most categories vary
one parameter at a time, so combinations of stress
mechanisms remain untested.

\textbf{Agreement subset.} The \emph{Compared} subset
contains expected matches completed by every candidate,
with both families internally consistent. It excludes
71 of 5\,869 S2ELab matches (about 1\%) and 236 of
443 synthetic matches (about 53\%), making the synthetic
comparison substantially less representative. In
alternation-heavy categories, excluded cases may also
be more ambiguous, since shared prefixes can increase
both derivation cost and the number of possible parses.
This need not apply to categories such as I, where
timeouts arise from input length alone.

\textbf{Measurement model.} Memory measurements cover
heap allocations, not total process memory. Timing depends
on the test machine and may vary with CPU and cache.
A recorded \emph{crash} means the process was killed by
a signal, not that stack overflow was confirmed
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-criteria-setup}). The separate
stack-depth test removes the memory cap and provides
direct evidence of stack overflow. Crashes in the
representative rows are instead consistent with the
memory cost of retaining intermediate derivatives.
