\chapter{Derivative-Based Parser}
\label{sec:derivative-parser}

Chapter~\ref{sec:matching-vs-parsing} fixed what is being computed --
the $\geq_r$-maximal parse tree of Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}
-- without saying how. This chapter presents Sulzmann and Lu's answer
\citep{sulzmannlu2014}: a parser built directly on the Brzozowski
derivative of Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}, in two forms. The
first, direct construction computes a derivative sequence and then
explicitly rebuilds a parse tree from it in a second, backward pass; the
second, bit-coded construction fuses parse-tree bookkeeping into the
derivative step itself, needing only one forward pass. Both are POSIX --
they compute the identical $\geq_r$-maximal tree, verified by direct
comparison in Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} -- and differ only in how that
tree is produced. Both are developed here as theory first (Sections
\ref{sec:core-algorithm}-\ref{sec:simplification-rules-theory}), then as
the two Rust implementations that realize them, \texttt{deriv\_std}
(Section~\ref{sec:derivative-parser-inj-rust}) and \texttt{deriv\_bc}
(Section~\ref{sec:derivative-parser-bitcoded-rust}).
Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} repeats the exercise once
more, replacing the Brzozowski derivative throughout with Antimirov's
partial derivative from Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}
-- and, as that chapter shows, changing more than just which derivative
operator is used.



\section{Core Algorithm}
\label{sec:core-algorithm}

The direct construction follows the same two-phase shape informally
sketched for Brzozowski's derivative in Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}:
consume the input by taking successive derivatives, then decide whether
the result is nullable. What is new here is what happens \emph{between}
those two phases -- instead of a single Boolean, a parse tree is built.
The construction is best understood in two halves: a forward pass that
is nothing more than the derivative sequence already introduced, and a
backward pass that walks that sequence in reverse to assemble a tree one
consumed character at a time.

\paragraph{Forward pass.} For input $x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n$, compute
$r_0 \xrightarrow{x_1} r_1 \xrightarrow{x_2} \cdots \xrightarrow{x_n} r_n$
exactly as before, where $r_0 = r$ and $r_{i} = r_{i-1} \backslash x_i$.
If $r_n$ is not nullable, no parse tree exists and parsing fails. This
half of the construction is identical to \texttt{match\_deriv}
(Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}) with one difference: no
simplification is applied between steps. \texttt{simplify} is safe for a
Boolean matcher, since it only ever normalizes $r_i$ to a
language-equivalent expression, but the backward pass below needs to
walk back through the \emph{exact} sequence of derivative
transformations that produced $r_n$ from $r_0$ -- a simplified $r_i$ may
no longer be the expression \texttt{inject} expects to see at that step.

\paragraph{Backward pass.} Once $r_n$ is known to be nullable, the parse
tree $v_n$ for the empty remainder is built directly, by a function
$\mathit{mkEps}$ defined by structural recursion on a nullable
expression:
\begin{align*}
\mathit{mkEps}_{\varepsilon} &= () &
\mathit{mkEps}_{r^{*}} &= [\,] \\
\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1 r_2} &= (\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1}, \mathit{mkEps}_{r_2}) &
\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1+r_2} &=
  \begin{cases}
    \mathit{Left}\ \mathit{mkEps}_{r_1} & \varepsilon \in L(r_1) \\
    \mathit{Right}\ \mathit{mkEps}_{r_2} & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
\end{align*}
$\mathit{mkEps}$ has no case for a literal $x$ or for $\emptyset$,
since neither is ever nullable -- the function is only ever called on an
expression already known to satisfy $n(r)$. Its only interesting case is
alternation: exactly one of rule (A1)/(A2) of
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost} is relevant here, since both
sides of an empty match have length $0$ -- (A2)'s tie-break, "prefer
left when lengths are equal", degenerates at the empty word to "prefer
left whenever left is even possible", which is exactly the
$\varepsilon \in L(r_1)$ guard above.

With $v_n$ in hand, the remaining task is to turn it back into a parse
tree of the \emph{original} $r_0$, one derivative step in reverse at a
time. This is the job of $\mathit{inj}$, an injection function that
takes a parse tree of a derivative $r \backslash x$ and re-inserts the
consumed character $x$ to produce a parse tree of $r$ itself:
\begin{align*}
\mathit{inj}_{r^{*} \backslash x}(v, vs) &= (\mathit{inj}_{r \backslash x}\ v) : vs \\
\mathit{inj}_{(r_1 r_2) \backslash x}(v) &=
  \begin{cases}
    (\mathit{inj}_{r_1 \backslash x}\ v_1,\ v_2) & v = (v_1, v_2),\ \text{not nullable}(r_1) \\
    (\mathit{inj}_{r_1 \backslash x}\ v_1,\ v_2) & v = \mathit{Left}(v_1, v_2),\ \text{nullable}(r_1) \\
    (\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1},\ \mathit{inj}_{r_2 \backslash x}\ v_2) & v = \mathit{Right}\ v_2,\ \text{nullable}(r_1)
  \end{cases} \\
\mathit{inj}_{(r_1+r_2) \backslash x}(v) &=
  \begin{cases}
    \mathit{Left}\ (\mathit{inj}_{r_1 \backslash x}\ v_1) & v = \mathit{Left}\ v_1 \\
    \mathit{Right}\ (\mathit{inj}_{r_2 \backslash x}\ v_2) & v = \mathit{Right}\ v_2
  \end{cases} \\
\mathit{inj}_{x \backslash x}() &= x
\end{align*}
The three-way $(r_1 r_2)$ case is worth reading against the derivative
rule it inverts (Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}): $(r_1 r_2)
\backslash x$ produced either $(r_1 \backslash x) r_2$ alone (when $r_1$
is not nullable) or that same term joined with $r_2 \backslash x$ via
$+$ (when it is), tagged $\mathit{Left}$/$\mathit{Right}$ respectively
by the $\mathit{Alt}$ this new $+$ introduces. $\mathit{inj}$ undoes
exactly that tagging: a bare pair means $r_1$ was not nullable and the
derivative went straight into it; a $\mathit{Left}$-tagged pair means it
was nullable but the derivative still chose to continue matching $r_1$;
a $\mathit{Right}$-tagged value means the derivative instead jumped
straight into $r_2$, so $r_1$'s own contribution to the reconstructed
pair has to be synthesized fresh via $\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1}$, since no
part of it was consumed by $x$ at all.

Parsing is then this two-phase process packaged into one function,
applying $\mathit{mkEps}$ once at the end of the forward pass and
$\mathit{inj}$ once per character on the way back:
\[
\mathit{parse}(r, \varepsilon) = \mathit{mkEps}_r \quad (\text{if } \varepsilon \in L(r))
\qquad
\mathit{parse}(r, xw) = \mathit{inj}_{r \backslash x}(\mathit{parse}(r \backslash x, w))
\]

\paragraph{Worked example.} Return to $r_1 = (a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$
against $\mathtt{ab}$, whose two candidate parse trees were already
established in Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem}. The forward
pass computes $r_1 \xrightarrow{a} (\varepsilon+\varepsilon b)(b+\varepsilon)
\xrightarrow{b} (\phi+(\phi b+\varepsilon))(b+\varepsilon)+(\varepsilon+\phi)$,
nullable via its right disjunct $(\varepsilon+\phi)$. The backward pass
starts from $\mathit{mkEps}$ of that final expression --
$\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Right}(), \mathit{Right}())$, abbreviated
$(\mathit{Right}(),\mathit{Right}())$ once the outermost tag (there is
only one alternative left at the top level once $\phi$-branches are
excluded) is elided -- then injects $\mathtt{b}$ and finally $\mathtt{a}$
back in, arriving at $(\mathit{Right}(a,b), \mathit{Right}())$: the
\emph{longer} $ab$ branch, matching the POSIX answer already claimed in
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost} rather than Greedy's
$(\mathit{Left}\ a, \mathit{Left}\ b)$.



\section{Correctness Properties}
\label{sec:correctness-properties}

Sulzmann and Lu prove three results establishing that $\mathit{parse}$
computes exactly the $\geq_r$-maximal tree of
Definition~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}, not merely \emph{a} valid
one \citep{sulzmannlu2014}. This section states them; full proofs are
in the reference and are not reproduced here, but the intuition behind
each is worth making explicit, since it is exactly what justifies trusting
the Rust implementation of Section~\ref{sec:derivative-parser-inj-rust}
beyond "it agrees with the reference on every test case we tried"
(Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} verifies that too, but the proofs are why
agreement on finitely many test cases is expected to generalize).

\begin{description}
  \item[Lemma 1 (Empty POSIX Parse Tree).] For $r$ with
  $\varepsilon \in L(r)$, $\mathit{mkEps}_r$ is the POSIX parse tree of
  $r$ for the empty word. This grounds the base case of the induction the
  other two results build on: at $\mathit{mkEps}$'s only interesting
  case, alternation, both sides can only ever match length $0$ (there is
  no remaining input to consume), so (A2)'s length comparison always
  ties and reduces to left-preference -- exactly the
  $\varepsilon \in L(r_1)$ test $\mathit{mkEps}$ performs.

  \item[Lemma 2 (POSIX Preservation under Injection).] If $v$ is the
  POSIX parse tree of $r \backslash x$, then $\mathit{inj}_{r
  \backslash x}(v)$ is the POSIX parse tree of $r$. This is the result
  doing the real work, and its proof is correspondingly the most
  involved of the three. The informal argument: the derivative operation
  always tries $r_1$ before falling through to $r_2$ at a concatenation
  or alternation node (Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}'s
  $(r_1 r_2) \backslash x$ case only reaches $s \backslash x$ when $r_1$
  is nullable, and even then keeps the $r_1$-continuation branch as the
  left disjunct of the resulting $+$); $\mathit{inj}$ reconstructing that
  exact same left-first bias at every backward step, rather than an
  arbitrary one, is what carries POSIX-maximality from the smaller
  expression $r \backslash x$ up to the original $r$.

  \item[Theorem 1 (POSIX Parsing).] $\mathit{parse}$ computes POSIX
  parse trees. This follows from Lemmas 1 and 2 by induction on the
  input word: the base case is Lemma 1 applied to $r_n$, and each
  inductive step is one application of Lemma 2, carrying
  POSIX-maximality from $r \backslash x_1 \cdots x_n$ back up through
  $n$ injections to $r$ itself.
\end{description}



\section{Bit-Coded Optimization (Theory)}
\label{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory}

The direct construction of Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm} must retain
the entire forward-pass sequence $r_0, r_1, \ldots, r_n$ (or, in an
implementation, an equivalent call stack) so that $\mathit{inj}$ has
each $r_{i-1} \backslash x_i$ available to reconstruct against on the way
back -- the backward pass cannot begin until the forward pass has fully
finished. Sulzmann and Lu's bit-coded construction (their Section 3.2,
"Incremental Bit-Coded Forward Parse Tree Construction")
\citep{sulzmannlu2014} removes this requirement by incrementally
building the parse tree \emph{during} the forward pass itself, so that
by the time the last character is consumed, the winning tree's
information is already sitting in the final expression -- no second pass
required.

\paragraph{Bit-code representation.} Rather than a \texttt{ParseTree}
value, a parse tree is represented as a flat sequence of bits: $0$ marks
a $\mathit{Left}$ choice, $1$ a $\mathit{Right}$ choice, and inside a
Kleene star, $0$ marks "one more iteration" and $1$ marks "stop". A pair
of mutually recursive functions $\mathit{encode}_r$/$\mathit{decode}_r$
convert between a $\vdash v : r$ parse tree and its bit-string
representation, structurally straightforward given the tagging already
present in $v$ itself.

\paragraph{Annotated regular expressions.} The construction's core idea
is to annotate the derivative operation's own \emph{input}, not just its
output, with partial bit-code information, so that by the time a
sub-expression becomes $\varepsilon$ its accumulated annotation already
\emph{is} the relevant slice of the final bit-string. An annotated
regular expression $\mathit{ri}$ extends plain $r$ with a bit-list $bs$
attached at every node except $\emptyset$ (which denotes no language and
so has no parse tree to annotate):
\[
\mathit{ri} \;::=\; \emptyset \mid (bs@\varepsilon) \mid (bs@x) \mid
(bs@\mathit{ri}_1 \oplus \mathit{ri}_2) \mid (bs@\mathit{ri}_1\, \mathit{ri}_2)
\mid (bs@\mathit{ri}^{*})
\]
Two operations connect plain $r$ to this annotated world.
$\mathit{internalize}$ converts $r$ into $\mathit{ri}$ with every
bit-list initially empty, except at $\mathit{Alt}$: the left operand of
$r_1 + r_2$ is tagged with a leading $0$ and the right with a leading
$1$ at construction time, so that which alternative a sub-tree came from
can always be recovered from its own annotation without re-inspecting
the surrounding structure. $\mathit{fuse}(bs, \mathit{ri})$ prepends a
bit-list onto whatever annotation $\mathit{ri}$'s own top-most node
already carries -- the one primitive every other operation in this
construction is built from.

\paragraph{The refined derivative.} The ordinary derivative operation is
lifted to annotated expressions, written $\mathit{ri} \backslash_b l$,
following the same case structure as $r \backslash l$
(Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}) but threading bits through
instead of discarding the annotation:
\begin{align*}
(bs@\mathit{ri}_1 \oplus \mathit{ri}_2) \backslash_b l &=
  (bs@(\mathit{ri}_1 \backslash_b l) \oplus (\mathit{ri}_2 \backslash_b l)) \\
(bs@\mathit{ri}_1\, \mathit{ri}_2) \backslash_b l &=
  \begin{cases}
    (bs@(\mathit{ri}_1 \backslash_b l)\, \mathit{ri}_2) \oplus
    \mathit{fuse}(\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_1}, \mathit{ri}_2 \backslash_b l)
      & \varepsilon \in L(\mathit{ri}_1) \\
    (bs@(\mathit{ri}_1 \backslash_b l)\, \mathit{ri}_2) & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases} \\
(bs@\mathit{ri}^{*}) \backslash_b l &= (bs@\mathit{fuse}([0], \mathit{ri} \backslash_b l)\; ([\,]@\mathit{ri}^{*}))
\end{align*}
The concatenation case mirrors $(r_1 r_2) \backslash x$'s two-way split
exactly, with one addition: on the branch where $r_1$'s derivative is
abandoned in favor of jumping into $r_2$, the bits $r_1$ would have
contributed to an empty match ($\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_1}$,
below) are fused directly onto $r_2$'s derivative rather than discarded
-- this is the incremental step that replaces the direct construction's
\emph{later} call to $\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1}$ inside $\mathit{inj}$
(Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}) with work done immediately, during
the forward pass. The star case attaches a leading $0$ bit ("one more
iteration") each time the body is derived once more; the outer $bs$
stays where it is, on the new alternation/concatenation node the
derivative rule produces, exactly as it does for plain $\mathit{Alt}$.

\paragraph{mkEpsBC.} Mirroring $\mathit{mkEps}$
(Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}) but returning accumulated bits
instead of a tree:
\begin{align*}
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{(bs@\varepsilon)} &= bs \\
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{(bs@\mathit{ri}_1 \oplus \mathit{ri}_2)} &=
  bs \mathbin{+\!\!+} \begin{cases}
    \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_1} & \varepsilon \in L(\mathit{ri}_1) \\
    \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_2} & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases} \\
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{(bs@\mathit{ri}_1\, \mathit{ri}_2)} &=
  bs \mathbin{+\!\!+} \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_1} \mathbin{+\!\!+} \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_2} \\
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{(bs@\mathit{ri}^{*})} &= bs \mathbin{+\!\!+} [1]
\end{align*}
Once the forward pass ends on a nullable annotated expression,
$\mathit{mkEpsBC}$ extracts its complete bit-string directly, and
$\mathit{decode}_r$ (using the \emph{original}, un-annotated $r$ --
$\mathit{decode}$ needs to know the shape it is decoding bits
\emph{for}, which the annotated $\mathit{ri}$ alone does not by itself
determine once its own annotations have been consumed) turns that
bit-string into an ordinary parse tree. No second, backward pass over
the input is needed at all.

\paragraph{Worked example.} Run Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}'s
$r_1 = (a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$ against $\mathtt{ab}$ through this
construction instead, to see the same POSIX answer assembled
incrementally rather than reconstructed afterward (this trace is exactly
what \texttt{cargo run -- parse "(a|ab)(b|}$\varepsilon$\texttt{)" "ab" --parser deriv\_bc
--diag 3} prints; the tool's own \texttt{false}/\texttt{true} bits are
written $0$/$1$ below to match this section's convention).
$\mathit{internalize}(r_1)$ tags the two $\mathit{Alt}$ nodes' operands
with $0$/$1$ and leaves every other bit-list empty:
\[
\mathit{ri}_0 = [\,]@\Big([\,]@\big(0@a \oplus 1@([\,]@a \cdot [\,]@b)\big)
\;\cdot\; [\,]@\big(0@b \oplus 1@\varepsilon\big)\Big)
\]
Deriving with respect to $\mathtt{a}$: the left \emph{Seq} operand
$[\,]@(0@a \oplus 1@ab)$ is not nullable, so the Seq case's first branch
applies, threading the derivative into it alone and leaving
$(b+\varepsilon)$'s side untouched:
\[
\mathit{ri}_1 = [\,]@\Big([\,]@\big(0@\varepsilon \oplus 1@b\big)
\;\cdot\; [\,]@\big(0@b \oplus 1@\varepsilon\big)\Big)
\]
$\mathit{ri}_1$'s left operand is now nullable (via $0@\varepsilon$), so
deriving with respect to $\mathtt{b}$ takes the Seq case's other branch:
the left operand's own derivative continues on one side of a freshly
introduced $\mathit{Alt}$, while $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$ of that same left
operand -- $[0]$, the bit its nullable $0@\varepsilon$ disjunct
contributes -- is fused onto the right operand's derivative on the other:
\[
\mathit{ri}_2 = [\,]@\Big(1@\big([\,]@(0@b \oplus 1@\varepsilon)\big)
\;\oplus\; [0,0]@\varepsilon\Big)
\]
(the leading $1$ tags "derivative continued into the left operand" as the
\emph{Right} alternative of this new node, since unlike $\mathit{inj}$ --
which only discovers this tagging when reconstructing backward --
$\mathit{deriv\_bc}$ must commit to it immediately as part of the forward
pass itself; the second $0$ in $[0,0]$ is $(b+\varepsilon)$'s own
not-yet-derived left-disjunct tag, fused after $\mathit{ri}_1$'s $[0]$).
$\mathit{ri}_2$'s top-level $\mathit{Alt}$ has \emph{both} disjuncts
nullable here -- $[0,0]@\varepsilon$ trivially, and
$1@([\,]@(0@b\oplus 1@\varepsilon))$ as well, via its own nested right
disjunct $1@\varepsilon$ -- so $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$'s $\mathit{Alt}$ rule,
which (mirroring $\mathit{mkEps}$'s own $\mathit{Left}$-preference from
Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}) always takes the left disjunct when it
is nullable, regardless of the right disjunct also qualifying, picks
$1@(\ldots)$ over the shorter $[0,0]@\varepsilon$:
\[
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_2} = [\,] \mathbin{+\!\!+} \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{1@([\,]@(0@b\oplus 1@\varepsilon))}
= 1 \mathbin{+\!\!+} \big([\,] \mathbin{+\!\!+} \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{1@\varepsilon}\big)
= 1 \mathbin{+\!\!+} [1] = [1,1]
\]
the same left-preference that makes $r_1$'s plain construction choose
$\mathit{Right}$ at the top level in Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}'s
worked example: both encode "prefer continuing the match already in
progress over falling back to the shorter alternative," just applied at
different points -- there, during $\mathit{inj}$'s backward pass; here,
directly inside $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$'s forward-accumulated bits. Decoding
against the original $r_1$:
\[
\mathit{decode}_{r_1}([1,1]) = (\mathit{Right}(a,b), \mathit{Right}())
\]
-- the same tree Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}'s worked example
reached via its explicit backward pass, this time read directly off the
bits accumulated during the forward pass alone.

\emph{On simplification between steps.} Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}
noted that the plain construction's forward pass must \emph{not}
simplify between steps, since the backward pass needs to walk back
through the exact sequence of derivatives that produced it. The
bit-coded construction has no such restriction, and
\texttt{parse\_bitcoded\_recursive}/\texttt{parse\_bitcoded\_loop}
(\texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_bc/parse.rs}) do call \texttt{simp} after
every step, exactly as this worked example's derivation does. This is
safe here for the same reason \texttt{fuse} is: the information a
backward pass would otherwise need to walk back for is already carried
\emph{on} the annotated expression itself, as bits attached to its
nodes, so simplifying $\mathit{ri}_i$ down to a smaller but
bit-equivalent expression loses nothing $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$ needs later --
unlike the plain construction, where simplifying $r_i$ would destroy the
very structure $\mathit{inject}$ needs to reconstruct against.



\section{Simplification Rules}
\label{sec:simplification-rules-theory}

Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}'s worked example already showed
plain derivatives growing an extra $+$ at every nullable-prefix step
with nothing to bound that growth; the bit-coded construction inherits
the same problem; \citep{sulzmannlu2014} give the concrete case
$a^{*} \xrightarrow{a} \varepsilon a^{*} \xrightarrow{a} \phi a^{*} +
\varepsilon a^{*} \xrightarrow{a} (\phi a^{*} + \varepsilon a^{*}) +
(\phi a^{*} + \varepsilon a^{*}) \xrightarrow{a} \cdots$, where every
step after the first is language-equivalent to $\varepsilon a^{*}$ but
syntactically doubles in size. Left unchecked, this defeats the whole
point of the bit-coded construction's incremental, single-pass promise:
an unboundedly growing $\mathit{ri}$ makes each subsequent
$\backslash_b$ step correspondingly more expensive.

A simplification function $\mathit{simp}$, applied to a fixpoint after
every derivative step, keeps the annotated expression's size bounded.
Its rules -- Phi-propagation through $\mathit{Seq}$/$\mathit{Alt}$,
epsilon-elimination ($(bs@(bs'@\varepsilon)\, \mathit{ri}) \to
\mathit{fuse}(bs \mathbin{+\!\!+} bs', \mathit{ri})$, folding the
eliminated node's own bits forward rather than discarding them), and
flattening/deduplicating nested alternations, keeping the first
occurrence of a syntactic duplicate -- are all instances of the
familiar algebraic identities from Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}
($\emptyset \cdot r = \emptyset$, $\varepsilon \cdot r = r$, $\emptyset +
r = r$, $r + r = r$), applied here to the annotated grammar and combined
with an explicit \emph{isPhi} test that recognizes "empty language"
expressions built up from these rules rather than only the literal
$\emptyset$ constructor. Applied to the worked example above, $\mathit{simp}$
would collapse every step back down to (a bit-annotated form of)
$\varepsilon a^{*}$, exactly as ordinary \texttt{simplify} does for the
un-annotated case.

Sulzmann and Lu claim, but do not fully prove, that the resulting
construction runs in time linear in the input, on the grounds that each
of $\backslash_b$, $\mathit{simp}$, $\mathit{fuse}$, $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$,
and \emph{isPhi} only ever recurses over sub-calls bounded by the
(now-simplified) expression's size, and note explicitly that detailed
space-complexity estimates are left as future work
\citep{sulzmannlu2014}. This thesis does not attempt to close that gap
either; Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}'s benchmarks measure the
implementation's actual behavior empirically rather than establishing a
tighter asymptotic bound than the reference itself provides.



\section{Rust Implementation: The \texttt{deriv\_std} Parser}
\label{sec:derivative-parser-inj-rust}

\texttt{deriv\_std} (\texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_std/}) is the Rust
realization of Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}'s direct construction.
It exposes two entry points computing the identical result by two
different means -- \texttt{parse\_recursive} and \texttt{parse\_loop}
-- discussed together in Section~\ref{sec:recursive-vs-loop} below,
after the shared machinery both call into.

\subsection{\texttt{ParseTree} and \texttt{flatten}}
\label{sec:parsetree-and-flatten}

The \texttt{ParseTree} type and its \texttt{flatten} function were
already introduced in Section~\ref{sec:parse-trees-as-evidence}
(\texttt{src/types/tree.rs}) -- \texttt{Empty}, \texttt{Char},
\texttt{Pair}, \texttt{Left}, \texttt{Right}, \texttt{Star}
corresponding one to one with $()$, a literal, a pair, the two tagged
injections, and a Kleene-star list. Nothing about the type itself is
specific to \texttt{deriv\_std}: it is the one representation every
parser in this and the following chapter ultimately produces, precisely
so that the agreement tests of Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} can compare
their outputs directly rather than through some intermediate
translation.

\subsection{\texttt{mk\_eps}}
\label{sec:mk-eps-impl}

\texttt{mk\_eps} (\texttt{src/regex/mk\_eps.rs}) transcribes
$\mathit{mkEps}$ case for case: \texttt{Eps} to \texttt{Empty},
\texttt{Star} to an empty \texttt{Vec}, \texttt{Seq} to a
\texttt{Pair} of the two recursive results, and \texttt{Alt} to
\texttt{Left}/\texttt{Right} depending on \texttt{nullable(r1)} exactly
as the theory prescribes. Called on \texttt{Lit} or \texttt{Phi} --
which \texttt{mkEps} has no case for, since neither is ever nullable --
it panics rather than silently returning a meaningless value; every call
site only ever reaches \texttt{mk\_eps} once nullability of its argument
has already been established, so the panic is an assertion of an
invariant the surrounding code maintains, not a reachable runtime error.
This function lives in \texttt{regex/}, flat and outside
\texttt{deriv\_std/} specifically, because Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}'s
\texttt{pderiv\_std} needs the identical function for the identical
reason -- both build a \texttt{ParseTree} directly rather than
bit-codes, and both need the same "parse tree for a nullable expression
matched against nothing" primitive to do it.

\subsection{\texttt{inject}}
\label{sec:inject-impl}

\texttt{inject} (\texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_std/inject.rs}) is the
direct transcription of $\mathit{inj}$. Its \texttt{Seq} case is the one
worth reading closely against the theory: when \texttt{r1} is not
nullable, only a \texttt{Pair} is ever handed in, and injection recurses
into its first component; when \texttt{r1} \emph{is} nullable, the
incoming value is either \texttt{Left(Pair(v1, v2))} (the "continue
\texttt{r1}" branch the derivative rule produces) or \texttt{Right(v2)}
(the "\texttt{r1} already matched empty" branch), and only the second
case calls \texttt{mk\_eps(r1)} to synthesize the otherwise-absent left
component -- exactly the three-way split of
Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}'s $\mathit{inj}_{(r_1 r_2) \backslash
x}$. Every case that receives a value shaped differently from what the
corresponding derivative rule could have produced -- a bare \texttt{Pair}
where a nullable \texttt{r1} demands a tagged \texttt{Left}/\texttt{Right},
for instance -- panics rather than guessing, since such a mismatch can
only mean the caller passed a tree that did not actually come from
\texttt{r}'s own derivative sequence.

\subsection{\texttt{parse\_recursive} vs.\ \texttt{parse\_loop}}
\label{sec:recursive-vs-loop}

Both functions (\texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_std/parse.rs}) compute the
identical result -- verified directly, not just by construction, via
\texttt{recursive\_traced\_agrees\_with\_loop\_traced}
(Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation}) -- by two different means of
implementing the same two-phase algorithm.
\texttt{parse\_recursive} recurses to the end of the input first, then
calls \texttt{inject} on the way back up the call stack as the
recursion unwinds -- \texttt{deriv}'s "leading character removed" and
\texttt{inject}'s "leading character re-inserted" mirror each other
directly across the recursion, needing no explicit intermediate storage
for the forward-pass sequence at all, since the call stack \emph{is}
that sequence. \texttt{parse\_loop} makes the same sequence explicit as
a \texttt{Vec<Regex>}, built by an ordinary forward \texttt{for} loop,
then walks that vector backward with a second loop calling
\texttt{inject} at each step. The two exist side by side because they
make different resource trade-offs unrelated to the algorithm itself:
\texttt{parse\_recursive}'s call stack depth grows with the input
length, and can overflow it on sufficiently long input, where
\texttt{parse\_loop}'s explicit \texttt{Vec} instead grows on the heap
-- Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} measures exactly where that difference
starts to matter.



\section{Rust Implementation: The Bit-Coded Version}
\label{sec:derivative-parser-bitcoded-rust}

\texttt{deriv\_bc} (\texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_bc/}) realizes
Section~\ref{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory}'s construction.

\subsection{\texttt{ARegex} -- Bit-Annotated Regex}
\label{sec:aregex-type}

\texttt{ARegex} (\texttt{src/types/aregex.rs}) mirrors the annotated
grammar $\mathit{ri}$ directly: every variant except \texttt{Phi}
carries its own \texttt{Vec<bool>} bit-prefix alongside the same
structural payload \texttt{Regex} already carries (a \texttt{char} for
\texttt{Lit}, boxed sub-expressions for the recursive cases). This is
the one point in the whole implementation where a plain \texttt{Regex}
is not enough: \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s entire purpose is threading partial
parse-tree information \emph{through} the tree as it is derived, and
that requires a place on every node to put it, not only at the leaves.

\subsection{\texttt{internalize}/\texttt{fuse}}
\label{sec:internalize-fuse}

Both in \texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_bc/internalize.rs}.
\texttt{internalize} converts a plain \texttt{Regex} into a freshly
bit-annotated \texttt{ARegex} with every list empty, except
\texttt{Alt}: the left branch is fused with a leading \texttt{false}
and the right with a leading \texttt{true} at construction time, exactly
as the theory's $\mathit{internalize}$ does. \texttt{fuse(bs, ri)}
prepends \texttt{bs} onto \texttt{ri}'s own top-level annotation and is
the one primitive the rest of this section's functions are all built
from -- including the degenerate case \texttt{fuse(bs, Phi) = Phi},
since there is no annotation slot on \texttt{Phi} to prepend anything
to.

\subsection{\texttt{deriv\_bc}}
\label{sec:deriv-bc}

\texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_bc/deriv.rs} transcribes $\backslash_b$ case
for case. Its \texttt{Alt} case keeps the outer bit-list on the
resulting \texttt{Alt} node itself, rather than pushing it into either
recursive call -- a detail that looks like an arbitrary implementation
choice here but turns out to matter a great deal once partial
derivatives are introduced in Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser},
where the same choice, applied to a construction that no longer has a
single combined node to attach bits to, is shown to cause genuine
performance problems rather than a merely cosmetic difference. Its
\texttt{Seq} case implements the nullable-$r_1$ branch's two strands
exactly as written above -- continue $r_1$'s own derivative, or jump
into $r_2$'s with $r_1$'s empty-match bits fused on -- and its
\texttt{Star} case attaches the leading "one more iteration" bit via
\texttt{fuse(\&[false], ...)} before rewrapping the untouched star body.
\texttt{deriv\_bc} is called in a loop, once per input character,
inside \texttt{parse\_bitcoded}/\texttt{parse\_bitcoded\_loop}
(\texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_bc/parse.rs}), with \texttt{simp} applied
after every step -- the Rust realization of
Section~\ref{sec:simplification-rules-theory}'s fixpoint simplification,
in \texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_bc/simplify.rs}, whose cases (Phi
propagation, epsilon-elimination via \texttt{fuse}, and
list-flattening/first-occurrence deduplication for nested
alternations) match $\mathit{simp}$'s directly.

\subsection{\texttt{mk\_eps\_bc}}
\label{sec:mk-eps-bc}

\texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_bc/mk\_eps\_bc.rs} extracts the accumulated
bit-string from a nullable \texttt{ARegex}, structurally identical to
\texttt{mk\_eps} but returning bits instead of a tree: \texttt{Alt}
prefers whichever branch is nullable (left checked first, matching
\texttt{mk\_eps}'s own left-preference), \texttt{Seq} concatenates both
children's bits after its own prefix, and \texttt{Star} appends a final
\texttt{true} marking "no further iterations" onto whatever bits the
completed iterations already accumulated.

\subsection{\texttt{decode}}
\label{sec:decode}

\texttt{src/regex/decode.rs} turns the plain, \emph{original}
\texttt{Regex} plus a flat \texttt{Vec<bool>} bit-string back into an
ordinary \texttt{ParseTree}: \texttt{Alt} consumes one leading bit to
choose \texttt{Left}/\texttt{Right}, \texttt{Seq} decodes both children
in sequence, and \texttt{Star} loops, consuming a \texttt{false} per
iteration until a \texttt{true} ends it -- the direct inverse of
\texttt{encode}, which this implementation never needs to run itself,
since nothing here starts from an already-built \texttt{ParseTree}
needing to be re-encoded. \texttt{decode} takes only a plain
\texttt{Regex}, not an \texttt{ARegex} -- by the time it is called, the
bit-string alone carries everything needed, decoded against the shape of
the \emph{un}-annotated original expression. This is exactly why
\texttt{decode} lives in flat \texttt{regex/} rather than inside
\texttt{deriv\_bc/} specifically: Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}'s
\texttt{pderiv\_bc} produces the same \texttt{(Regex, bits)} pair by an
entirely different route and needs the identical final step to turn it
into a tree.

\section{Worked Example: \texttt{paper\_r1} End to End}
\label{sec:deriv-worked-example}

Section~\ref{sec:parse-trees-as-evidence} introduced $r_1 = (a + ab)(b +
\varepsilon)$ against $\mathtt{ab}$ (\texttt{paper\_r1}) on paper,
deriving by hand that the POSIX result is $v =
(\mathit{Right}(a,b), \mathit{Right}())$. What follows is that same
derivation at diagnostics level 3 -- the full structural trace, not the
summarized construction steps of level 2 -- produced by running
\texttt{deriv\_std} and \texttt{deriv\_bc} themselves
(\texttt{cargo run -- parse "(a|ab)(b|\ensuremath{\varepsilon})" "ab"
--diag 3}, and again with \texttt{--parser deriv\_bc}) rather than
reconstructed from the theory alone: every derivative rule application,
every intermediate expression, and every nullability check this
chapter's two constructions actually perform, character by character.

\paragraph{Success: \texttt{deriv\_std}.} The forward pass names each
rule applied at each step -- Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}'s own
$\partial(r_1 \cdot r_2, x)$ case split, visibly taken once per
character -- followed by the backward pass
(Section~\ref{sec:mk-eps-impl}/\ref{sec:inject-impl}) naming each
$\mathit{mkEps}$/\texttt{inject} call in turn:
\begin{verbatim}
-------------------------------====
REGEX ENGINE DEBUG REPORT
-------------------------------====
Mode:                 POSIX Standard (recursive parser)
Regex:                (a|ab)(b|epsilon)
Input:                "ab"
Result:               MATCH
Policy:               POSIX
-------------------------------====
TIMING
-------------------------------====
Parse time:           0.011ms
Expressions stored:   3 (r0 -> r1 -> r2)
-------------------------------====
FORWARD PASS (Derivatives)
-------------------------------====
r0 = Seq(Alt(Lit('a'), Seq(Lit('a'), Lit('b'))), Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))

Step 1: d(r0, 'a')
  rule: d(r1.r2, 'a')  [nullable(r1)=false]
       = d(r1, 'a').r2
r1 = Seq(Alt(Eps, Seq(Eps, Lit('b'))), Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))
  nullable(r1) = true  [OK] position 1 matched

  rule: d(r1.r2, 'b')  [nullable(r1)=true]
       = d(r1, 'b').r2  +  d(r2, 'b')

  inject(Seq(Alt(Eps, Seq(Eps, Lit('b'))), Alt(Lit('b'), Eps)),
    'b', Left (Right Right (), Right ()))
  -> (Right ((), b), Right ())
v1 = (Right ((), b), Right ())

inject(r0, 'a', v1):
  inject(Seq(Alt(Lit('a'), Seq(Lit('a'), Lit('b'))), Alt(Lit('b'),
    Eps)), 'a', (Right ((), b), Right ()))
  -> (Right (a, b), Right ())

Every intermediate $r_1$/$r_2$ this report names is printed in full, not
just referenced by number -- \texttt{Expressions stored: 3 (r0 -> r1 ->
r2)} is exactly the derivative sequence Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm}
builds, and the backward pass's own \texttt{v2}/\texttt{v1}/\texttt{v0}
trace $\mathit{mkEps}(r_2)$ through two \texttt{inject} calls back to the
final tree -- character-for-character the $v$
Section~\ref{sec:parse-trees-as-evidence} derived on paper, as Theorem 1
(Section~\ref{sec:correctness-properties}) guarantees it must be.

\paragraph{Success: \texttt{deriv\_bc}.} The same input against the same
$r_1$, through the bit-coded construction of
Section~\ref{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory}: \texttt{internalize}
annotating the regex once, then each \texttt{deriv\_bc + simp} step shown
against the derivative-on-bits rule it applies, then \texttt{mkEpsBC}
extracting bits and \texttt{decode} turning them back into a tree:
\begin{verbatim}
-------------------------------====

-------------------------------====

Input:                "ab"
