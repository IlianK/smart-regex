\chapter{Derivative-Based Parser}
\label{sec:derivative-parser}

Chapter~\ref{sec:matching-vs-parsing} fixed \emph{what} is to be
computed, the POSIX parse tree of
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}, without saying how. This
chapter gives Sulzmann and Lu's answer \citep{sulzmannlu2014}: a parser
built on the Brzozowski derivative of
Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}, in two forms.

The \emph{direct} construction computes the derivative sequence and then
rebuilds a parse tree from it in a second, backward pass. The
\emph{bit-coded} construction folds the tree-building into the
derivative step itself and needs one forward pass only. Both are POSIX
and both return the identical tree; they differ in when the tree is
assembled and in what has to be kept in memory until it is.

The chapter develops each construction as theory first, on paper, and
then as the Rust code that realizes it. Section~\ref{sec:deriv-tracing}
introduces the tracing facility used throughout, since every derivation
below is checked against the implementation's own report for the same
input. Section~\ref{sec:core-algorithm} gives the direct construction,
Section~\ref{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory} the bit-coded one, and
Section~\ref{sec:simplification-rules-theory} the simplification the
second of them depends on. Sections~\ref{sec:derivative-parser-inj-rust}
and \ref{sec:derivative-parser-bitcoded-rust} cover the two
implementations, and Section~\ref{sec:deriv-worked-example} sets them
against each other.
Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} then repeats the whole
exercise with Antimirov's partial derivative
(Section~\ref{sec:antimirov-partial-derivatives}) in place of
$r\backslash x$, and finds that more changes than the derivative
operator.



\section{Reading the Implementation's Traces}
\label{sec:deriv-tracing}

Every derivation in this chapter is stated twice: once on paper, as a
sequence of rule applications, and once as an excerpt from the
implementation running the same input. The second is what makes the
first checkable, so the conventions are fixed here before any derivation
uses them.

Each of the four parsers exists in a plain form and a \emph{traced}
form. The traced form is not a debugging layer bolted on afterwards: it
computes the same result by the same code path, and additionally emits,
at each step, the rule it applied and the value that rule produced.
\texttt{--diag} selects how much of that is reported:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Level & Adds & Destination \\
\hline
\texttt{--diag 0} & \texttt{true}/\texttt{false} only & stdout \\
\texttt{--diag 1} & regex, input, tree, error caret & stdout \\
\texttt{--diag 2} & timing, step count, construction steps & stdout \\
\texttt{--diag 3} & full derivation trace & \texttt{reports/report.txt} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Level 3 writes to a file because its output grows with both the input
length and the size of the intermediate expressions, and
Section~\ref{sec:simplification-rules-theory} shows how fast the latter
can grow. \texttt{--diag-report} overrides the destination.

A level-3 report is divided into labelled sections, and those sections
correspond to the construction's own phases closely enough that a single
fragment can be read on its own:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\hline
Report section & Construction phase \\
\hline
\texttt{FORWARD PASS} & the derivative sequence \\
\texttt{NULLABILITY CHECK} & the accept or reject decision \\
\texttt{BACKWARD PASS} & $\mathit{mkEps}$ and $\mathit{inj}$, direct construction \\
\texttt{INTERNALIZE}, \texttt{MKEEPSBC + DECODE} & the bit-coded construction's two ends \\
\texttt{PARTIAL RECOVERY}, \texttt{ERROR SUMMARY} & failure diagnostics \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Excerpts are quoted, never whole reports; each one is placed beside the
step it illustrates. All eight reports drawn on in this chapter and the
next appear in full in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostic-reports}, so any
excerpt can be read back in context. They share one running example,
$r_1 = (a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$ from
Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem}, against the matching input
\texttt{"ab"} and the non-matching input \texttt{"b"}. In the frontend's
surface syntax (Section~\ref{sec:frontend-parsing}) that expression is
written \texttt{"(a|ab)(b|)"}, with $\varepsilon$ spelled as an empty
right alternative, so every trace in this chapter comes from

\begin{verbatim}
cargo run -- parse "(a|ab)(b|)" "ab" --parser <PARSER> --diag 3
\end{verbatim}

Excerpts appear as the tool writes them, except that its Unicode
operators are transliterated to ASCII ($\partial$ as \texttt{d}, $\cdot$
as \texttt{.}, $\oplus$ as \texttt{(+)}, $\varepsilon$ as
\texttt{epsilon}, $\phi$ as \texttt{phi}, $\checkmark$ as
\texttt{[OK]}), and long lines are wrapped.



\section{The Direct Construction}
\label{sec:core-algorithm}

\subsection{What the Derivative Sequence Throws Away}

Start with the smallest expression that has anything to rebuild:
$r = a\cdot b$ against $w = \mathtt{ab}$. The forward pass is the
derivative sequence of Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives} and
nothing more:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-ab-forward}
\begin{aligned}
a\cdot b
  &\xrightarrow{\ a\ } \varepsilon\cdot b
  &&\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{seq}},\ r = a,\ s = b,\ n(r) = \mathrm{false} \\
  &\xrightarrow{\ b\ } \phi\cdot b + \varepsilon
  &&\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n},\ r = \varepsilon,\ s = b,\ n(r) = \mathrm{true}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The final expression is nullable, by $\mathsf{N}_{+}$ and
$\mathsf{N}_{\varepsilon}$ on its right summand, so $w$ matches. But
consider what survives. Each step discards the character it consumed:
the $a$ became $\varepsilon$ at the first step and then $\phi$ at the
second, and the $b$ became $\varepsilon$. The final expression records
that nothing remains to be matched, and records nothing else. The tree
$(a,b)$ that the match deserves is not in it and cannot be read out of
it, because the information needed to build it was thrown away one
character at a time on the way down.

It can, however, be recovered by walking the same sequence
\emph{backwards}, putting each consumed character back where it came
from. That is the whole idea, and the implementation prints it in three
lines:

\begin{verbatim}
$ cargo run -- parse "ab" "ab" --parser deriv_std_rec --diag 2
Tree:   (a, b)

Construction steps:
  mkEps(r2) -> Right ()
  inject(ab, 'b', Right ()) -> ((), b)
  inject(ab, 'a', ((), b)) -> (a, b)
\end{verbatim}

The first line builds a tree for the \emph{empty} word at the end of the
input, which is all the final nullable expression can justify. The two
\texttt{inject} lines then run in the reverse of the order the
characters were consumed, each reinstating one character, until the tree
describes the original expression against the original word.
Figure~\ref{fig:two-pass} draws the same three steps against the
expressions they operate on.

The construction therefore has the same two-phase shape as the Boolean
matcher, consume then decide, with one addition: between the two phases
a tree is built instead of a Boolean being returned. The rest of this
section states the two phases precisely.

\subsection{Forward Pass}

For input $x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n$ compute
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-forward}
r_0 \xrightarrow{\ x_1\ } r_1 \xrightarrow{\ x_2\ } \cdots
\xrightarrow{\ x_n\ } r_n,
\qquad r_0 = r,\quad r_i = r_{i-1} \backslash x_i .
\end{equation}
If $r_n$ is not nullable, no parse tree exists and parsing fails.

This is \texttt{match\_deriv} of
Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives} with one difference: no
simplification is applied between steps. \texttt{simplify} is safe for a
Boolean matcher, since it only ever replaces $r_i$ by a
language-equivalent expression, and language equivalence is all a
Boolean answer depends on. The backward pass needs more than that. It
walks back through the \emph{exact} sequence of derivative
transformations that produced $r_n$ from $r_0$, and a simplified $r_i$
may no longer be the expression $\mathit{inj}$ expects to meet at that
step.

The trace shows both halves of that claim at once. Each forward step
names the rule it applied and prints the result in full:

\begin{verbatim}
Step 1: d(r0, 'a')
  rule: d(r1.r2, 'a')  [nullable(r1)=false]
       = d(r1, 'a').r2
r1 = Seq(Alt(Eps, Seq(Eps, Lit('b'))), Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))
  nullable(r1) = true  [OK] position 1 matched
\end{verbatim}

The rule line records which branch of the $(r_1 r_2)\backslash x$ case
split was taken and why. The expression is printed unsimplified: the
\texttt{Eps} inside \texttt{Seq(Eps, Lit('b'))} is exactly the node
\texttt{simplify} would have removed, and it is kept because
$\mathit{inj}$ will need it.

\subsection{Building a Tree for the Empty Word}

Once $r_n$ is known to be nullable, a parse tree $v_n$ for the empty
remainder is built directly by $\mathit{mkEps}$, defined by structural
recursion on a nullable expression:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mkeps}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{mkEps}_{\varepsilon} &= () &&(\mathsf{E}_{\varepsilon}) \\
\mathit{mkEps}_{r^{*}} &= [\,] &&(\mathsf{E}_{*}) \\
\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1 r_2} &= (\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1}, \mathit{mkEps}_{r_2})
  &&(\mathsf{E}_{\mathrm{seq}}) \\
\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1+r_2} &=
  \begin{cases}
    \mathit{Left}\ \mathit{mkEps}_{r_1} & \varepsilon \in L(r_1) \\
    \mathit{Right}\ \mathit{mkEps}_{r_2} & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  &&\begin{array}{l}(\mathsf{E}_{+}^{L}) \\ (\mathsf{E}_{+}^{R})\end{array}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The rule names continue the scheme of
Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}, and every derivation below
cites them with the binding of $r_1$ and $r_2$ stated, since which
subexpression each stands for is what decides the case taken.

There is no case for a literal $x$ or for $\emptyset$, because neither
is ever nullable and the function is only ever called on an expression
already known to satisfy $n(r)$. The only case with a choice in it is
alternation, and POSIX settles that choice without any comparison at
all. Both sides of an empty match consume nothing, so "longest
possible" cannot separate them, and the policy falls back on position
(Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost}). At the empty word that comes
to "prefer left whenever left is possible at all", which is exactly the
$\varepsilon\in L(r_1)$ guard.

That guard is the single most consequential line in the backward pass.
On the running example it fires at the top of $r_2$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-mkeps-derivation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{mkEps}_{r_2}
&= \mathit{Left}\ \mathit{mkEps}_{X\,Y}
&&\mathsf{E}_{+}^{L},\ r_1 = X\,Y,\ r_2 = \varepsilon+\phi \\
&= \mathit{Left}\ (\mathit{mkEps}_{X}, \mathit{mkEps}_{Y})
&&\mathsf{E}_{\mathrm{seq}},\ r_1 = X,\ r_2 = Y \\
&= \mathit{Left}\ (\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{mkEps}_{\phi b+\varepsilon},\
   \mathit{Right}\ \mathit{mkEps}_{\varepsilon})
&&\mathsf{E}_{+}^{R}\ \text{twice},\ \varepsilon \notin L(\phi),\
   \varepsilon \notin L(b) \\
&= \mathit{Left}\ (\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}\ (),\ \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{E}_{+}^{R},\ \mathsf{E}_{\varepsilon}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

writing $X = \phi + (\phi b + \varepsilon)$ and $Y = b + \varepsilon$
for the two factors of $r_2$'s left disjunct. The trace prints the
decisive step and the result:

\begin{verbatim}
mkEps(r2):
  mkEps(r1 + r2): nullable(r1)=true -> Left(mkEps(r1))
v2 = Left (Right Right (), Right ())
\end{verbatim}

At that outer node \emph{both} disjuncts are nullable, so it is worth
being precise about why there is no ambiguity. $\mathsf{E}_{+}^{L}$ and
$\mathsf{E}_{+}^{R}$ are not both applicable, because the case split
tests only $\varepsilon\in L(r_1)$: the moment the left disjunct passes
that test, $\mathsf{E}_{+}^{L}$ fires and the right disjunct is never
examined. $\mathit{mkEps}$ does not compare the two. It takes the left
one because the left one is nullable at all. Everything the backward
pass produces downstream follows from that single choice, and Sulzmann
and Lu prove that making it this way is the POSIX-correct one
\citep{sulzmannlu2014}.

\subsection{Undoing One Derivative}

With $v_n$ in hand, the remaining task is to turn it back into a parse
tree of the \emph{original} $r_0$, one derivative step in reverse at a
time. $\mathit{inj}$ takes a parse tree of a derivative $r\backslash x$
and reinserts the consumed character $x$, producing a parse tree of $r$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:inj}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{inj}_{x \backslash x}() &= x &&(\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{lit}}) \\
\mathit{inj}_{(r_1+r_2) \backslash x}(\mathit{Left}\ v_1)
  &= \mathit{Left}\ (\mathit{inj}_{r_1 \backslash x}\ v_1)
  &&(\mathsf{I}_{+}^{L}) \\
\mathit{inj}_{(r_1+r_2) \backslash x}(\mathit{Right}\ v_2)
  &= \mathit{Right}\ (\mathit{inj}_{r_2 \backslash x}\ v_2)
  &&(\mathsf{I}_{+}^{R}) \\
\mathit{inj}_{r^{*} \backslash x}(v, vs)
  &= (\mathit{inj}_{r \backslash x}\ v) : vs
  &&(\mathsf{I}_{*}) \\
\mathit{inj}_{(r_1 r_2) \backslash x}(v_1, v_2)
  &= (\mathit{inj}_{r_1 \backslash x}\ v_1,\ v_2)
  &&(\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}),\ n(r_1) = \mathrm{false} \\
\mathit{inj}_{(r_1 r_2) \backslash x}(\mathit{Left}(v_1, v_2))
  &= (\mathit{inj}_{r_1 \backslash x}\ v_1,\ v_2)
  &&(\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{L}),\ n(r_1) = \mathrm{true} \\
\mathit{inj}_{(r_1 r_2) \backslash x}(\mathit{Right}\ v_2)
  &= (\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1},\ \mathit{inj}_{r_2 \backslash x}\ v_2)
  &&(\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{R}),\ n(r_1) = \mathrm{true}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The three concatenation rules are the only part that is not immediate,
and they become so when read against the derivative rule each one
inverts. Recall from Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives} that
$(r_1 r_2)\backslash x$ produced either
\[
(r_1 \backslash x)\, r_2
\qquad\text{when } n(r_1) = \mathrm{false}
\quad(\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{seq}}),
\]
or that same term joined with $r_2 \backslash x$ by a \emph{fresh} $+$
when $n(r_1) = \mathrm{true}$ ($\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$). That
fresh $+$ is what tags the incoming tree, and each
$\mathit{inj}$ rule undoes one of the three possible tags:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6.4cm}@{}}
\hline
Incoming tree & Rule & What the derivative did \\
\hline
$(v_1,v_2)$ & $\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ &
  $r_1$ was not nullable, so no $+$ was introduced and the derivative
  went straight into $r_1$. \\[2pt]
$\mathit{Left}(v_1,v_2)$ & $\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{L}$ &
  $r_1$ was nullable, and the derivative nonetheless continued matching
  inside $r_1$. \\[2pt]
$\mathit{Right}\ v_2$ & $\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{R}$ &
  $r_1$ was nullable and the derivative skipped it, jumping into $r_2$.
  Nothing of $r_1$ was consumed by $x$, so its half of the reconstructed
  pair must be synthesized from scratch by $\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1}$. \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last row is where $\mathit{mkEps}$ is called a second time, in the
middle of the backward pass and not at its start, and it is the step
Section~\ref{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory} eliminates.

Parsing is these two phases packaged as one recursive function, applying
$\mathit{mkEps}$ once at the end of the input and $\mathit{inj}$ once
per character on the way back:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:parse}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{parse}(r, \varepsilon) &= \mathit{mkEps}_r
  \qquad (\text{if } \varepsilon \in L(r)) \\
\mathit{parse}(r, xw) &= \mathit{inj}_{r \backslash x}(\mathit{parse}(r \backslash x, w))
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Running Example End to End}

Return to $r_1 = (a+ab)(b+\varepsilon)$ against $\mathtt{ab}$, whose two
candidate parse trees were established in
Section~\ref{sec:disambiguation-problem}. The forward pass is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-4}
\begin{aligned}
r_1 &\xrightarrow{\ a\ } (\varepsilon+\varepsilon b)(b+\varepsilon)
    &&\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{seq}},\ r = a+ab,\ s = b+\varepsilon,\ n(r) = \mathrm{false} \\
    &\xrightarrow{\ b\ } (\phi+(\phi b+\varepsilon))(b+\varepsilon)
       + (\varepsilon+\phi)
    &&\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n},\ r = \varepsilon+\varepsilon b,\ n(r) = \mathrm{true}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The two steps take different cases of the same rule, and the reason is
the binding. After one character the left operand has become
$\varepsilon+\varepsilon b$, which is nullable by $\mathsf{N}_{+}$ and
$\mathsf{N}_{\varepsilon}$, where the original $a+ab$ was not. That is
what adds the second summand $(\varepsilon+\phi)$, and it is that
summand which makes the whole expression nullable. The trace's second
step confirms the rule and prints the expression the hand derivation
abbreviates:

\begin{verbatim}
Step 2: d(r1, 'b')
  rule: d(r1.r2, 'b')  [nullable(r1)=true]
       = d(r1, 'b').r2  +  d(r2, 'b')
r2 = Alt(Seq(Alt(Phi, Alt(Seq(Phi, Lit('b')), Eps)), Alt(Lit('b'),
  Eps)), Alt(Eps, Phi))
  nullable(r2) = true  [OK] position 2 matched
\end{verbatim}

This is the nullable branch, the one introducing a fresh $\mathit{Alt}$,
and it is that new $\mathit{Alt}$ whose two disjuncts
$\mathit{mkEps}$ then chose between in
Equation~\eqref{eq:dp-mkeps-derivation}. From that section's
\[
v_2 = \mathit{Left}\,(\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}\ (),\ \mathit{Right}\ ())
\]
the backward pass runs two injections. The first undoes the
$\mathtt{b}$ step:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-inj-b}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{inj}_{r_1\backslash \mathtt{b}}(v_2)
&= (\mathit{inj}_{X\backslash \mathtt{b}}(\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{Right}\ ()),\
    \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{L},\ n(X) = \mathrm{true} \\
&= (\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{inj}_{(\varepsilon b)\backslash \mathtt{b}}(\mathit{Right}\ ()),\
    \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{I}_{+}^{R},\ r_2 = \varepsilon b \\
&= (\mathit{Right}\ (\mathit{mkEps}_{\varepsilon},\
    \mathit{inj}_{b\backslash \mathtt{b}}()),\ \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{R},\ n(\varepsilon) = \mathrm{true} \\
&= (\mathit{Right}\ ((\,), b),\ \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{E}_{\varepsilon},\ \mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{lit}}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $X = \varepsilon+\varepsilon b$ is the left factor of $r_1$ after
the first derivative. The third line is
$\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{R}$ doing the work the table above
describes: the derivative had skipped the nullable $\varepsilon$ and
jumped into $b$, so $\varepsilon$'s half of the pair is synthesized by
$\mathit{mkEps}$ and not recovered. The second injection undoes the
$\mathtt{a}$ step:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-inj-a}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{inj}_{r_0\backslash \mathtt{a}}(v_1)
&= (\mathit{inj}_{(a+ab)\backslash \mathtt{a}}(\mathit{Right}\ ((\,),b)),\
    \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}},\ n(a+ab) = \mathrm{false} \\
&= (\mathit{Right}\ \mathit{inj}_{(ab)\backslash \mathtt{a}}((\,),b),\
    \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{I}_{+}^{R},\ r_2 = ab \\
&= (\mathit{Right}\ (\mathit{inj}_{a\backslash \mathtt{a}}(\,),\ b),\
    \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}},\ n(a) = \mathrm{false} \\
&= (\mathit{Right}\ (a,b),\ \mathit{Right}\ ())
&&\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{lit}}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Both are what the trace reports:

\begin{verbatim}
inject(r1, 'b', v2):
  -> (Right ((), b), Right ())
v1 = (Right ((), b), Right ())

inject(r0, 'a', v1):
  -> (Right (a, b), Right ())
v0 = (Right (a, b), Right ())
\end{verbatim}

Reading the two together shows $\mathit{inj}$ doing its characteristic
work. After the first step the tree still contains $(\,)$, an
empty-word placeholder sitting where the literal $\mathtt{a}$ has not
yet been reinserted; at the second, $\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{lit}}$ replaces
it with $a$ and the result is a complete tree of $r_1$ itself. That
answer, $(\mathit{Right}(a,b), \mathit{Right}())$, selects the
\emph{longer} $ab$ branch, which is the POSIX result derived in
Section~\ref{sec:posix-longest-leftmost} and not Greedy's
$(\mathit{Left}\ a, \mathit{Left}\ b)$.

The report's header quantifies the cost of the two-pass shape in one
line:

\begin{verbatim}
Expressions stored:   3 (r0 -> r1 -> r2)
\end{verbatim}

one expression per input character plus the original, every one of them
live until the backward pass has consumed it. That is exactly the
requirement Figure~\ref{fig:two-pass} draws, and exactly the one the
next section removes.



\section{The Bit-Coded Construction}
\label{sec:bitcoded-optimization-theory}

The direct construction must retain the whole forward sequence, every
$r_i$ from $r_0$ to $r_n$, or in an implementation an equivalent call
stack, so that $\mathit{inj}$ has each $r_{i-1}$ available to
reconstruct against on the way back. The backward pass cannot begin
until the forward pass has finished.

Sulzmann and Lu's bit-coded construction (their Section~3.2,
"Incremental Bit-Coded Forward Parse Tree Construction")
\citep{sulzmannlu2014} removes that requirement by building the parse
tree \emph{during} the forward pass, so that when the last character is
consumed the winning tree's information is already sitting in the final
expression and no second pass is needed.
Figure~\ref{fig:single-pass} shows the resulting shape on $a^{*}$
against \texttt{"aaa"}, the case where it is clearest.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[
  expr/.style={draw, rounded corners=2pt, minimum height=6.5mm,
               font=\scriptsize, inner xsep=3pt},
  bits/.style={draw, rounded corners=2pt, minimum height=6.5mm,
               font=\scriptsize, inner xsep=3pt, fill=black!5},
  lbl/.style={font=\scriptsize, inner sep=1.5pt},
]
\node[expr] (r)   at (0,0)    {$a^{*}$};
\node[expr] (ri0) at (2.5,0)  {$[\,]@(a)^{*}$};
\node[expr] (ri1) at (5.3,0)  {$[0]@(a)^{*}$};
\node[expr] (ri2) at (8.1,0)  {$[0,0]@(a)^{*}$};
\node[expr] (ri3) at (11.2,0) {$[0,0,0]@(a)^{*}$};
\node[bits] (bs)  at (5.3,-1.9)  {$[0,0,0,1]$};
\node[bits] (v)   at (9.0,-1.9)  {$[a,a,a]$};

\draw[->] (r)   -- node[lbl, above] {$\mathit{internalize}$} (ri0);
\draw[->] (ri0) -- node[lbl, above] {$\backslash_b \mathtt{a}$}

\subsection{Parse Trees as Bit-Strings}

A parse tree is represented as a flat sequence of bits: $0$ marks a
$\mathit{Left}$ choice and $1$ a $\mathit{Right}$ choice, and inside a
Kleene star, $0$ marks "one more iteration" and $1$ marks "stop". A
pair of mutually recursive functions
$\mathit{encode}_r$/$\mathit{decode}_r$ converts between a
$\vdash v : r$ parse tree and its bit-string, and both are
straightforward given the tagging already present in $v$.

The star convention is worth spelling out, since it is the one place
where a bit does not correspond to an alternation. Under $r = a^{*}$,
the word $\mathtt{aaa}$ encodes as $[0,0,0,1]$: three $0$s, one per
iteration taken, then a closing $1$. The empty word under the same $r$
encodes as $[1]$ alone, a star that stops immediately. Two consequences
follow. The bit-string's length grows with the number of iterations and
not with the size of $r$; and reading it back requires knowing $r$,
which is what $\mathit{decode}_r$ is given.

\subsection{Annotated Expressions}

The construction's core idea is to annotate the derivative's own
\emph{input}, not just its output, with partial bit-code information, so
that by the time a subexpression has become $\varepsilon$ its
accumulated annotation already \emph{is} the relevant slice of the final
bit-string. An annotated expression $\mathit{ri}$ extends plain $r$ with
a bit-list $bs$ attached at every node except $\emptyset$, which denotes
no language and so has no parse tree to annotate:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:aregex-grammar}
\mathit{ri} \;::=\; \emptyset \mid (bs@\varepsilon) \mid (bs@x) \mid
(bs@\mathit{ri}_1 \oplus \mathit{ri}_2) \mid (bs@\mathit{ri}_1\, \mathit{ri}_2)
\mid (bs@\mathit{ri}^{*})
\end{equation}

Two operations connect plain $r$ to this annotated world.
$\mathit{internalize}$ converts $r$ into $\mathit{ri}$ with every
bit-list empty, except at $\mathit{Alt}$: the left operand of
$r_1 + r_2$ is tagged with a leading $0$ and the right with a leading
$1$ at construction time, so that which alternative a subtree came from
can always be recovered from its own annotation without inspecting the
surrounding structure. $\mathit{fuse}(bs, \mathit{ri})$ prepends a
bit-list onto whatever annotation $\mathit{ri}$'s top-most node already
carries, and is the primitive every other operation here is built from.

The trace's \texttt{INTERNALIZE} section shows the result for $r_1$,
with the alternation tags in place before any input is read:

\begin{verbatim}
ri0 = []@([]@([false]@'a' (+) [true]@([]@'a' . []@'b')) .
  []@([false]@'b' (+) [true]@epsilon))
\end{verbatim}

Reading the four tags off: \texttt{[false]} on $a$ and \texttt{[true]}
on $ab$ record the two alternatives of $a+ab$, and the same pair appears
again on $b$ and $\varepsilon$. Every other bit-list is empty. Those
four tags are the only bits this expression will ever produce.
Everything the derivative does from here moves them; it never invents
new ones.

\subsection{The Refined Derivative}

The derivative is lifted to annotated expressions, written
$\mathit{ri} \backslash_b l$, following the same case structure as
Equation~\eqref{eq:derivative}'s $r \backslash l$ but threading bits
through instead of discarding them:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bderiv}
\begin{aligned}
(bs@\mathit{ri}_1 \oplus \mathit{ri}_2) \backslash_b l &=
  (bs@(\mathit{ri}_1 \backslash_b l) \oplus (\mathit{ri}_2 \backslash_b l))
  &&(\mathsf{B}_{+}) \\
(bs@\mathit{ri}_1\, \mathit{ri}_2) \backslash_b l &=
  (bs@(\mathit{ri}_1 \backslash_b l)\, \mathit{ri}_2)
  &&(\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}),\ \varepsilon \notin L(\mathit{ri}_1) \\
(bs@\mathit{ri}_1\, \mathit{ri}_2) \backslash_b l &=
  (bs@(\mathit{ri}_1 \backslash_b l)\, \mathit{ri}_2) \oplus
  \mathit{fuse}(\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_1}, \mathit{ri}_2 \backslash_b l)
  &&(\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}),\ \varepsilon \in L(\mathit{ri}_1) \\
(bs@\mathit{ri}^{*}) \backslash_b l &=
  (bs@\mathit{fuse}([0], \mathit{ri} \backslash_b l)\; ([\,]@\mathit{ri}^{*}))
  &&(\mathsf{B}_{*})
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The concatenation cases mirror $(r_1 r_2)\backslash x$'s two-way split
exactly, with one addition. On the branch where the derivative abandons
$\mathit{ri}_1$ and jumps into $\mathit{ri}_2$, the bits $\mathit{ri}_1$
would have contributed to an empty match are fused onto
$\mathit{ri}_2$'s derivative instead of being discarded. That is the
incremental step: it does immediately, during the forward pass, the work
$\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{R}$ postponed to a later
$\mathit{mkEps}_{r_1}$ call inside $\mathit{inj}$.

The star case attaches a leading $0$, "one more iteration", each time
the body is derived again, which is where the $[0,0,0]$ prefix of
Figure~\ref{fig:single-pass} comes from. The outer $bs$ stays where it
is, on the new node the rule produces.

$\mathit{mkEpsBC}$ mirrors Equation~\eqref{eq:mkeps}'s $\mathit{mkEps}$
but returns accumulated bits instead of a tree:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mkepsbc}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{(bs@\varepsilon)} &= bs &&(\mathsf{EB}_{\varepsilon}) \\
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{(bs@\mathit{ri}_1 \oplus \mathit{ri}_2)} &=
  bs \mathbin{+\!\!+} \begin{cases}
    \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_1} & \varepsilon \in L(\mathit{ri}_1) \\
    \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_2} & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  &&\begin{array}{l}(\mathsf{EB}_{+}^{L}) \\ (\mathsf{EB}_{+}^{R})\end{array} \\
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{(bs@\mathit{ri}_1\, \mathit{ri}_2)} &=
  bs \mathbin{+\!\!+} \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_1} \mathbin{+\!\!+} \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_2}
  &&(\mathsf{EB}_{\mathrm{seq}}) \\
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{(bs@\mathit{ri}^{*})} &= bs \mathbin{+\!\!+} [1] &&(\mathsf{EB}_{*})
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The alternation case has the same left-preference guard, and for the
same POSIX reason, as $\mathsf{E}_{+}^{L}$ in
Equation~\eqref{eq:mkeps}. The star case emits the closing $1$ that ends
a run of iterations, which is the last bit of
Figure~\ref{fig:single-pass}'s $[0,0,0,1]$.

Once the forward pass ends on a nullable annotated expression,
$\mathit{mkEpsBC}$ extracts its complete bit-string and
$\mathit{decode}_r$ turns that into an ordinary parse tree.
$\mathit{decode}$ uses the \emph{original}, un-annotated $r$, because it
needs to know the shape it is decoding bits \emph{for}, and the
annotated $\mathit{ri}$ no longer determines that once its own
annotations have been consumed. That is the dashed dependency in
Figure~\ref{fig:single-pass}, and the only thing the construction still
has to remember from before the forward pass began.

\subsection{The Running Example, Bit by Bit}

Run $r_1$ against $\mathtt{ab}$ through this construction instead, to
see the same POSIX answer assembled incrementally. Starting from the
$\mathit{ri}_0$ quoted above, the first character takes
$\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}$, since the left operand is not nullable:

\begin{verbatim}
Step 1 (char: 'a', position 1)
  rule: (bs@ri1.ri2) \ 'a'  [nullable_bc(ri1)=false]
       = bs@(ri1\'a'.ri2)
  simp -> []@([]@([false]@epsilon (+) [true]@'b') . []@([false]@'b'
    (+) [true]@epsilon))
\end{verbatim}

Comparing this against $\mathit{ri}_0$: the literal \texttt{'a'} has
become \texttt{epsilon}, \texttt{[]@'a' . []@'b'} has become
\texttt{'b'}, and both alternation tags are exactly where they were.
Deriving one character consumed structure and moved no bits.

$\mathit{ri}_1$'s left operand is now nullable, through
\texttt{[false]@epsilon}, so the second character takes
$\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$, and this is where bits move:

\begin{verbatim}
Step 2 (char: 'b', position 2)
  rule: (bs@ri1.ri2) \ 'b'  [nullable_bc(ri1)=true]
       = bs@((ri1\'b'.ri2) (+) (fuse (mkEpsBC ri1) (ri2\'b')))
  simp -> []@([true]@([false]@'b' (+) [true]@epsilon) (+) [false,
    false]@epsilon)
\end{verbatim}

Two things happened in that one step. The rule's
\texttt{fuse (mkEpsBC ri1)} produced the \texttt{[false, false]} prefix
on the right disjunct: the first \texttt{false} is $\mathit{ri}_1$'s own
empty-match bit, the second is $(b+\varepsilon)$'s left-alternative tag,
fused in front of it. And a \texttt{[true]} appeared on the left
disjunct, tagging "the derivative continued into the left operand" as
the \emph{Right} alternative of the node $\mathsf{B}_{\mathrm{seq}}^{\,n}$
has just introduced. Where $\mathit{inj}$ discovers that tagging on the
way back, $\backslash_b$ must commit to it immediately.

Both disjuncts of $\mathit{ri}_2$ are nullable, so $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$
faces the same choice $\mathit{mkEps}$ faced in
Equation~\eqref{eq:dp-mkeps-derivation}, and resolves it the same way:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-7}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{mkEpsBC}_{\mathit{ri}_2}
&= [\,] \mathbin{+\!\!+} \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{[1]@(\,[0]@b \,\oplus\, [1]@\varepsilon\,)}
&&\mathsf{EB}_{+}^{L},\ bs = [\,],\ \varepsilon \in L(\mathit{ri}_1) \\
&= [\,] \mathbin{+\!\!+} [1] \mathbin{+\!\!+} \mathit{mkEpsBC}_{[1]@\varepsilon}
&&\mathsf{EB}_{+}^{R},\ bs = [1],\ \varepsilon \notin L([0]@b) \\
&= [\,] \mathbin{+\!\!+} [1] \mathbin{+\!\!+} [1] \;=\; [1,1]
&&\mathsf{EB}_{\varepsilon},\ bs = [1]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The outer step takes $\mathsf{EB}_{+}^{L}$ and the inner one
$\mathsf{EB}_{+}^{R}$, for the same reason in both cases: each rule
tests only whether its own $\mathit{ri}_1$ is nullable. At the outer
node that $\mathit{ri}_1$ is the $[1]@(\ldots)$ disjunct, which is
nullable; at the inner node it is $[0]@b$, a bare literal, which is not,
so the right branch is taken and contributes the second $1$. The trace
confirms the result and the decode that follows:

\begin{verbatim}
mkEpsBC(ri2) = [11]

decode((a|ab)(b|), [11]):
  = (Right (a, b), Right ())
\end{verbatim}

Both constructions therefore encode the same preference, continuing the
match already in progress over falling back to the shorter alternative,
and apply it at different moments: inside $\mathit{inj}$'s backward pass
there, and inside $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$'s forward-accumulated bits here.
The two bits select $\mathit{Right}$ at both alternation points of
$r_1$, which is the tree Equation~\eqref{eq:dp-inj-a} reached through an
explicit backward pass, read this time off the forward pass alone.

\paragraph{On simplification between steps.} The direct construction's
forward pass must \emph{not} simplify, since its backward pass has to
walk back through the exact sequence of derivatives that produced $r_n$.
The bit-coded construction is under no such restriction, and both entry
points in \texttt{src/parsers/deriv\_bc/parse.rs} call \texttt{simp}
after every step, which is why every excerpt above shows a
\texttt{simp ->} line and not a raw derivative. This is safe for the
same reason \texttt{fuse} is: the information a backward pass would need
is already carried \emph{on} the annotated expression, as bits attached
to its nodes, so simplifying $\mathit{ri}_i$ to a smaller but
bit-equivalent expression loses nothing $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$ needs later.
Simplifying $r_i$ in the direct construction would destroy the very
structure $\mathit{inj}$ reconstructs against.



\section{Simplification and Its Limits}
\label{sec:simplification-rules-theory}

Section~\ref{sec:brzozowski-derivatives}'s worked example already showed
plain derivatives growing an extra $+$ at every nullable-prefix step
with nothing to bound that growth, and the bit-coded construction
inherits the problem. \citet{sulzmannlu2014} give the concrete case

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp-8}
\begin{aligned}
a^{*} &\xrightarrow{\ a\ } \varepsilon a^{*}
      \xrightarrow{\ a\ } \phi a^{*} + \varepsilon a^{*} \\
      &\xrightarrow{\ a\ } (\phi a^{*} + \varepsilon a^{*})
        + (\phi a^{*} + \varepsilon a^{*})
      \xrightarrow{\ a\ } \cdots
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where every step after the first is language-equivalent to
$\varepsilon a^{*}$ and syntactically double the size. Left unchecked,
this defeats the point of a single-pass construction: an unboundedly
growing $\mathit{ri}$ makes each subsequent $\backslash_b$ step
correspondingly more expensive.

A simplification function $\mathit{simp}$, applied after every
derivative step, keeps the annotated expression small. It propagates
$\phi$ through $\mathit{Seq}$ and $\mathit{Alt}$, flattens and
deduplicates nested alternations while keeping the first occurrence of a
syntactic duplicate, and eliminates $\varepsilon$ from a concatenation
by
\[
(bs@(bs'@\varepsilon)\, \mathit{ri})
\;\longrightarrow\;
\mathit{fuse}(bs \mathbin{+\!\!+} bs',\ \mathit{ri}),
\]
folding the eliminated node's own bits forward instead of discarding
them. All are instances of the identities of
Equation~\eqref{eq:simp-identities}, applied to the annotated grammar
and combined with an explicit \emph{isPhi} test that recognizes
empty-language expressions built up from these rules and not only the
literal $\emptyset$ constructor.

\paragraph{On the paper's own example.} Applied to $a^{*}$ against
$\mathtt{aaa}$, $\mathit{simp}$ collapses every step back to a
bit-annotated $a^{*}$. The trace shows one result per character, each a
single star node whose bit-prefix has grown by one $0$:

\begin{verbatim}
Step 1 ... simp -> [false]@([]@'a')*
Step 2 ... simp -> [false, false]@([]@'a')*
Step 3 ... simp -> [false, false, false]@([]@'a')*

mkEpsBC(ri3) = [0001]
decode(a*, [0001]):
  = [a, a, a]
\end{verbatim}

This is Figure~\ref{fig:single-pass} as the implementation prints it.
The expression stays two nodes wide while the bit-prefix grows linearly,
and the three $0$s plus $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$'s closing $1$ are exactly the
$[0,0,0,1]$ encoding of "three iterations, then stop". It is the
clearest single illustration of what the bit-coded construction is for:
all of the parse-tree information is in the prefix and none of it is in
the expression.

\paragraph{Where the bound fails.} That result does not generalize, and
it is worth being precise about the failure instead of letting the
$a^{*}$ case stand for the general one. Figure~\ref{fig:simp-growth}
measures node counts per derivative step for two patterns, each with and
without $\mathit{simp}$.

The two simplified series behave completely differently. For $a^{*}$ the
line is flat. For $(a+(b+ab))^{*}$ it is straight on a logarithmic axis,
which is to say exponential. Every two characters take the size $s$ to
$2s+1$, so $s+1$ doubles: from $35$ at step 4 the series runs $71$,
$143$, $287$, $575$, $1151$, $2303$ at step 16, and $36\,863$ at step
24. The growth is $\Theta(2^{n/2})$ in the input length, not the bounded
size the $a^{*}$ case suggests. The figures are unchanged if the
three-way alternation is grouped the other way, as $((a+b)+ab)^{*}$,
which is what the CLI spelling \texttt{"(a|b|ab)*"} parses to
(Section~\ref{sec:regex-syntax}); the growth depends on the pattern's
shape and not on that grouping. Applying $\mathit{simp}$ repeatedly to a
genuine fixpoint changes nothing either: a single pass already
\emph{is} the fixpoint here, producing byte-identical expressions at
every step, so this is not the implementation stopping too early.

The mechanism is specific and easy to state. $\mathit{simp}$'s
deduplication discards an alternation branch only when it is
\emph{syntactically identical} to an earlier one, and an annotated
branch carries its bit-list as part of its syntax. Counting the
top-level alternation branches of $\mathit{simp}(\mathit{ri}_i)$ at each
step, and counting them again after erasing every annotation, separates
the two:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\hline
Step & 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 & 10 & 12 \\
\hline
Branches & 2 & 4 & 8 & 16 & 32 & 64 \\
Distinct branches & 2 & 4 & 8 & 16 & 32 & 64 \\
Distinct ignoring bits & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At every step all of the branches are structurally the same expression,
differing only in the bits recording which route through the alternation
reached it, so the last row stays at $1$ while the first two double. The
$r + r = r$ rule never fires, because on annotated expressions the two
sides are not equal.

The bits are not redundant. Each branch's annotation is a different
candidate parse tree, and $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$'s left-preference is what
eventually selects among them, so dropping duplicates on shape alone
would change which tree the parser returns and not merely shrink the
expression. That is why the rule is stated on full syntactic equality in
the first place, and why the growth here is a property of the
construction and not a defect in this transcription of it.

Sulzmann and Lu claim, but do not fully prove, that the construction
runs in time linear in the input, on the grounds that each of
$\backslash_b$, $\mathit{simp}$, $\mathit{fuse}$, $\mathit{mkEpsBC}$ and
\emph{isPhi} only ever recurses over subcalls bounded by the
now-simplified expression's size, and they note explicitly that detailed
space-complexity estimates are left as future work
\citep{sulzmannlu2014}. Figure~\ref{fig:simp-growth} is one concrete
reason that gap matters: the argument's premise, that $\mathit{simp}$
keeps the expression small, holds for $a^{*}$ and fails for
$(a+(b+ab))^{*}$, and the linear-time claim rests on it.
Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} measures the resulting wall-clock behaviour
on both kinds of pattern, and
Section~\ref{sec:pathological-inputs} returns to this family
specifically.



\section{Rust Implementation: The Direct Parser}
\label{sec:derivative-parser-inj-rust}

Both parsers in this chapter are transcriptions of the equations above.
Each definition becomes one function over the \texttt{Regex} or
\texttt{ARegex} enum, the case split of the equation becomes the arms of
a \texttt{match}, and the recursion becomes recursion. The mapping is
mechanical enough that reading the code against the equation adds
nothing the equation does not already say, and
Appendix~\ref{app:code-listings} lists the code itself. What is worth
setting out here is the short list of places where Rust forces a
decision the specification does not make.

\paragraph{Partial functions become panics.}
\label{sec:mk-eps-impl}
\label{sec:inject-impl}
$\mathit{mkEps}_r$ is defined only for nullable $r$:
Equation~\eqref{eq:mkeps} has no clause for $\phi$ or for a literal,
because neither is ever reached. Rust has no partial functions, so the
transcription has to say something, and it says this:

\begin{verbatim}
Regex::Lit(c) => panic!("mk_eps called on Lit('{}')", c),
Regex::Phi   => panic!("mk_eps called on Phi"),
\end{verbatim}

The panic is an assertion about the caller, not error handling. Every
call site establishes nullability first, so reaching either arm would
mean the invariant the backward pass rests on has already been broken.
\texttt{inject} makes the same move at a finer grain: a bare
\texttt{Pair} arriving where a nullable $r_1$ requires a tagged
\texttt{Left} or \texttt{Right} can only mean the tree handed in did not
come from that expression's own derivative sequence. The alternative in
both cases is to invent a plausible value and return it, and a wrong
parse tree that looks well formed is precisely what the agreement tests
of Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} could not detect: they would be
comparing two parsers that had each guessed.

\paragraph{Recursion or an explicit vector.}
\label{sec:recursive-vs-loop}
The two entry points of \texttt{deriv\_std} compute the same tree by
different means. The recursive one descends to the end of the input and
calls \texttt{inject} as the stack unwinds, so the call stack \emph{is}
Figure~\ref{fig:two-pass}'s top row and nothing else stores it. The loop
one builds that row as an explicit \texttt{Vec<Regex>} going forward,
then walks it backward. The algorithm is identical; the two differ in
where the top row is kept while the bottom row consumes it. That is a
resource decision, and in Rust a consequential one, because a thread's
stack is a fixed allocation while the heap is not: the recursive entry
point overflows on input the loop entry point handles without
difficulty. Section~\ref{sec:stack-depth} measures where the difference
begins.

\paragraph{Trees and their flattening.}
\label{sec:parsetree-and-flatten}
\texttt{ParseTree} (Section~\ref{sec:parse-trees-as-evidence}) is the
one representation every parser in this thesis produces, which is what
lets the agreement tests compare outputs directly. \texttt{flatten} is
kept separate from tree construction so that it can serve as an
independent check: it discards every tag and pair boundary and returns
the concatenated literals, so a successful parse of $w$ must flatten
back to $w$ whatever the disambiguation policy was. Every diagnostic
report prints the two next to each other for that reason.



\section{Rust Implementation: The Bit-Coded Parser}
\label{sec:derivative-parser-bitcoded-rust}

\paragraph{One extra field, and the traits that come with it.}
\label{sec:aregex-type}
\texttt{ARegex} is \texttt{Regex} with a \texttt{Vec<bool>} added to
every variant but $\phi$, which has no annotation slot. This is the one
point in the implementation where a plain \texttt{Regex} will not do,
since the whole purpose of the construction is to thread partial
parse-tree information through the expression as it is derived, and that
needs somewhere on every node to put it.

The two types then diverge in the traits they derive, and Rust makes
that divergence explicit, not incidental. \texttt{Regex} derives
\texttt{Hash} because
Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}'s deduplication uses it as a
\texttt{HashSet} key; \texttt{ARegex} does not, because
\texttt{deriv\_bc} never hashes an annotated expression. Its own
deduplication, inside \texttt{simp}, compares alternation branches
pairwise instead. A language deriving a fixed bundle of instances at
once would leave that difference implicit; here it is a visible fact
about which operations each type is expected to support.

\paragraph{Ownership makes the common case free.}
\label{sec:internalize-fuse}
\label{sec:deriv-bc}
\texttt{fuse(bs, ri)} prepends a bit-prefix to an expression's top-level
annotation, and it opens by handing the expression straight back when
there is nothing to prepend:

\begin{verbatim}
pub fn fuse(bs: &[bool], ri: ARegex) -> ARegex {
    if bs.is_empty() { return ri; }
    // ... otherwise rebuild the node with bs in front of its prefix
}
\end{verbatim}

Because \texttt{fuse} takes \texttt{ri} by value, that early return is a
move and not a copy: the expression is handed on untouched.
\texttt{fuse} runs on every \texttt{Seq} and \texttt{Star} step of every
derivative, and the overwhelming majority of those calls carry an empty
prefix, so the check converts the common case from a node rebuild into
nothing at all.

\paragraph{Decoding, and where a bad bit-string is caught.}
\label{sec:mk-eps-bc}
\label{sec:decode}
\texttt{decode} turns the original plain \texttt{Regex} plus a flat
\texttt{Vec<bool>} back into a \texttt{ParseTree}, consuming one bit per
alternation and one per star iteration. It takes the un-annotated
expression and not the derived one, which is the dashed dependency in
Figure~\ref{fig:single-pass} and the reason it is shared with
Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser}'s \texttt{pderiv\_bc}: that
parser arrives at a \texttt{(Regex, bits)} pair by an entirely different
route and needs the identical final step. \texttt{decode} asserts that
no bits remain once the expression has been walked, and panics on an
unexpected end of bits, which is the same invariant-assertion discipline
as \texttt{mk\_eps} and \texttt{inject} applied at the other end of the
construction.



\section{The Two Constructions Compared}
\label{sec:deriv-worked-example}

The same expression has now been run against the same input through both
constructions, with each trace quoted at the point where it illustrated
a rule. Setting the two reports against each other as wholes makes the
structural difference visible in one view. The full reports are
Appendix~\ref{app:report-deriv-std-match} and
Appendix~\ref{app:report-deriv-bc-match}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{3.9cm}p{4.3cm}@{}}
\hline
& \texttt{deriv\_std} & \texttt{deriv\_bc} \\
\hline
Report sections & forward, nullability, backward & internalize, forward, nullability, decode \\[2pt]
Named intermediates & \texttt{r0 -> r1 -> r2} & \texttt{ri0 -> ri1 -> ri2} \\[2pt]
Retained at the end & all three & the last one only \\[2pt]
Tree-building calls & 1 \texttt{mkEps} \newline + 2 \texttt{inject} & 1 \texttt{mkEpsBC} \newline + 1 \texttt{decode} \\[2pt]
Simplification & none, by necessity & \texttt{simp} after every step \\[2pt]
Final answer & \multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{\texttt{(Right (a, b), Right ())}} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The number of tree-building calls is the row that does not scale with
the input. \texttt{deriv\_std} needs one \texttt{inject} per character
and so grows with the input length; \texttt{deriv\_bc} needs exactly one
\texttt{mkEpsBC} and one \texttt{decode} however long the input is. That
is the whole content of the optimization, and it is visible in the
reports as the difference between a \texttt{BACKWARD PASS} section whose
length depends on the input and a fixed-size
\texttt{MKEEPSBC + DECODE} section.

The final bits \texttt{[11]} and the final tree
\texttt{(Right (a, b), Right ())} encode the same answer in the two
representations, and the correspondence is exact: each \texttt{Right} in
the tree is a \texttt{1} in the bit-string, read in the order
\texttt{decode} walks the expression.

\paragraph{What happens on a failure.} Running the same $r_1$ against
$\mathtt{b}$, a string not in $L(r_1)$ since every derivation of $r_1$
must begin by matching $\mathtt{a}$, both parsers stop after one
derivative step and neither reaches a tree-building phase at all. What
each holds at that point differs, and the difference is instructive:

\begin{verbatim}
deriv_std:  r1 = Seq(Alt(Phi, Seq(Phi, Lit('b'))), Alt(Lit('b'), Eps))
              nullable(r1) = false  [FAIL] position 1

deriv_bc:   simp -> phi
            ri1 = phi
              nullable_bc(ri1) = false  [FAIL]
\end{verbatim}

\texttt{deriv\_std}'s expression denotes the empty language but is not
syntactically \texttt{Phi}; it is a \texttt{Seq} whose left operand has
both alternatives dead. \texttt{deriv\_bc}'s \texttt{simp} collapses
exactly that expression all the way down to \texttt{phi}. This is the
clearest single illustration of what
Section~\ref{sec:simplification-rules-theory}'s \emph{isPhi} test adds
over plain \texttt{simplify}: it recognizes an empty-language expression
that is not the literal $\emptyset$ constructor.

Both constructions then report the identical failure, character for
character:

\begin{verbatim}
Failure at position:  1 (1-indexed)
Found:                'b'
Expected:             'a'
  b
  ^
\end{verbatim}

The diagnostics layer reads its expected set directly off the
first-literal set of the expression at the point of failure, so it does
not depend on which construction produced that expression or on how much
simplification was applied to it. This is the general pattern for
failures across all four parsers: the constructions differ in what they
build on a successful match, and agree on what they report when there is
nothing to build. The two full failure reports are
Appendix~\ref{app:report-deriv-std-nomatch} and
Appendix~\ref{app:report-deriv-bc-nomatch}.

\paragraph{Agreement between the three POSIX entry points.} The two
parsers of this chapter, together with \texttt{deriv\_std}'s loop
variant, are three separate routes to the same tree, so their outputs
are directly comparable. Running all three over 300 randomly generated
expressions against 39 inputs each, 11\,700 cases in total, they agree
on every one, both on whether the input matched and on the exact
\texttt{ParseTree} returned when it did.
Section~\ref{sec:cross-implementation-equivalence} describes the
generator and the full test setup; what matters here is that the
bit-coded route and the inject route, which share no code beyond
\texttt{Regex} itself and \texttt{nullable}, are not observed to diverge
on any of them.

\paragraph{Where this leaves the next chapter.} Both constructions
compute the POSIX tree, and both do it by the same underlying move:
consume the input with $r\backslash x$, then resolve the one remaining
choice, at an alternation whose two sides both match the empty word, by
preferring the left. The direct construction makes that choice in
$\mathit{mkEps}$ on the way back, the bit-coded one in
$\mathit{mkEpsBC}$ at the end of the way out, and everything else about
them follows from where the choice sits.
Chapter~\ref{sec:partial-derivative-parser} keeps the shape and replaces
$r\backslash x$ with Antimirov's $pd(r,x)$, which returns a \emph{set}.
With a set there is no single expression to be nullable and no single
alternation to prefer the left of, and that turns out to change not only
the machinery but the answer.
